1The one idea everything here hangs on

Before any names: what is a living body actually doing, and why does it need food and air at all?

A living body is constantly running down. Cells wear out, muscles pull, nerves fire, broken parts have to be rebuilt — and every one of those jobs costs energy. A body also has to build itself: a growing 15-year-old is literally made of what she has eaten. So every living thing needs two things from outside:

  • Fuel and building material — that is food. Taking it in and breaking it down into pieces small enough to be used is nutrition.
  • A way of releasing the energy locked inside that food — that is respiration.

Two words you will otherwise mix up all year

Breathing is the mechanical business of pulling air into your lungs and pushing it out again. It happens in your chest, and it involves no chemistry.

Respiration is the chemical business of breaking down glucose inside a cell to release energy. It happens in every living cell of your body, day and night, whether you are awake or asleep — and it happens in plants too, which have no lungs at all.

Breathing is what delivers the oxygen that respiration uses. Allen has already punished the confusion once: 2018-S Q38 asked which substances must be eliminated from the body, and the answer included carbon dioxide — a waste product of respiration — alongside urea.

The energy that respiration releases is not used directly. It is first packed into a small molecule called ATP (adenosine triphosphate), which every part of the cell can spend. Think of glucose as a ₹2000 note and ATP as ₹10 coins: you cannot pay a bus fare with the note, so the cell breaks it into coins first. ATP is the energy currency of the cell — that phrase is worth remembering exactly.

The trap in the very first sentence of this topic

Photosynthesis and respiration look like opposites — one takes in CO₂ and gives out O₂, the other does the reverse — and students conclude that plants "breathe in CO₂". They do not. A plant respires exactly like you do, taking in O₂ and giving out CO₂, in every living cell, all the time. In daylight photosynthesis simply runs so much faster that it swallows all the CO₂ respiration makes and more besides, so the net flow reverses. Section 10 draws this.

2Two ways to get food — and the words Allen uses for them

Every living thing is on one side of a single line: either it makes its own food, or it takes food that something else made.

One word first — enzyme

Food arrives as huge molecules: a grain of starch is thousands of sugar units chained together, and a chain like that cannot cross into your blood. Breaking those chains by heat alone would cook you. So a living body uses enzymes — special proteins, each one shaped to cut a particular kind of chain, and to cut it fast at body temperature. A catalyst is the general word for something that speeds a reaction up without being used up itself; an enzyme is the living world's version.

Three consequences you will use all through this page: each enzyme handles one food type (starch, or protein, or fat — never all three); each works only in its own medium, acidic or alkaline, and stops in the wrong one; and an enzyme is not used up, so a little goes a long way. Every enzyme's name here ends in -ase — amylase, lipase, proteinase — with two old exceptions the exam still uses, pepsin and trypsin.

Autotrophic
auto = self, troph = feeding. Makes its own food from simple inorganic things — CO₂ and water — using an outside energy source. Green plants, algae, and some bacteria.
Heterotrophic
hetero = other. Depends on food made by somebody else. All animals, all fungi, most bacteria.

Heterotrophs then split by how they get at that food — and this three-way split is pure vocabulary, which is exactly the kind of thing a one-line MCQ asks:

3Photosynthesis — the whole thing in one picture

Look at the diagram before you read a word. Four things go in or come out of a leaf, and each arrives or leaves by a different route.

Photosynthesis is the process by which green plants use light energy to build their own food — glucose — out of carbon dioxide and water, giving out oxygen as a by-product. It is the door through which nearly all the energy in the living world enters it: the energy in your chapati arrived as sunlight on a wheat leaf.

The equation — learn it as written

6CO₂  +  6H₂O  — sunlight, chlorophyll →  C₆H₁₂O₆  +  6O₂

Carbon dioxide and water in; glucose and oxygen out; sunlight supplies the energy and chlorophyll is the catalyst-like pigment that absorbs it. Note that chlorophyll sits above the arrow, not in the reactants — it is not used up.

The three events, in order

NCERT breaks photosynthesis into three events, and an exam question can ask for any one of them by name:

  1. Absorption of light energy by chlorophyll. Chlorophyll sits inside tiny green bodies in the leaf cells called chloroplasts.
  2. Conversion of light energy into chemical energy, and the splitting of water molecules into hydrogen and oxygen. This is the step that releases the oxygen you breathe — the O₂ comes from the water, not from the carbon dioxide.
  3. Reduction of carbon dioxide to carbohydrate — the hydrogen from step 2 is joined to CO₂ to build glucose.

These three need not happen one immediately after the other. In many desert plants the carbon dioxide is taken in at night and stored as an acid; the light-dependent steps then use it during the day, so the plant can keep its stomata shut through the scorching afternoon and lose far less water.

That single fact is what makes the iodine test possible, and the iodine test is what every classic photosynthesis experiment (section 5) actually measures: iodine solution turns blue-black where starch is present, and stays brown where it is not. "Did this bit of leaf photosynthesise?" becomes "did this bit of leaf make starch?"

4Stomata — the door, and the bill that comes with it

A leaf has a problem. To let carbon dioxide in it must have holes; but holes also let water out. Its solution is a door it can shut.

A stoma (plural stomata) is a tiny pore in the leaf's surface, mostly on the underside — out of the direct sun, where less water evaporates. Through it a leaf does two things: it exchanges gases (CO₂ in, O₂ out during the day), and it loses water vapour, which is called transpiration.

How the door works — the mechanism in four lines

  1. The pore is simply the gap between two guard cells, which are shaped like a pair of kidney beans facing each other.
  2. Each guard cell has a thick inner wall (the side next to the pore) and a thin, elastic outer wall. The two walls are not alike — this is the whole trick.
  3. When water flows into the guard cells they swell up (turgid). The thin outer wall stretches easily, the thick inner wall does not, so each cell bows outwards like a bent banana — and the pore between them opens.
  4. When water flows out, the cells go limp (flaccid), straighten, and the pore closes.

So the plant keeps its doors shut whenever it is not actively taking in carbon dioxide — at night, for instance, when there is no photosynthesis to feed and open pores would only waste water.

The exact statements Allen has set on this

2018-S Q33 gave three statements and asked which are correct. The key is only I and III:

  • I — "Transpiration and gaseous exchange occur mainly through stomata." TRUE.
  • II — "The cell wall of guard cells is homogeneous in nature." FALSE — and it is the trap of this topic. "Homogeneous" means the same all over; the guard-cell wall is deliberately uneven, thick on the inside and thin on the outside. Without that unevenness the stoma could not open at all.
  • III — "Guard cells are bean-shaped in dicots." TRUE (in grasses and other monocots they are dumb-bell shaped instead).

One more fact worth carrying: guard cells are the only cells of the epidermis that contain chloroplasts. (The epidermis is the single outermost layer of cells — the leaf's skin.) Every other epidermal cell is transparent, to let light through to the green cells below. And dicot just means a plant whose seed splits into two halves, like a gram or a bean; a monocot, like wheat or grass, does not.

5The four experiments — each one has been a question

Every experiment below answers exactly one question, and each does it the same way: remove one thing, keep everything else identical, then test for starch. What differs between the two halves is the cause of what differs in the result.

The starch test — the step every experiment ends with

  1. De-starch first. Keep the plant in complete darkness for 2–3 days, so that it uses up the starch already in its leaves. Without this step every leaf tests positive and the experiment proves nothing.
  2. Set up the condition you are testing, then leave the plant in sunlight for a day.
  3. Pluck the leaf and boil it in water to kill it and burst the cells.
  4. Boil it in alcohol (over a water bath — alcohol catches fire easily) to dissolve out the green chlorophyll, so that a colour change can be seen. The leaf goes pale.
  5. Wash it in warm water to soften it, then add iodine solution.
  6. Blue-black = starch was made there. Brown = it was not.

Two things a question can turn on

The glowing splinter is the oxygen test, not the carbon dioxide test. A glowing splinter that relights means oxygen. A burning splinter that is put out, together with limewater turning milky, means carbon dioxide. 2018-S Q44 planted "to prove that carbon dioxide is produced during respiration in green plants" as option 1 — the right apparatus, the wrong gas.

Every one of these needs a control, and the exam loves asking which comparison is the fair one. In experiment A the control is built into the same leaf (its green part), which is why a variegated leaf is used rather than two separate plants.

6Feeding when you are only one cell

An Amoeba has no mouth, no gut and no anus. It manages the entire business of eating with the surface it already has.

Both are holozoic — they take food in whole — and both are single cells. The difference is mechanical: Amoeba changes shape to reach the food; Paramoecium cannot, so it moves the food instead.

The five steps of holozoic nutrition — true of an Amoeba and of you

  1. Ingestion — taking the food in. Amoeba pushes out temporary arms of cytoplasm called pseudopodia ("false feet"), which surround the particle and fuse, trapping it in a food vacuole.
  2. Digestion — breaking it into small, soluble molecules. Enzymes are released into the food vacuole.
  3. Absorption — the soluble products pass out of the vacuole into the cytoplasm.
  4. Assimilation — they are actually used: built into the body or burnt for energy.
  5. Egestion — the undigested remains are pushed back out through the cell surface.

Learn the order, and keep absorption (taken into the body) separate from assimilation (put to work) and from egestion (thrown out). Egestion is not excretion: egested food never entered the body's cells at all.

7The human digestive system — the diagram to know cold

Four of the five cached papers drew this and asked you to read a label off it. Learn the picture first and the words will attach themselves to it.

The journey, one stop at a time

  1. Mouth. Teeth break the food up physically. Saliva from the salivary glands wets it and brings the enzyme salivary amylase (also called ptyalin), which starts breaking starch into a sugar, maltose. Chew a plain chapati for a minute and it turns sweet — that is amylase working.
  2. Oesophagus (food pipe). No digestion here at all: it is a delivery tube. The food is pushed down by peristalsis — rhythmic waves of muscular contraction in the wall of the canal. Peristalsis happens all along the canal, which is why you can swallow while standing on your head.
  3. Stomach. The gastric glands in its wall release three things at once:
    • Hydrochloric acid — makes the contents strongly acidic, which is the medium the stomach's enzyme needs, and kills most germs swallowed with the food;
    • Pepsin — the enzyme that begins protein digestion, and works only in acid;
    • Mucus — a slime that protects the stomach's own lining from being digested by its own acid.
    A ring of muscle called a sphincter then lets the food through into the small intestine in small doses.
  4. Small intestine. The longest part of the canal, and where digestion is actually completed for all three food types. It receives two outside juices:
    • Bile, made by the liver and stored in the gall bladder. Bile does two jobs and contains no enzyme at all: it makes the food alkaline (the pancreatic enzymes need that, and the arriving food is acidic), and it emulsifies fat — breaks big fat droplets into many tiny ones, so enzymes have far more surface to work on.
    • Pancreatic juice, from the pancreas, which carries an enzyme for each food type: trypsin for proteins, lipase for emulsified fats, pancreatic amylase for starch.
    The wall of the small intestine adds its own intestinal juice, which finishes the job.
  5. Absorption through the villi — see below.
  6. Large intestine. Its wall absorbs water from the leftover, undigested material, so that what is finally egested is fairly solid. (Most of the water you drink is actually absorbed further up, with the food, in the small intestine — NCERT says only that the large intestine absorbs more water.)
  7. Anus. The undigested remains are egested, released by the anal sphincter.

The four things Allen has actually tested on this table

  • "Which pancreatic enzymes digest fats and carbohydrates?" — 2024-A Q34 and Sample Q37, the same question word for word, key (3): lipase and amylase. Pepsin is the distractor and it is not in pancreatic juice at all — it is the stomach's. Erepsin is the intestinal juice's.
  • Bile is not an enzyme and contains none. Emulsification is a physical break-up, not a chemical digestion. A question that calls bile "the enzyme that digests fat" is wrong, and a question that says fats are digested "by bile" is wrong too — bile prepares them, lipase digests them.
  • Acid in the stomach, alkali in the small intestine. 2024-U Q34 is built entirely on this: it asked which meal would digest better and keyed high protein with acidic fruits — because protein digestion (pepsin) needs an acidic medium, so extra acid helps. A meal of high starch with acidic fruits is the trap: acid stops salivary amylase.
  • Read the figure before you judge the statement. 2024-U Q39's statement (iv) says "the part labelled as B continues the digestion of food in highly acidic medium" — and it is one of the two incorrect statements. The reason is not that digestion finishes further down: it is that B is the oesophagus, as that question's own correct statement (iii) establishes ("leads to part B where no digestive glands are present & peristalsis occur"). The oesophagus has no glands and no acid — nothing is digested there at all. Fix what each letter is from the statements you can trust, then judge the rest.
  • Where digestion is completed. The small intestine, for all three food types — not the stomach. That is the point carried by the same question's statement (ii), which the key marks correct.

Absorption — why the gut is fluffy inside

Digestion is useless until the products get into the blood. Villi (singular villus) are finger-like projections of the intestinal wall that do two things: they enormously increase the surface area for absorption, and they are richly supplied with blood vessels so that the absorbed food is carried away at once. Glucose and amino acids go into the blood capillaries — the finest blood vessels, one cell thick, which is what lets anything cross into the blood at all. The products of fat digestion are taken up instead by the lacteal, a vessel carrying lymph: a pale fluid that drains from the tissues and eventually empties into the blood. The blood then delivers everything to every cell of the body, where it is used to release energy, to build new tissues and to repair old ones — that last step is assimilation.

Deer against lion — Sample Q38

The paper showed a labelled alimentary canal and asked which part is longer in a deer than in a lion. Key (1): B, the small intestine. Grass is mostly cellulose — the tough material plant cell walls are built from, slow and hard to digest — so a herbivore needs a much longer small intestine to finish the job; a carnivore eating meat needs far less. The same logic answers any "why is the gut of a cow so long" question.

8Respiration — one start, three endings

Every cell begins by breaking glucose exactly the same way. What happens next depends on whether oxygen is there — and the three endings are told apart by what they produce.

  1. Step one is the same everywhere. Glucose, a 6-carbon molecule, is broken in the cytoplasm — the jelly filling a cell, outside its nucleus and outside any of its little compartments — into two molecules of pyruvate (3 carbons each). This is glycolysis, it needs no oxygen, and it releases a little energy — a net 2 ATP.
  2. No oxygen, in yeast → ethanol + carbon dioxide. This is fermentation, and it is how bread rises and how alcohol is made. It does release CO₂.
  3. Not enough oxygen, in our muscle cells → lactic acid. When you sprint, your muscles use oxygen faster than your blood can deliver it, so pyruvate is converted to lactic acid in the cytoplasm. The build-up of lactic acid is what causes cramps. This route releases no CO₂.
  4. With oxygen, in the mitochondria → carbon dioxide + water. This is aerobic respiration, and it releases far more energy from the same glucose than either route above — which is why we bother breathing at all.

The overall aerobic equation

C₆H₁₂O₆  +  6O₂  →  6CO₂  +  6H₂O  +  energy

Compare it with the photosynthesis equation in section 3 and you will see it read backwards. That is exactly why the two processes are so easy to confuse, and exactly why the exam keeps asking you to tell them apart.

The distinctions that carry the marks

  • Which route makes CO₂? Yeast's does; the muscle's does not; the aerobic one does. 2024-U Q43 is precisely this question — "carbon dioxide is released in which process?" — and the key is (3) ethanol fermentation. The distractors were lactic acid fermentation (no CO₂), glycolysis (no CO₂) and the light reaction of photosynthesis (which releases O₂, not CO₂).
  • Where does it happen? Glycolysis and the lactic-acid step: cytoplasm. The oxygen route: mitochondria — which is why mitochondria are called the powerhouse of the cell. 2018-S Q36 asked where the Krebs cycle takes place and keyed (2) mitochondria. The Krebs cycle is a stage of aerobic respiration that NCERT does not name at Class 10 — Allen asked it anyway. All you need is its address.
  • Anaerobic ≠ no energy. A muscle short of oxygen still makes ATP, just much less of it. An option saying "no energy is produced without oxygen" is always wrong.

9Breathing — the machine that delivers the oxygen

Respiration needs oxygen at every cell and has to get carbon dioxide away from every cell. In an animal your size, diffusion alone is far too slow — so there is a pump, a pipe network and a transport fluid.

The false claims, which are the ones worth learning, were: "the wall has complete cartilaginous rings" (they are C-shaped, incomplete); "the trachea divides into bronchioles which lead into lungs" (it divides into bronchi — bronchioles come several branchings later); and "the pharynx opens into this part by a slit-like aperture called the gullet" (the larynx opens by the glottis; the gullet is another name for the oesophagus, which is the food pipe, not an airway).

Down and flat, not down and domed; the dome is the relaxed, breathed-out shape.

Note also that the lungs never empty. They always keep a residual volume of air, so that there is time for oxygen to be absorbed and carbon dioxide released even between breaths. At rest an adult breathes roughly 15–18 times a minute (a standard figure; none of the cached papers has asked for a number).

The exchange itself

  1. Blood arrives at the alveolus through the pulmonary artery. It is deoxygenated: low in oxygen, loaded with carbon dioxide. This is the one artery in the body that carries deoxygenated blood, and it is a favourite trap.
  2. The air inside the alveolus is the opposite way round — plenty of oxygen, little carbon dioxide.
  3. So each gas simply moves from where it is plentiful to where it is scarce. Oxygen diffuses into the blood; carbon dioxide diffuses out into the air. The process is diffusion — no energy is spent, nothing is pumped. 2024-A Q43 and Sample Q39 are this sentence, and both key (3).
  4. In the blood, oxygen is picked up by haemoglobin, the red pigment inside red blood cells. It has a high affinity for oxygen — high enough to load up at the lungs, loose enough to let go again at the tissues — and the loaded form is called oxyhaemoglobin. Carbon dioxide is much more soluble in water than oxygen is, so most of it needs no carrier at all: it travels dissolved in the blood plasma.
  5. Oxygen-rich blood returns to the heart through the pulmonary vein — the one vein that carries oxygenated blood.

Carbamino­haemoglobinCO₂the small share of carbon dioxide that is carried on haemoglobin
Carboxy­haemoglobinCO (carbon monoxide)the poison — CO binds far more tightly than O₂ and will not let go, so the blood can no longer carry oxygen

Read the prefixes, not the shapes: carbamino­haemoglobin is carbon di­oxide, carboxy­haemoglobin is carbon mon­oxide. 2024-A Q36 swapped exactly those two and marked the swap false. One more number from the same question: most carbon dioxide travels in the plasma as hydrogencarbonate (bicarbonate), a smaller share on haemoglobin and a little simply dissolved — which is what "mostly dissolved in the plasma" means once you look closely. All of this is beyond the NCERT Class 10 chapter; it is here only because Allen asked it.

Why alveoli are shaped like that

Balloons pack an enormous surface into a small chest. If the alveolar surface of your two lungs were spread out flat it would cover of the order of 80 m² — roughly the floor of a small flat. (Textbooks quote anywhere from 70 to 100 m²; no cached paper has ever asked for a number.) Gas exchange by diffusion is slow per square metre, so the body's answer is simply to have a colossal number of square metres, each one cell thick and each wrapped in capillaries.

In water and on land

A fish breathes with gills: it takes water in at the mouth and forces it past the gills, where dissolved oxygen is taken up by the blood. Fish breathe much faster than land animals, and the reason is a number: the amount of oxygen dissolved in water is far smaller than the amount in air. Less oxygen per gulp means more gulps. This is a classic one-line MCQ.

10How a plant breathes — and the day/night trap

Plants respire too. They just do it without lungs, without blood, and without any transport system for gases at all.

  • Every living plant cell respires, taking in O₂ and giving out CO₂, all day and all night.
  • Gases get in and out by diffusion, through stomata in leaves and lenticels in woody stems. Roots take their oxygen from the air trapped between soil particles — which is why a plant standing in waterlogged soil can drown.
  • A plant needs no transport system for gases, for two reasons. The distances are short: each part looks after its own needs — roots take their oxygen from the soil, leaves take theirs from the air — so no gas has far to travel. And the demand is low: much of a plant is dead or non-respiring tissue that needs nothing at all, and a plant is not running anywhere, so its rate of respiration is far below an animal's. Slow diffusion is therefore enough.
  • At night there is no photosynthesis, so the only exchange is respiration: O₂ in, CO₂ out — exactly like an animal. By day, photosynthesis runs several times faster than respiration, so the leaf's own CO₂ is used up at once and the net flow is CO₂ in, O₂ out.

The tube experiment this sets up — 2018-S Q41

Eight sealed tubes of pond water: some with a pond weed, some with a pond snail, some with both, some with neither; four in bright light and four in the dark. Which has the most oxygen after 24 hours, and which the most carbon dioxide? Key (4): most O₂ in C, the tube with weed only, in the light (the weed photosynthesises and there is no animal to consume the oxygen); most CO₂ in F, the tube with weed and snail together, in the dark (nothing photosynthesises, and two living things respire). The trap is forgetting that the weed respires in the dark too. Worked example 2 runs the same machine one step further.

Proving that a seed respires — the flask-and-KOH set-up

The same two facts — living things use up O₂ and give out CO₂, and KOH absorbs CO₂ — run a classic practical that a paper can draw. Put moist germinating seeds in a corked flask with a small tube of KOH solution hanging inside, and lead a delivery tube from the cork down into a beaker of water. Over a few hours the seeds respire: the oxygen they use is not replaced, because the carbon dioxide they give out is swallowed by the KOH. Less gas in a sealed flask means lower pressure, so water rises up the delivery tube. Two controls make it a fair test: boiled seeds are dead, respire nothing, and the water stays put; and plain water in place of the KOH absorbs almost no CO₂, so the CO₂ given out simply replaces the O₂ used and the water stays put too. Read a question about this set-up as "which flask loses gas?"

13Eight worked questions — at TALLENTEX pitch

Give yourself 90 seconds each before opening the working — that is the real paper's average. The elimination reasoning is written out in full, because on the day, ruling two options out is worth nearly as much as solving.

12017-E Q44 / 2024-A Q39 machine, carried one step on · ~90 seconds

In the diagram, the organ that produces a digestive juice containing no enzyme at all, and the organ whose juice acts on all three food types, are respectively

1B and C
2D and E
3C and F
4B and E
Working

Step 1 — name the letters. A oesophagus · B liver · C stomach · D gall bladder · E pancreas · F small intestine · G large intestine · H appendix.

Step 2 — the enzyme-free juice. That is bile, and bile is made by the liver — B. It only makes the medium alkaline and emulsifies fat.

Step 3 — the juice that acts on all three. Pancreatic juice: trypsin for protein, lipase for fat, amylase for starch — the pancreas, E. So B and E, option (4).

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) B and C — right about the liver, then picks the stomach as the all-rounder; gastric juice has exactly one enzyme, pepsin, and it touches only protein. (2) D and E — right about the pancreas, but names the gall bladder, which only stores bile. The question says produces. (3) C and F — the stomach again, plus the small intestine; the small intestine's own juice does act on all three, but it is the tube itself, not a gland, and the stomach fails the first test anyway. Elimination. Two seconds of reading kills half the paper: only one organ on the list makes an enzyme-free juice, so any option not starting with the liver is dead. That leaves (1) and (4), and the second half decides it.
22018-S Q41 machine, one step further · ~2 minutes

Six sealed tubes of pond water are set up as shown — some with a water weed, some with a pond snail, some with both. P, Q and R stand in bright light for 24 hours; S, T and U are wrapped in black paper for the same 24 hours. In which tube is the dissolved oxygen highest, and in which is it lowest?

1highest in P, lowest in U
2highest in P, lowest in T
3highest in R, lowest in U
4highest in R, lowest in T
Working

Step 1 — who adds oxygen, who removes it? Only photosynthesis adds O₂, and it happens only in the weed and only in the light. Respiration removes O₂, and it happens in both the weed and the snail, in light and dark alike.

Step 2 — highest. Among the lit tubes, P has a weed photosynthesising and no animal eating the oxygen. R has the same weed but a snail respiring alongside it, so it must end lower. Q has no weed at all. Highest = P.

Step 3 — lowest. In the dark nothing photosynthesises, so every dark tube only loses oxygen. U holds two respiring organisms, T and S one each. Lowest = U, option (1).

Where each wrong option comes from. (2) lowest T — comes from believing the weed goes on making oxygen even in the dark, so that U (which has a weed) cannot be the lowest and the lone snail in T must be. Photosynthesis needs light; in the dark the weed only respires, so U has two consumers and no producer. (3) highest R — "more living things, more photosynthesis"; in fact the snail is a net consumer, so adding it can only lower the final oxygen. (4) makes both mistakes at once. Elimination. P versus R is decided without any biology beyond "the snail breathes": the two tubes are identical except for a consumer. So the answer starts with P, and only (1) and (2) do.
32024-A Q34 / Sample Q37 machine, turned into a route · ~90 seconds

A meal of chapati (mostly starch), dal (mostly protein) and a spoon of ghee (fat) is eaten. The place where each of the three is first chemically attacked is, in that order,

1stomach, mouth, small intestine
2mouth, small intestine, small intestine
3mouth, stomach, small intestine
4mouth, stomach, stomach
Working

Step 1 — starch. Saliva carries salivary amylase, so starch is attacked in the mouth, before the food is even swallowed.

Step 2 — protein. Nothing in saliva touches protein. The first protein enzyme is pepsin, in the stomach.

Step 3 — fat. No fat enzyme you need for this exam acts before the small intestine: fat has to be emulsified by bile first, and only then does lipase work on it. Small intestine. Option (3).

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) swaps the first two — the "everything starts in the stomach" default. (2) forgets pepsin altogether and sends protein straight to the small intestine, which is where it is finished, not where it starts. (4) assumes the stomach handles fat too; it handles only protein. Elimination. "First attacked" is the whole question. Three of the four options already agree that starch goes first in the mouth, and putting fat in the small intestine kills only (4) — so what actually decides between the two survivors is protein: (2) sends it to the small intestine, (3) to the stomach. Pepsin is in the stomach, so (3).
42024-U Q43 machine, moved into the body · ~90 seconds

A student sprints 200 m. Towards the end her leg muscles are getting oxygen more slowly than they are using it. In those muscle cells during the sprint,

1pyruvate is converted to ethanol and carbon dioxide inside the mitochondria
2pyruvate is converted to lactic acid in the cytoplasm, and that step releases no carbon dioxide
3glucose is broken straight down to lactic acid inside the mitochondria, releasing carbon dioxide
4glycolysis stops, so no ATP at all is made until the oxygen supply catches up
Working

Step 1 — which of the three endings is this? A human muscle cell short of oxygen takes the lactic acid route. Ethanol is yeast's route, not ours.

Step 2 — where, and what comes out? That conversion happens in the cytoplasm, and it makes no CO₂ — the only anaerobic route that releases CO₂ is the yeast one. Option (2).

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) gives a human the yeast pathway and puts it in the mitochondria — two errors, and the mitochondria are the aerobic address. (3) skips pyruvate (glucose never goes straight to lactic acid) and adds a CO₂ that this route does not produce. (4) is the "no oxygen, no energy" belief. Glycolysis needs no oxygen at all; it keeps running and still nets 2 ATP — which is precisely how a sprinter finishes the race. Elimination. Any option containing both "lactic acid" and "carbon dioxide" is self-contradictory, which kills (3) on sight; "ethanol" in a human kills (1).
52024-A Q43 / Sample Q39 machine, with the vessels added · ~90 seconds

At an alveolus, blood arrives through the pulmonary artery and leaves by the pulmonary vein. Which single statement is correct?

1The pulmonary artery brings oxygen-rich blood, and oxygen diffuses out of it into the alveolus.
2Oxygen and carbon dioxide are both pumped across the alveolar wall using energy from ATP.
3Carbon dioxide diffuses out of the blood and oxygen diffuses into it, and the pulmonary vein carries the oxygen-rich blood away.
4Both gases diffuse in the same direction, from the alveolus into the blood.
Working

Step 1 — which vessel is which? Blood arriving at the lungs has just come back from the body, so it is deoxygenated: the pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood. Blood leaving the lungs has just been loaded with oxygen: the pulmonary vein carries oxygenated blood. These are the two famous exceptions.

Step 2 — which way does each gas go? Each moves from plentiful to scarce: O₂ is plentiful in the alveolar air and scarce in that blood, so it goes in; CO₂ is the other way round, so it goes out. Opposite directions, by diffusion, with no energy spent. Option (3).

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) is the artery trap — "arteries carry oxygenated blood" is true everywhere except here — and it then sends oxygen the wrong way. (2) confuses diffusion with active transport. 2024-A Q43 and Sample Q39 both offer "active transport" as a distractor, in two of their four options. (4) would mean the blood never loses its carbon dioxide, so breathing out would achieve nothing. Elimination. Two options can be killed without knowing any vessel names: gases crossing a membrane down their own gradients are never "pumped", and two gases with opposite gradients cannot travel the same way.
62018-S Q33 machine, carried to its consequence · ~90 seconds

A well-watered potted plant stands in bright sunlight. It is then not watered for two days, though it stays in the same sunlight. Compared with day one, on day three its leaves have

1turgid guard cells, open stomata and an unchanged rate of photosynthesis
2flaccid guard cells, open stomata and an increased rate of transpiration
3turgid guard cells, closed stomata and a reduced rate of photosynthesis
4flaccid guard cells, closed stomata and a reduced rate of photosynthesis
Working

Step 1 — what does the water shortage do to the guard cells? With little water reaching the leaf, water moves out of the guard cells; they go limp — flaccid — straighten up, and the pore between them closes.

Step 2 — and what does a closed pore do to photosynthesis? CO₂ gets into a leaf only through the stomata. Shut the door and the CO₂ supply is cut off, so photosynthesis slows down even though the light is unchanged. Option (4).

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) assumes sunlight alone decides the rate, forgetting that CO₂ is a raw material too. (2) pairs flaccid guard cells with an open pore, which is the mechanism backwards — and a water-starved plant losing more water is exactly what closing the stomata prevents. (3) pairs turgid cells with a closed pore, the same contradiction the other way up. Elimination. Two options die on internal contradiction alone: flaccid must go with closed and turgid with open, so (2) and (3) are out before you think about photosynthesis at all.
7section 5's experiments A and D run at once · ~2 minutes

A variegated leaf (green in places, white in others) is de-starched, and a strip of black paper is then clipped across it so that the strip covers part of the green area and part of the white. The plant stands in sunlight all day. The leaf is then tested with iodine. Which part turns blue-black?

1every green part of the leaf
2only the green part that the paper did not cover
3every part the paper did not cover
4the whole leaf, since the plant was in sunlight all day
Working

Step 1 — list what starch needs. Chlorophyll and light and CO₂ and water. The last two reach every part of this leaf equally, so they cannot separate one region from another; the experiment varies the first two.

Step 2 — apply both filters. The white areas fail on chlorophyll whether they were covered or not. The covered areas fail on light whether they were green or not. Only the region that is both green and uncovered passes. Option (2).

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) applies only the chlorophyll test and forgets the paper. (3) applies only the light test and forgets the white patches. (4) forgets the de-starching step matters and that a leaf in sunlight still needs chlorophyll. Elimination. When an experiment removes two different things at once, the answer is always the intersection — the region that survives both tests. (1) and (3) are the two halves of the answer; the key is where they overlap.
82017-E Q41 / 2024-A Q36 machine, all four columns at once · ~90 seconds

During a deep breath in, which row is correct?

1diaphragm contracts and flattens · ribs move up and out · pressure inside the lungs is below atmospheric
2diaphragm relaxes and becomes dome-shaped · ribs move down · pressure inside the lungs is above atmospheric
3diaphragm contracts and moves down, becoming dome-shaped · ribs move up · pressure inside the lungs is below atmospheric
4diaphragm contracts and flattens · ribs move up and out · pressure inside the lungs is above atmospheric
Working

Step 1 — the muscle. A muscle can only pull. The diaphragm sits as a dome when relaxed; when it contracts it pulls itself flat, which moves it downwards and makes the chest taller.

Step 2 — the consequence. Bigger chest, same amount of air, so the pressure inside must fall below the pressure outside — and air flows in on its own. Nothing sucks it; the pressure difference does all the work. Option (1).

Where each wrong option comes from. (2) is exhalation, described correctly but attached to the wrong breath. (3) is 2024-A Q36's planted error, word for word: "During inhalation, diaphragm moves downward & become dome shape". Down and flat. A dome that has moved down is still a dome, and it would not enlarge the chest. (4) gets the mechanics right and then reverses the pressure — if the pressure inside were higher, air would leave, not enter. Elimination. The last clause alone settles it: air moves from high pressure to low, so on the way in the lung pressure must be the lower one. That kills (2) and (4) without touching the diaphragm.
