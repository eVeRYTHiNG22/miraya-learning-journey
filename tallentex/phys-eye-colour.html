The Human Eye & Colourful World

Official topic: The Human Eye and Colourful World (Class 10) — one topic, one card. Your IGCSE course covers exactly one corner of it (dispersion by a prism and the seven colours, syllabus 3.2.4). Everything else here is new to you: how the eye focuses, the three defects of vision and the spectacle-lens arithmetic that fixes them, the geometry of a prism, the rainbow, why stars twinkle and the sun sets late, and why the sky is blue. It is a short chapter with a lot of vocabulary — a 30-minute read, then the paper.

What the paper actually asks here

Every Physics question (Q1–15) in all five cached papers — four real sittings and Allen's official sample — was read page by page and every question from this chapter re-solved against Allen's own key — including the 2017 and 2018 booklets, which are image scans and return nothing to a text search.   4. Allen adds a twist NCERT does not: spectacles worn a few centimetres in front of the eye (2024-A). The lens must then image at the far point measured from the lens, not from the eye.   5. Scattering and atmospheric refraction have not been asked in any cached paper — yet they are squarely on the 2027 list and are the easiest part of the chapter to set statement-type questions on ("which of the following are correct?"). They are here at the depth NCERT gives them, no more.

Budget accordingly. This is the thinnest Physics unit on the list — about 4 marks a paper against 16 for mirrors and lenses. Learn the lens-power recipe cold, learn the vocabulary, and do not spend a second evening here.

1The eye — seven parts, and what each one is for

The classic eye question is a labelled section with letters on it: name the part and its job. The names are easy; the marks are lost on the jobs, because two of them are the opposite of what most students assume.

The parts, in NCERT's words

  • Cornea — the thin transparent membrane bulging at the front. Most of the refraction of the light entering the eye happens at its outer surface. (Not at the lens — that is the first trap.)
  • Iris — the dark muscular diaphragm behind the cornea. It controls the size of the pupil.
  • Pupil — the hole in the iris. It regulates the amount of light entering the eye: small in bright light, wide in dim light.
  • Eye lens (crystalline lens) — a convex lens of jelly-like material. It only provides the finer adjustment of focal length needed to focus objects at different distances on the retina.
  • Ciliary muscles — hold the lens and change its curvature, and so its focal length. This is accommodation (below).
  • Retina — the delicate light-sensitive membrane lining the back. The lens forms a real, inverted image on it; its light-sensitive cells generate electrical signals.
  • Optic nerve — carries those signals to the brain.

The eyeball is roughly spherical, about 2.3 cm across, and the distance from lens to retina is fixed — which is the whole reason the eye has to change its focal length instead.

Accommodation — the eye is a lens of variable focal length

A camera focuses by moving its lens. The eye cannot: the retina is at a fixed distance. So the eye changes the focal length of its lens instead, by squeezing it.

Three numbers to know cold

  • Near point — the closest distance at which the eye can see clearly without strain, also called the least distance of distinct vision. For a young adult with normal vision it is about 25 cm. It moves further away with age.
  • Far point — the farthest point the eye can see clearly. For a normal eye, infinity.
  • Power of accommodation — how much the eye's power can change between those two settings. With the retina a fixed distance d from the lens, the power for a distant object is 1/d and for the near point is 1/d + 1/0.25. The difference is 1/0.25 m = 4 D — the standard figure for a normal eye. (Notice it does not depend on d. NCERT does not print the 4 D; it is the value every problem book uses.)

Two things this chapter says that surprise people

  • The lens is not the main refractor — the cornea is. A question that says "most of the refraction takes place at the eye lens" is wrong, and Allen knows students will tick it.
  • The eye's lens must get thicker to see near objects — shorter focal length, more converging. Reading works the ciliary muscles; looking into the distance rests them. With age the muscles weaken and the lens stiffens, so it can no longer be squeezed thick enough — and the near point recedes (presbyopia, section 2).

Cataract. In old age the crystalline lens can become milky and cloudy, so it scatters the light instead of transmitting it — partial or complete loss of vision. It is not a focusing defect and no spectacle corrects it; it is treated by surgery. NCERT also notes that the cornea can be transplanted from a donor, which is why eye donation matters: the eyes of a donor must be removed within 4–6 hours of death.

Where the marks go on this table

  • Myopia is about the far point; hypermetropia is about the near point. A question that gives you a far point is a myopia question even if it never says the word.
  • Presbyopia is not "just hypermetropia in old people". Its cause is different — the muscles and the lens, not the shape of the eyeball — which is exactly the phrase a match-the-following pairs it with.

  • NCERT also mentions that these defects can be corrected by contact lenses or surgical interventions — a one-line fact, occasionally a distractor.

3The spectacle-lens recipe — the one calculation in the chapter

Both 2024 sittings asked this at Q14. It is the lens formula from the previous unit with one idea added: the spectacle lens does not form the image on the retina — the eye still does that. The lens's only job is to make a virtual image at a distance the defective eye can see. Everything below is that sentence with numbers.

The two lines you need from the last unit

1/v − 1/u = 1/f  ·  P = 1/f with f in metres, giving dioptres (D). Sign convention: distances measured against the incoming light are negative, so an object in front of the lens has u < 0 and a virtual image on the same side has v < 0. Concave lens: f < 0, P < 0. Convex: both positive.

Recipe A — a far point is given (myopia)

The eye cannot see beyond its far point F. The lens must take a distant object (u = ∞) and form its image at the far point, on the same side as the object: v = −F.

1/f = 1/v − 1/u = −1/F − 0  ⟹  f = −F,   P = −1/F  (F in metres)

The focal length of the correcting lens is simply the far point, made negative. Far point 125 cm → f = −1.25 m → P = −1/1.25 = −0.8 D. Far point 250 cm → −1/2.5 = −0.4 D. Concave, always.

Recipe B — a near point is given (hypermetropia)

The eye cannot see closer than its near point N, but she wants to read at the normal 25 cm. So the lens must take an object at u = −25 cm and form its virtual image at the near point: v = −N.

1/f = 1/v − 1/u = −1/N + 1/25  ⟹  P = 4 − 100/N  (N in cm, P in dioptres)

That last form is worth memorising: 100/25 = 4 is the power of "25 cm", and you subtract the power of the near point. 2024-U Q14: near point 75 cm → P = 4 − 100/75 = 4 − 4/3 = +8/3 D, Allen's option (4). Near point 100 cm → +3 D. Near point 50 cm → +2 D. Convex, always, and the answer is positive — if it comes out negative you have swapped u and v.

Recipe C — Allen's twist: the spectacles are worn some distance in front of the eye

The far point is measured from the eye, but the lens formula measures from the lens. If the spectacles sit d in front of the eye, the image must be formed at F − d from the lens:

f = −(F − d)

2024-A Q14: far point 4.03 m, glasses 3 cm in front → f = −(4.03 − 0.03) = −4 m → P = −0.25 D, option (1). In that question the 3 cm barely matters (−1/4.03 = −0.248 D rounds to the same option); with a far point of 52 cm and spectacles 2 cm in front, the two readings differ by 4% and a paper can build an option on it — worked example 2. The same correction applies to a near-point question: both 25 cm and N become 25 − d and N − d.

Recipe D — "with the spectacles on, the nearest he can see is …"

A two-step question, and the shape both 2024 papers set up without quite asking. Step 1 finds the lens from the far point (Recipe A: f = −F). Step 2 runs the lens formula the other way: the lens now takes an object at some unknown distance x and must put its virtual image at the unaided near point n, since that is the closest the eye itself can see:

−1/n + 1/x = −1/F  ⟹  1/x = 1/n − 1/F  ⟹  x = nF / (F − n)

Near point 15 cm, far point 60 cm → x = 900/45 = 20 cm. The near point moves outward when distance glasses are worn — a concave lens makes everything look further away, the near point included. That is the physical reason a myopic reader takes the glasses off to read, and why an older myope ends up with a bifocal.

Reading the question — which recipe?

  • "far point", "cannot see beyond", "to see distant objects", "sees clearly up to …" → A: P = −1/F, concave, negative.
  • "near point", "cannot see closer than", "to read at 25 cm", "correct the near point" → B: P = 4 − 100/N, convex, positive.
  • "sees clearly between 75 cm and 250 cm" → both numbers are on the table; the question then tells you which end to fix. 2024-U said "correct the near point", so 250 cm was a decoy.
  • "spectacles worn … cm in front of the eye" → C: subtract that distance first.
  • "bifocal" → two separate questions: the upper part is the distance lens (Recipe A, negative), the lower part is the reading lens (Recipe B, positive).
  • "power of the eye lens itself" → the image is on the retina, a real image, so v is positive — the lens–retina distance, which a question will give you (about 2 cm; problem books often take 2.5 cm) — and 1/f = 1/v − 1/u with u = ∞ or −25 cm. With 2 cm that gives 1/0.02 = 50 D for distance and 50 + 4 = 54 D for reading; whatever the distance, the difference is the 4 D power of accommodation. Powers in dioptres need metres: a cm⁻¹ figure is multiplied by 100.

The three ways to get the right physics and the wrong option

  • Centimetres left in. P = 1/f needs metres. Far point 50 cm is −2 D, not −0.02 D — and a paper will happily print the −0.02 as an option. Convert before you divide.
  • The sign dropped. A myopia answer is negative, a hypermetropia answer positive. If the options include both +2 and −2, one of them is for you and the other is a trap.
  • Answering with the focal length. "Power" in dioptres; "focal length" in metres or centimetres. f = −1.25 m and P = −0.8 D describe the same lens, and both numbers will be on the option list.

4The prism — a slab whose faces are not parallel

You met the glass slab in the last unit: the ray comes out parallel to the way it went in, merely shifted sideways. Tilt the second face and that cancellation is gone — the ray comes out turned. That turn is the whole point of a prism.

The five angles, in NCERT's words

  • Angle of the prism (A) — the angle between its two refracting faces.
  • Angle of incidence (i) and angle of refraction (r₁) at the first face; angle of refraction (r₂) and angle of emergence (e) at the second.
  • Angle of deviation (D) — the angle between the emergent ray and the direction of the incident ray.

One relation competitive papers use that NCERT only draws

The two normals meet at the same angle as the two faces, so r₁ + r₂ = A. The deviation is the sum of the two bends, D = (i − r₁) + (e − r₂). Put them together:

i + e = A + D

Given any three of i, e, A, D, the fourth is one subtraction. A 60° prism, i = 40°, e = 55° → D = 35°. The usual slips are subtracting the two given angles from each other (e − i) or moving A to the wrong side.

Prism versus slab — a contrast competitive papers like to test

  • Slab: faces parallel → the second refraction undoes the first → emergent ray parallel to the incident ray, shifted sideways (lateral displacement). No net deviation, no visible spectrum.
  • Prism: faces inclined at A → the second refraction adds to the first → the ray is deviated towards the base, and because the deviation depends on colour, white light is dispersed (section 5).
  • The angle of deviation depends on three things: the angle of incidence, the angle of the prism and the refractive index of the material — which, for glass, depends on the colour. The bigger the refractive index, the bigger the deviation; that single sentence is why violet comes out at the bottom of the spectrum.

5Dispersion — the one part you already know, made exam-shaped

Your IGCSE course taught the prism spectrum and the seven colours. A competitive paper asks the same thing with the reason attached — refractive index, speed, deviation — and asks it as a chain: violet → most deviated → largest refractive index → slowest in glass. Learn the chain, not the picture.

Definitions, NCERT wording

  • Dispersion — the splitting of light into its component colours.
  • Spectrum — the band of coloured components of a beam of light. Sunlight gives the seven-colour band VIBGYOR: violet, indigo, blue, green, yellow, orange, red.
  • Why it happens: different colours bend through different angles with respect to the incident ray as they pass through a prism, because the glass has a slightly different refractive index for each colour — largest for violet, smallest for red.

Three facts that each make a whole question

  • Newton's second prism. Isaac Newton was the first to obtain the spectrum of sunlight with a glass prism. He then placed a second, identical prism inverted in the path of the spectrum — and a beam of white light came out. That is the evidence that white light is a mixture of seven colours and the prism only separates them, rather than colouring the light.
  • In vacuum (and, near enough, in air) there is no dispersion — every colour travels at the same speed c. Dispersion needs a medium whose refractive index depends on colour. A single colour (monochromatic light) cannot be dispersed at all: it is deviated, but it does not split.
  • A glass slab shows no spectrum even though the colours separate slightly inside it: at the second, parallel face each colour is bent back to its original direction, so they emerge parallel and overlap into white again. Dispersion needs the faces to be inclined.

6The rainbow — refract, reflect, refract

NCERT gives the rainbow one paragraph; the sample paper gave it a question. Every statement-type item about rainbows tests the same three things: the sequence inside the drop, the direction you must look, and the order of the colours.

NCERT's paragraph, reorganised

  • A rainbow is a natural spectrum appearing in the sky after a rain shower, caused by the dispersion of sunlight by tiny water droplets in the atmosphere. The droplets act like small prisms.
  • Sequence in each drop: refract and disperse the incoming sunlight → reflect it internally → refract it again on the way out. Two refractions, one reflection. The colours are produced at the two refractions; the reflection only turns the light round.
  • A rainbow is always formed in the direction opposite to the sun — you stand with the sun behind you and the rain in front.

Beyond the paragraph — the facts the questions actually use

  • Primary bow: one internal reflection. Red on the outside (about 42° from the point directly opposite the sun), violet on the inside (about 40°). Red is deviated least at each refraction, so it leaves the drop at the larger angle from the anti-sun direction — which is the outer edge of the bow.
  • Secondary bow: two internal reflections, so the light is fainter and the colours are reversed — red inside, violet outside — at a larger angle (about 50–53°), outside the primary.
  • A rainbow is a circle, centred on the point opposite the sun (where the shadow of your head would be). From the ground the earth cuts it off, so you see an arc. From an aircraft, with rain below you, you can see the whole thing: the primary and secondary bows as concentric circles — Allen's sample paper, Q4, key (2). The distractor "concentric arcs" was there for people who did not know the bow is a circle.
  • The internal reflection is a partial reflection, not a total one — the ray meets the back of the drop at about 40°, below water's critical angle of about 49°, so most of the light actually escapes and only a fraction turns back. That is why a rainbow is faint. (Critical angle is outside this syllabus; the point is only that "total internal reflection" is the wrong phrase here, and NCERT is careful to say "reflect it internally".)

7Atmospheric refraction — the air is a lens that will not keep still

The atmosphere gets denser towards the ground, so its refractive index rises as you go down. Light coming in from space therefore bends, gradually and continuously, towards the vertical. Three famous effects follow, and each one is asked as "explain why".

Twinkling of stars

  • Starlight is refracted continuously on its way down, through a medium of gradually changing refractive index. Since the air bends the light towards the normal, the star's apparent position is slightly higher than its actual position when it is viewed near the horizon (a star straight overhead is not displaced).
  • The air is never still — its density and temperature keep changing — so the path keeps changing, the apparent position wanders slightly, and the amount of starlight entering the eye flickers: the star appears sometimes brighter, sometimes fainter. That is twinkling.
  • Planets do not twinkle because they are much closer and so appear as extended sources — a collection of many point sources. Each point flickers, but not in step, so the total light averages out and stays steady. It is the planet's nearness (and hence its apparent size) that does the work — not its brightness, and not the fact that it shines by reflected light.

Advance sunrise and delayed sunset

  • The sun is visible about 2 minutes before the actual sunrise and about 2 minutes after the actual sunset — the light from a sun that is still below the horizon curves over the edge of the atmosphere to reach you. The day is about 4 minutes longer than geometry says.
  • The same refraction produces the apparent flattening of the sun's disc at sunrise and sunset: the lower edge, nearer the horizon, is lifted more than the upper edge, so the disc looks squashed.

The everyday version — hot air

Look at something through the air just above a fire or a hot road: it seems to waver. The hot air is lighter (less dense) than the cooler air above it, so its refractive index is slightly lower; and because that hot air keeps moving, the light's path keeps changing. Same physics as the twinkling star, one metre away instead of a hundred light-years.

Refraction or scattering? — keep the two lists apart

Atmospheric refraction explains position and timing: twinkling, the star seen higher, early sunrise, late sunset, the flattened disc, the hot-air shimmer. Scattering (next section) explains colour: the blue sky, the red sunset, the dark sky in space, red danger lights. "The sun looks red at sunset because of atmospheric refraction" is a wrong statement, and one that Allen can drop into a which-are-correct list.

8Scattering — why the sky is blue and the sunset is red

When light meets particles much smaller than itself, some of it is thrown sideways — scattered — and the shorter the wavelength, the more of it goes. Every colour question about the sky is that one sentence plus a path length.

The Tyndall effect, in NCERT's words

When a beam of light strikes fine particles — smoke, tiny water droplets, suspended dust, the molecules of air — its path becomes visible, because the particles scatter light towards you. Sunlight entering a smoke-filled room through a small hole, or the visible beams under a dense forest canopy where mist droplets hang in the air, are the two standard examples. This scattering of light by particles in a colloid is the Tyndall effect.

The colour of the scattered light depends on the size of the particles. Very fine particles scatter mainly blue light (the shorter wavelengths); larger particles scatter light of longer wavelengths too; and particles that are large enough scatter all colours by almost the same amount, so the scattered light may even appear white.

ObservationWhy — in NCERT's words
The clear sky is blueAir molecules and other fine particles are smaller than the wavelength of visible light, so they scatter the short-wavelength blue end far more than the long-wavelength red end. (NCERT: red light has a wavelength about 1.8 times that of blue.) The scattered blue comes to you from every direction — that is the sky.
Danger signals are redRed is scattered the least by fog or smoke, so a red light stays visible from the greatest distance.
The sun is red-orange at sunrise and sunsetNear the horizon its light crosses a much longer stretch of atmosphere. Most of the blue and shorter wavelengths are scattered away before the light reaches you; the red, least scattered, gets through.
The sun looks white at noonOverhead, the path through the air is the shortest, so only a little of the blue is lost.

The sunrise/sunset and noon rows, and the sulphur-beaker experiment below, were in NCERT until the 2023 rationalisation trimmed them; Allen's list is not bound by that cut, and Paper 1 asks them, so learn them at the same depth.

Beyond NCERT — a number Allen sometimes uses

For particles much smaller than the wavelength, the scattered intensity goes as 1/λ⁴ (Rayleigh scattering). So light of 400 nm is scattered (800/400)⁴ = 16 times more strongly than light of 800 nm, and halving the wavelength multiplies the scattering by sixteen. The same law raises the usual follow-up, "why blue and not violet?" — violet is scattered more, but sunlight contains less violet, some of it is absorbed high in the atmosphere, and our eyes are far more sensitive to blue — so the mixture looks blue. Keep this for a numerical option; the NCERT reasoning above is what a statement question expects.

Scattering is not absorption, and not reflection

A red sunset is not "the atmosphere absorbing blue" and a blue sky is not "the sky reflecting the sea". Scattering redirects light without changing its colour; the atmosphere throws the blue sideways and lets the red through. In the older NCERT experiment with fine sulphur particles forming in a beaker, the light seen from the side is blue while the beam going straight through turns orange — the two halves of the same effect.

10Five worked questions — at TALLENTEX pitch

Try each before opening the working and give yourself 90 seconds — the real paper's average. The elimination reasoning is written out because on the day, ruling two options out is worth almost as much as solving.

1Recipe B, run backwards · ~2 minutes

Dadi can read the newspaper comfortably only when it is held at least 100 cm away. Instead of seeing an optician she borrows her neighbour's reading glasses, which are +2.5 D. Wearing them, the closest she can hold the newspaper and still read it is

125 cm
228.6 cm
340 cm
466.7 cm
Working

Step 1 — what the lens is. P = +2.5 D → f = 1/2.5 = 0.40 m = +40 cm. Convex, as a reading lens must be.

Step 2 — what it has to do. Her eye cannot see anything closer than 100 cm, so the lens must take the newspaper at some distance x and form a virtual image of it at her near point: v = −100 cm, u = −x.

1/v − 1/u = 1/f → −1/100 + 1/x = 1/40 → 1/x = 1/40 + 1/100 = (5 + 2)/200 = 7/200, so x = 200/7 = 28.6 cm. Option (2).

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) 25 cm — assuming any reading lens restores normal reading distance. Only the correct lens does that, and hers would be P = 4 − 100/100 = +3 D; a +2.5 D lens is weaker, so it cannot get her all the way in. (3) 40 cm — the focal length, quoted as the answer. A lens's focal length is a property of the lens; the reading distance depends on her eye as well. (4) 66.7 cm — writing v = +100, as if the image were real and on the far side: 1/x = 1/40 − 1/100 = 3/200. The image a spectacle lens makes is always virtual and on the same side as the object; v is negative. Elimination. The borrowed lens is weaker than the +3 D she needs, so the answer must lie between 25 cm and 100 cm but not at 25 — (1) goes at once. That leaves three, and the formula settles it in one line.
2Recipe C — the vertex distance that bites · ~75 seconds

A short-sighted boy's far point is 52 cm from his eyes. His spectacles sit 2 cm in front of his eyes. The power of the lens he needs to see distant objects clearly is

1−2.0 D
2−1.92 D
3−0.02 D
4+2.0 D
Working

The lens must form the image of a distant object at his far point — measured from the lens, which is 2 cm nearer than his eye: 52 − 2 = 50 cm. So f = −50 cm = −0.50 m and P = 1/(−0.5) = −2.0 D. Option (1).

Where each wrong option comes from. (2) −1.92 D — ignoring the 2 cm: −1/0.52. In 2024-A the equivalent slip (4.03 m vs 4.00 m) did not change the option; here the paper has left it on the list. (3) −0.02 D — −1/50 with the centimetres left in — the classic slip of the chapter. (4) +2.0 D — right size, wrong sign. Short sight is corrected by a concave lens; a positive power would make his far point worse. Elimination. Short sight → concave → negative: (4) is gone on sight. A far point of about half a metre needs a power of about −2, not −0.02: (3) is gone. The last two differ only by whether you read the phrase "2 cm in front of his eyes", which is the entire question.
3Statement type — prism and dispersion · ~90 seconds

A narrow beam of white light passes through a glass prism. Read the following statements.

  1. Inside the prism, red light travels faster than violet light.
  2. Red light is deviated through the largest angle by the prism.
  3. If a second, identical prism is placed inverted in the path of the spectrum, the colours recombine into white light.
  4. A rectangular glass slab would spread the same beam into a spectrum in the same way as the prism does.
  5. The frequency of the violet light does not change when it enters the glass, but its wavelength decreases.

Which of the statements are correct?

1I, II and III only
2II, IV and V only
3I, III and IV only
4I, III and V only
Working

I true. Glass has a smaller refractive index for red than for violet, and v = c/μ, so red is the faster colour inside the glass.

II false. Violet is deviated most; red least. (The statement is the exact reverse of the rule.)

III true. Newton's experiment: the inverted second prism recombines the colours and white light emerges.

IV false. A slab's faces are parallel, so each colour is bent back to its original direction at the second face; the colours emerge parallel and overlap — no spectrum.

V true. Frequency is set by the source and never changes; speed drops by μ, so wavelength drops by the same factor.

I, III and V — option (4).

Elimination. Five statements, four options: find the one statement you are surest of and cut. II is the reverse of the most basic fact in the section, so any option containing II is out — that removes (1) and (2) at once. Between (3) and (4) the only difference is IV versus V; the slab is the standard "no spectrum" case, so (3) is out. You never had to judge I or III at all. This is how a five-statement item is done inside 90 seconds.
4Match the following — refraction or scattering? · ~75 seconds

Match each observation in Column A with its cause in Column B.

Column AColumn B
(a) A star twinkles(i) dispersion, internal reflection and refraction of sunlight in water drops
(b) The sun is seen about two minutes before it has actually risen(ii) refraction through air whose refractive index keeps changing from moment to moment
(c) Danger signals are red(iii) light of the longest wavelength is scattered least by fog and smoke
(d) A rainbow(iv) refraction by the atmosphere lifts the apparent position of an object above the horizon
1(a)-(ii), (b)-(iv), (c)-(i), (d)-(iii)
2(a)-(ii), (b)-(iv), (c)-(iii), (d)-(i)
3(a)-(iv), (b)-(ii), (c)-(iii), (d)-(i)
4(a)-(iii), (b)-(iv), (c)-(ii), (d)-(i)
Working

(d) → (i) is the only rainbow description, and (c) → (iii) is the only scattering one. That already leaves (a) and (b), both atmospheric refraction, to be told apart: twinkling is the changing refraction (ii); early sunrise is the lifting of an apparent position (iv). So (a)-(ii), (b)-(iv), (c)-(iii), (d)-(i): option (2).

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) swaps (c) and (d) — pairing red signals with "internal reflection in water drops", which describes nothing about a signal lamp. (3) swaps (a) and (b): both are refraction, but a star twinkles because the refraction changes from moment to moment, whereas the sun's early appearance is a steady lift. The phrase "keeps changing" is the tell. (4) pairs twinkling with scattering — the classic refraction/scattering mix-up from section 7's trap box. Elimination. Fix the two easy pairs first, (d)-(i) and (c)-(iii); only (2) and (3) have both. Then one word — "changing" — decides it.
5Recipe D — 2024-U's numbers, the question they did not ask · ~2 minutes

A girl can see clearly only between 75 cm and 250 cm from her eyes. She is given a single pair of spectacles that lets her see distant objects clearly. Wearing them, the nearest point at which she can see clearly is

157.7 cm
275 cm
3107 cm
4175 cm
Working

Step 1 — the lens. Far point 250 cm → f = −250 cm (Recipe A; the power, −0.4 D, is not needed here).

Step 2 — push it back to her near point. With the lens on, an object at x must be imaged at her unaided near point, 75 cm, on the same side: v = −75, u = −x:

−1/75 + 1/x = −1/250 → 1/x = 1/75 − 1/250 = (10 − 3)/750 = 7/750, so x = 750/7 ≈ 107 cm. Option (3).

The lesson. Her distance glasses have made her reading worse — the near point has moved out from 75 cm to 107 cm — because a concave lens makes everything look further away. That is why 2024-U asked instead for the other lens, the reading one: P = 4 − 100/75 = +8/3 D. In real life she would wear both, as a bifocal.

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) 57.7 cm — the sign slip 1/x = 1/75 + 1/250 = 13/750, which would have the near point move in. A concave lens cannot bring anything closer. (2) 75 cm — assuming distance glasses leave the near point alone. They do not; the lens acts on every object, near ones included. (4) 175 cm — 250 − 75, the reflex of subtracting the two numbers in the question. Nothing in the optics is a difference of two distances. Elimination. A concave lens pushes every image further away, so the answer must be more than 75 cm — (1) and (2) are out before any arithmetic. Between (3) and (4), ask what route produces each: 175 is a bare subtraction that no formula in the chapter performs, so it is a guess, not a candidate. Only (3) is left standing.
