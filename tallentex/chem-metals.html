1Physical properties — and the exceptions

Metals and non-metals are opposites on almost every physical property — and NCERT's point is that the exceptions are why physical properties alone cannot classify an element. The exceptions are exactly what gets asked.

Words NCERT uses

Allotrope — one of the different forms of the same element: diamond and graphite are allotropes of carbon. Sonorous — produces a sound on striking. Metallic lustre — the shine of a pure metal surface. Sample Q16 is this table as a match: sodium ↔ soft metal, helium ↔ noble gas, sulphur ↔ yellow, hydrogen ↔ diatomic.

2Burning in air — basic, amphoteric and acidic oxides

Metal + oxygen → metal oxide, and metal oxides are basic. Non-metal oxides are acidic or neutral. Two metal oxides break the rule — and 2024-U Q20 asked about one of them.

2Cu + O₂ → 2CuO
Copper does not burn — the hot metal gets a black coat of copper(II) oxide.
4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃
Aluminium oxide — the one to know is amphoteric.
Na₂O + H₂O → 2NaOH
K₂O + H₂O → 2KOH
Most metal oxides are insoluble; these two dissolve to give alkalis.
Al₂O₃ + 6HCl → 2AlCl₃ + 3H₂O
Al₂O₃ + 2NaOH → 2NaAlO₂ + H₂O
Reacts with an acid and a base, giving salt + water both times (NaAlO₂ = sodium aluminate). That is 2024-U Q20's key.

Amphoteric oxide

A metal oxide that reacts with both acids and bases to produce salts and water. NCERT names two: aluminium oxide (Al₂O₃) and zinc oxide (ZnO). Both are metal oxides — an option that calls an amphoteric oxide "acidic because it reacts with a base" is half-reading the definition.

How readily each metal burns (NCERT's "Do you know?")

  • K and Na react so vigorously they catch fire in the open — stored under kerosene.
  • Mg burns with a dazzling white flame; its ash dissolved in water turns red litmus blue (basic).
  • Mg, Al, Zn, Pb are covered by a thin protective oxide layer at ordinary temperature that stops further oxidation — which is why aluminium, a reactive metal, still makes safe cooking pots.
  • Iron does not burn as a lump, but iron filings burn vigorously when sprinkled in a flame.
  • Copper does not burn — it gets the black CuO coat. Silver and gold do not react with oxygen even when hot.

Anodising

Making aluminium's oxide layer thicker. A clean aluminium article is made the anode and electrolysed with dilute sulphuric acid; the oxygen released at the anode reacts with the aluminium to thicken the protective oxide, which can then be dyed. (Anode, not cathode — oxygen comes off at the anode.)

The non-metal side — NCERT Exercise 9, which Allen set as 2024-A Q20

NCERT's summary of the non-metal side: non-metals form oxides that are either acidic or neutral (NCERT names no example; carbon monoxide is the everyday neutral one); they do not displace hydrogen from dilute acids; they react with hydrogen to form hydrides; and when they react with metals they gain electrons to form negative ions.

One oxide that is neither — beyond NCERT, asked in 2018-S Q21

Water, H₂O, is an oxide of a non-metal (hydrogen) that behaves as both an acid and a base: it can give or take a proton (H⁺), so it reacts with acidic oxides and basic oxides (SO₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₃; Na₂O + H₂O → 2NaOH). As steam it oxidises iron — 3Fe + 4H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + 4H₂ (section 3) — and it is a poor conductor. That list of four properties was 2018-S Q21; its key was water.

3Water and dilute acids — the reactivity ladder, rung by rung

NCERT builds the series from three tests. Learn the rung each metal sits on — questions ask "reacts with hot water but not cold", "only with steam", "not at all".

MetalWith waterWhat NCERT says happens
K, NaCold water, violentlySo exothermic that the hydrogen catches fire. 2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂ + heat
CaCold water, less violentlyHeat not enough to ignite the H₂. Ca floats — H₂ bubbles stick to it. Ca + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + H₂
MgHot water onlyGives Mg(OH)₂ + H₂; also floats. No reaction with cold water.
Al, Zn, FeSteam onlyMetal oxide + H₂: 2Al + 3H₂O → Al₂O₃ + 3H₂ ; 3Fe + 4H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + 4H₂ (Fe₃O₄, not Fe₂O₃)
Pb, Cu, Ag, AuNo reaction at all—

Dilute acids: metal + dilute acid → salt + hydrogen, but only for metals above hydrogen in the series. In NCERT's Activity 3.11 with dilute HCl, bubbling is fastest and warmest with magnesium, and the order is Mg > Al > Zn > Fe; copper gives no bubbles and no temperature change. (Sodium and potassium are left out — they react violently with the water in the acid.)

Two acid exceptions NCERT prints

Nitric acid gives no hydrogen with metals: HNO₃ is a strong oxidising agent, oxidises the H₂ to water and is itself reduced to nitrogen oxides (N₂O, NO, NO₂). The exception to the exception: magnesium and manganese do give H₂ with very dilute HNO₃.

Aqua regia ("royal water") = freshly prepared concentrated HCl : concentrated HNO₃ = 3 : 1. It dissolves gold and platinum, though neither acid can alone. (3 : 1 — hydrochloric is the bigger part.)

4Displacement and the reactivity series

The cleanest evidence for the order is displacement: if metal A pushes metal B out of a solution of B's salt, A is more reactive than B.

Metal A + salt solution of B → salt solution of A + metal B
Happens only if A is above B in the series. Works in solution or molten form.
Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu
Blue solution fades to pale green; the nail gets a reddish-brown copper coat.
Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu
Blue fades to colourless — zinc sulphate has no colour. (2017-E Q20.)
Cu + 2AgNO₃ → Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2Ag
Copper does displace silver — it is above Ag — and the solution turns blue.

A way to hold the order

K Na Ca Mg Al | Zn Fe Pb | [H] | Cu Hg Ag Au — "King Napoleon's Cat Made All Zebras Feel Proud; He Carried His Silver Gold." The pairs to hold firm: Ca above Mg and Mg above Zn — the two that 2024-U Q29's wrong options reversed.

The displacement grid — NCERT's in-text question 3

You get a table: metals down the side, salt solutions across, "displacement" or "no reaction" in the cells. Rank the metals by how many solutions they displace from, then place each one between the metals whose salts it can and cannot attack. Two rules close most options:

  1. A metal in a solution of its own salt shows "no reaction". That cell tells you nothing — so a metal with "no reaction" in iron(II) sulphate might still be iron.
  2. A metal that displaces from none of the solutions is at or below the lowest metal on the list — if silver nitrate is there, it is silver itself or gold.

Why food cans are tin-plated, not zinc-plated (NCERT Exercise 4)

Zinc is more reactive than tin. A zinc coat would react with the acids in food; tin, lower in the series, does not. The same fact read the other way explains galvanising in section 7.

5How metals and non-metals combine — ionic compounds

Noble gases have a full outer shell and barely react, so NCERT explains all of this chapter's reactivity as the drive to reach a full outer shell. A metal gets there by losing electrons; a non-metal by gaining them.

Ionic (electrovalent) compound

A compound formed by the transfer of electrons from a metal to a non-metal. Sodium chloride does not exist as molecules — it is an aggregate of oppositely charged ions held by strong electrostatic attraction.

The four properties of ionic compounds (NCERT §3.3.1)

  1. Physical nature — solids, somewhat hard (strong attraction between ions), brittle: break when pressure is applied.
  2. High melting and boiling points — a lot of energy is needed to break the strong inter-ionic attraction.
  3. Solubility — generally soluble in water, insoluble in kerosene and petrol.
  4. Conduction — not as a solid (the ions are held in a rigid structure and cannot move); yes when molten or dissolved in water, because then the ions move to the opposite electrodes.

6Getting metals out of the ground — extraction by reactivity

Three words, one sentence each

Mineral — an element or compound occurring naturally in the earth's crust. Ore — a mineral from which the metal can be profitably extracted. Gangue — the soil, sand and other impurities mixed with the mined ore. Metallurgy — extracting metals from their ores and refining them for use (2018-S Q24's definition).

Where a metal sits in the series decides how it is found and how it is extracted. The top metals (K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al) are never found free; middle metals (Zn, Fe, Pb) occur as oxides, sulphides or carbonates; the bottom ones are found free — NCERT names gold, silver, platinum and copper, adding that copper and silver also occur as sulphide or oxide ores. Many ores are oxides because oxygen is reactive and abundant.

Bottom of the series — heat alone (§3.4.3)

2HgS + 3O₂ → 2HgO + 2SO₂
2HgO → 2Hg + O₂
Cinnabar (HgS), heated in air, gives mercuric oxide; heated further, the oxide itself gives mercury.
2Cu₂S + 3O₂ → 2Cu₂O + 2SO₂
2Cu₂O + Cu₂S → 6Cu + SO₂
Copper from Cu₂S by heating in air. In step 2 the sulphide itself reduces the oxide (Sample Q21 calls this auto-reduction; 2017-E Q28 says "sulphide may act as a reducing agent").

Middle of the series — convert to the oxide, then reduce (§3.4.4)

It is easier to get a metal from its oxide than from its sulphide or carbonate — so those are turned into oxides first:

2ZnS + 3O₂ → 2ZnO + 2SO₂
Roasting — a sulphide ore, excess air. Gives off SO₂.
ZnCO₃ → ZnO + CO₂
Calcination — a carbonate ore, limited air. Gives off CO₂ (turns lime water milky).
ZnO + C → Zn + CO
Reduction with carbon (coke). Extracting a metal from its compound is always a reduction.
Fe₂O₃ + 2Al → 2Fe + Al₂O₃ + heat
The thermit reaction — so exothermic the iron comes out molten. Used to join railway tracks and cracked machine parts. Also 3MnO₂ + 4Al → 3Mn + 2Al₂O₃ + heat.

Top of the series — electrolysis (§3.4.5)

Carbon cannot reduce the oxides of Na, Mg, Ca or Al, because these metals have more affinity for oxygen than carbon does. They are obtained by electrolytic reduction: Na, Mg and Ca from their molten chlorides, aluminium from aluminium oxide. The metal is deposited at the cathode, chlorine is liberated at the anode:

Cathode (−): Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na
Metal ions gain electrons — reduction.
Anode (+): 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻
Chloride ions lose electrons.

Copper sits on a border — read which part of NCERT the question means

NCERT's Fig 3.9 puts copper in the "reduction using carbon" band, and §3.4.4 lists it among the middle metals — yet §3.4.3 extracts copper from Cu₂S by heating alone, and the text says copper is found free. All three are in the book. If a question gives you Cu₂S heated in air, it is the heat-alone route; if it asks which band Fig 3.9 puts Cu in, it is carbon.

7Corrosion — and the four-tube experiment

NCERT recalls three corrosions from Chapter 1: silver turns black (silver sulphide, from sulphur in the air); copper turns green (basic copper carbonate, from moist CO₂); iron gets a brown flaky coat called rust when exposed to moist air.

Prevention — NCERT's list, and the one reason it asks for

Painting, oiling, greasing, galvanising, chrome plating, anodising, or making alloys.

Galvanisation

Coating steel or iron with a thin layer of zinc. The article stays protected even if the zinc coat is broken — NCERT asks you to reason it out: zinc is more reactive than iron, so it reacts (corrodes) in the iron's place. That is why Sample Q27's answer is zinc and not copper, lead or silver, which all sit below iron.

Alloys

Alloy
A homogeneous mixture of two or more metals, or a metal and a non-metal. Made by melting the main metal and dissolving the others in it in definite proportions.
Steel = Fe + ~0.05% C
Pure iron is soft and stretches when hot; a little carbon makes it hard and strong. Stainless steel = iron + nickel + chromium — hard, does not rust.
Brass = Cu + Zn
Bronze = Cu + Sn
Poorer conductors than copper — which is why wiring is pure copper.
Solder = Pb + Sn
Low melting point — used to weld electrical wires together.
Amalgam
Any alloy in which one metal is mercury.
22 carat = 22 Au : 2 (Cu or Ag)
Pure gold is 24 carat and too soft for jewellery. 22 carat is 22 parts gold in 24, i.e. 22/24 ≈ 91.7 % gold.

Rule NCERT states: the electrical conductivity and the melting point of an alloy are lower than those of the pure metals. And the Delhi iron pillar near the Qutub Minar — over 1600 years old, about 8 m high, about 6 tonnes — is NCERT's showcase of ancient Indian rust-resistant iron.

Ten traps from the papers and the chapter

  1. Roasting ↔ calcination swapped. 2017-E Q28's false statement concentrated a sulphide by calcination; 2024-U Q28's options swapped W and X. Sulphide ↔ SO₂ ↔ roasting in excess air.
  2. "Least to most" read as "most to least". 2017-E Q24 printed both orders among its options.
  3. Mg put above Ca, or Zn above Mg — the reversals in 2024-U Q29's wrong options.
  4. A metal in its own salt solution "does not react" — that proves nothing about its reactivity.
  5. Dry versus moist litmus. SO₂ (and CO₂) need water to form an acid; dry litmus shows no change.
  6. Amphoteric oxides are metal oxides (Al₂O₃, ZnO) — and they give salt + water with NaOH, not hydrogen.
  7. Ionic bonding is transfer, not sharing. "Share a pair" and "double bond" are covalent words (2024-A Q24).
  8. Boiled water alone does not stop rust — without the oil seal, air dissolves back in (2024-A Q28, tube C).
  9. Iron with steam gives Fe₃O₄, and aluminium is reactive but protected by its oxide layer — "unreactive" is the wrong reason it makes good pans.
  10. Alloys conduct worse and melt lower than their pure metals — the reverse of what "stronger" suggests.

9Five worked questions — at TALLENTEX pitch

Try each before opening the working and give yourself 90 seconds — the real paper's average. The elimination reasoning is written out because on the day, ruling two options out is worth almost as much as solving.

1displacement grid, then a colour · ~2 minutes

Metals W, X, Y and Z were added, one at a time, to four solutions:

MetalZnSO₄FeSO₄CuSO₄AgNO₃
Wno reactionno reactiondisplacementdisplacement
Xdisplacementdisplacementdisplacementdisplacement
Yno reactionno reactionno reactiondisplacement
Zno reactionno reactionno reactionno reaction

Which statement is correct?

1The order is X > W > Y > Z, and Y turns copper sulphate solution colourless
2W could be iron, and the solution W leaves behind in the CuSO₄ tube would be pale green
3Z could be copper
4X could be zinc
Working

Step 1 — rank. X displaces from all four, W from two, Y from one, Z from none: X > W > Y > Z.

Step 2 — place each metal between its "yes" and "no" salts. W attacks Cu and Ag salts but not Zn or Fe salts, so it sits below Zn and at or below Fe, above Cu — iron fits, because iron's "no reaction" in iron(II) sulphate is just its own salt. Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu, and iron(II) sulphate solution is pale green. Option (2).

Where each wrong option goes wrong. (1) — the order is right, but Y shows "no reaction" with CuSO₄, so it changes nothing; one false half kills the statement. (3) — copper displaces silver (Cu + 2AgNO₃ → Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2Ag), but Z displaced nothing, so Z is below silver: silver itself, or gold. (4) — X displaced zinc from zinc sulphate, and zinc cannot displace itself; X is above zinc (magnesium or aluminium, say). Options (2) and (4) are the two halves of rule 1 in section 4 — a "no reaction" in a metal's own salt tells you nothing, but a "displacement" there proves the metal is not that one.
2three clues, three eliminations · ~90 seconds

An ore R is heated strongly in a limited supply of air; the gas given off turns lime water milky. The residue is heated with coke and gives a metal M. M reacts with dilute sulphuric acid, giving hydrogen and a pale green solution. Which is correct?

1R is a sulphide ore; the first step is roasting; M is iron
2R is a carbonate ore; the first step is roasting; M is iron
3R is a carbonate ore; the first step is calcination; M is zinc
4R is a carbonate ore; the first step is calcination; M is iron
Working

Clue 1 — the gas. Lime water milky = CO₂, so R is a carbonate. A sulphide would give SO₂.

Clue 2 — the air. Heating strongly in limited air is calcination; roasting is excess air and is for sulphides.

Clue 3 — the colour. M + H₂SO₄ → MSO₄ + H₂ with a pale green solution: iron(II) sulphate. Siderite, FeCO₃, is exactly such an ore. Option (4).

Elimination. Every wrong option fails at least one clue: the CO₂ kills (1), "limited air" kills (1) and (2), the pale green kills (3). Option (3) is the careful trap — zinc carbonate (calamine) really is calcined and reduced with coke, NCERT's own example — but zinc sulphate solution is colourless. When every option fits two clues, the third clue is where the marks are.
3five statements, from atomic numbers · ~2 minutes

Element X has atomic number 12 and element Y has atomic number 17. They combine to form a compound.

  1. The compound is XY₂, formed when each X atom loses two electrons.
  2. The solid compound conducts electricity, because it is made of ions.
  3. It dissolves in water but not in kerosene.
  4. The ion of Y has the same electronic configuration as argon.
  5. It has a low melting point, because it is made of small molecules.

The correct statements are

1I, III and IV only
2I and IV only
3I, II, III and IV
4III, IV and V
Working

Step 1 — configurations. X = 2,8,2 (magnesium) loses 2 → X²⁺; Y = 2,8,7 (chlorine) gains 1 → Y⁻ = 2,8,8, argon's configuration. Charges balance as XY₂ — MgCl₂. I ✓, IV ✓.

Step 2 — properties of an ionic compound. Solid ionic compounds do not conduct — the ions cannot move (II ✗, and its "because" is the trap: being made of ions is not enough). Soluble in water, insoluble in kerosene (III ✓). High melting point — MgCl₂ melts at 981 K (Table 3.4) — and there are no molecules at all (V ✗ twice). Option (1).

Elimination. V is false, so (4) goes. II is false, so (3) goes. That leaves (1) and (2), which differ only on III — the solubility line NCERT prints. Statements with a "because" are false if either half is.
4oxide trail · ~90 seconds

Metal A does not react with cold water but reacts with hot water, and it floats as it reacts. The oxide of metal B reacts both with hydrochloric acid and with sodium hydroxide solution, giving a salt and water each time. Element C is a yellow solid whose oxide turns moist blue litmus red but leaves dry blue litmus unchanged. A, B and C are

1Mg, Al, C (carbon)
2Mg, Cu, S
3Mg, Al, S
4Ca, Al, S
Working

A. Hot water only, and floats: magnesium (section 3). Calcium also floats, but it reacts with cold water.

B. Oxide reacts with acid and base → amphoteric → aluminium (or zinc).

C. Yellow solid with an acidic oxide that needs water to act: sulphur (SO₂). Option (3).

Elimination. (4) fails on A — calcium reacts with cold water. (2) fails on B — CuO is basic, not amphoteric. (1) fails on C — carbon is black, even though CO₂ would also redden moist litmus. Every wrong option fails exactly one clue, so check all three clues on your chosen option before bubbling.
5thermit, with the mole · ~2 minutes

A rail joint needs 112 g of molten iron from the thermit reaction. The least masses of aluminium powder and of iron(III) oxide needed are (Fe = 56, Al = 27, O = 16)

127 g Al, 160 g Fe₂O₃
254 g Al, 160 g Fe₂O₃
354 g Al, 320 g Fe₂O₃
4108 g Al, 320 g Fe₂O₃
Working

Step 1 — balanced equation. Fe₂O₃ + 2Al → 2Fe + Al₂O₃. Ratios: 1 Fe₂O₃ : 2 Al : 2 Fe.

Step 2 — moles of iron. 112 ÷ 56 = 2 mol Fe.

Step 3 — scale. 2 mol Fe needs 2 mol Al = 54 g and 1 mol Fe₂O₃ = 2(56) + 3(16) = 160 g. Option (2).

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) 27 g — the skeleton equation Fe₂O₃ + Al → … used without its 2: one mole of aluminium. (3) 320 g — "2 mol Fe" taken as 2 mol of Fe₂O₃, forgetting each Fe₂O₃ carries two iron atoms. (4) — the same slip carried through consistently: 2 mol Fe₂O₃ then needs 4 mol Al = 108 g. Check. Mass must balance: 54 + 160 = 214 g in; 112 g Fe + 102 g Al₂O₃ = 214 g out. Only (2) balances.
