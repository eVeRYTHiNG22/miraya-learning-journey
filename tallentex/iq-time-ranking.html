Clocks, Calendars & Ranking

Official topic: Number, Ranking and Time Sequence Test. This is the densest, most formula-able cluster in the whole Mental Ability section — in the 2024-A paper four consecutive questions came from it. Almost every question here is worth 4 marks in under a minute if you know three formulas cold. Learn them, then never think about them again.

What the paper actually asks here

  • Angle between the hands of a clock at a stated time — the single most common form.
  • When do the hands coincide / oppose / make a right angle between two given hours.
  • A clock that gains or loses a few minutes a day — what does it read?
  • What day of the week was a given date (often an Indian date — 15 August 1947, 26 January 1950).
  • Position in a row — total from two positions, or how many people sit between two named students.
  • Counting inside a digit string — "how many 7s are immediately preceded by 3 and followed by 5".

Format-wise this topic is mostly plain and diagram items. About one in three carries a drawn clock face. Statement items (I / II / III — which are correct) show up on calendar facts. Expect a mix: roughly half go in 40 seconds, and one or two will genuinely eat two minutes.

1Ranking — position in a row

The easiest marks in the whole paper, and the ones most often thrown away by an off-by-one. Every ranking formula is really one idea: the person is counted from both ends, so they get counted twice.

Total = L + R − 1
L = position from the left, R = from the right, for the same person. Subtract 1 because that person is counted twice.
R = Total − L + 1
The same formula rearranged — use it to convert a "from the right" position into a "from the left" one before comparing two people.
Between = |L₂ − L₁| − 1
People strictly between two students, once both positions are measured from the same end. The subtraction already drops the first of them; the −1 drops the second.

The two traps that cost the marks

  • "Between" is not "difference". If Ravi is 12th and Meena is 26th from the same end, the number between them is 26 − 12 − 1 = 13, not 14. Positions 13 to 25.
  • Mixing ends. You can never subtract a from-the-left position from a from-the-right one. Convert first. This is the single most common slip in the topic.

Interchange questions

"Ravi and Meena exchange places. After the exchange Ravi is 22nd from the left and 9th from the right. How many students are in the row, and what was Meena's original position?"

One idea unlocks all of these: after a swap, Ravi is standing in exactly the seat Meena used to occupy. So:

  • Total — both of Ravi's new counts belong to one person at one moment, so 22 + 9 − 1 = 30 students.
  • Meena's original position — it is the seat Ravi now holds: 22nd from the left (equivalently 9th from the right).

The rule to hold onto: only ever add L + R − 1 for one person at one moment. Mixing Ravi's new position with Meena's old one is the mistake the question is fishing for.

2Number sequence — counting inside a digit string

A long string of digits, and a condition with the words immediately preceded by and/or immediately followed by. Pure care, no maths — and worth 4 marks in 45 seconds once you have a method.

The method — scan for the middle digit, then look both ways

  1. Underline every occurrence of the digit the question is about (the middle one).
  2. For each, check the digit immediately to its left and immediately to its right.
  3. Count only those satisfying every clause. Never count the first or last digit of the string if the condition needs a neighbour it does not have.

Worked scan. How many 5s are immediately preceded by 3 and immediately followed by 7?

3 5 7 2 3 5 9 5 7 1 3 5 7 4 7 5 3 6 3 5 7

Every 5 in the string, in order, with its neighbours:

#Left5RightCounts?
1357Yes
2359No — followed by 9
3957No — preceded by 9
4357Yes
5753No — preceded by 7
6357Yes

Answer: 3. Notice how close the wrong answers sit — count only the 5s preceded by 3 and you get 4; count only those followed by 7 and you also get 4. Either clause on its own overcounts. Read both clauses before you start counting.

3Clocks

Everything about clocks follows from two speeds. Learn these two numbers and the whole topic collapses into arithmetic.

Minute hand: 6° per minute
360° in 60 minutes. A full lap every hour.
Hour hand: 0.5° per minute
30° per hour = half a degree a minute. It never stops moving — this is the fact that every wrong answer forgets.
θ = |30H − 5.5M|
H = hour, M = minutes past it. If the result exceeds 180°, the answer is 360 − θ (the paper wants the smaller angle).
Relative speed = 5.5° per minute
6 − 0.5. The minute hand gains on the hour hand at this rate — every "when do the hands…" question is one division by 5.5.

Where θ = |30H − 5.5M| comes from

Measure both hands clockwise from 12. After H hours and M minutes:

  • Minute hand = 6M degrees.
  • Hour hand = 30H + 0.5M degrees — the 30H for whole hours, the 0.5M for the creep during those M minutes.

The gap is |30H + 0.5M − 6M| = |30H − 5.5M|. That is the entire derivation; if you ever blank on the formula in the hall, rebuild it in ten seconds from the two speeds.

When do the hands do something special?

Set |30H − 5.5M| equal to the angle you want and solve for M:

SituationSet θ toTimes in 12 hours
Hands coincide (together)0°11 — not 12
Hands opposite (straight line, apart)180°11
Hands at right angles90°22
Hands in a straight line (either way)0° or 180°22

Why 11 and not 12

In 12 hours the minute hand laps the hour hand only 11 times: between 11 o'clock and 1 o'clock there is just one coincidence (at 12:00 exactly), not two. The hands coincide every 720/11 = 65 5/11 minutes, which is a little over 65 minutes — so the coincidences drift, and one gets swallowed.

Clocks that gain or lose

Treat it as a rate: a clock gaining 5 minutes a day gains 5 minutes per 24 hours. After t hours it is ahead by 5 × t / 24 minutes. Two cautions:

  • A clock that is set right at a moment and then gains, shows the true time plus the gain. A losing clock shows true time minus the loss.
  • "How much does it gain in a day" questions about the hands are different: a correct clock's hands coincide every 65 5/11 minutes. If a clock's hands coincide every 65 minutes, it is running fast, gaining 5/11 of a minute every 65 minutes.

4Calendars — the odd-days method

One idea does all of it: an odd day is a leftover day after removing whole weeks. Count the odd days between a known reference and your date, and the leftover tells you the weekday.

Definitions

  • Odd days = remainder when the total number of days is divided by 7.
  • Ordinary year = 365 days = 52 weeks + 1 → 1 odd day.
  • Leap year = 366 days = 52 weeks + 2 → 2 odd days.
  • Leap year test: divisible by 4 — except century years, which must be divisible by 400. So 1900 was not a leap year; 2000 was.
BlockOdd daysWhy
100 years576 ordinary + 24 leap = 124 days over whole weeks; 124 ÷ 7 leaves 5
200 years310 ÷ 7 leaves 3
300 years115 ÷ 7 leaves 1
400 years0the extra leap day in the 400th year pushes it back to a whole number of weeks

Because 400 years carries 0 odd days, the calendar repeats every 400 years — and you can always throw away whole multiples of 400 before you start counting.

Month codes — days to carry, month by month

Add up complete months using their day counts, then take the remainder mod 7. The four 31-day/30-day patterns worth memorising as odd days:

Month lengthOdd daysMonths
31 days3Jan, Mar, May, Jul, Aug, Oct, Dec
30 days2Apr, Jun, Sep, Nov
28 days (Feb, ordinary)0February in a common year
29 days (Feb, leap)1February in a leap year

Reading the leftover

With the counting convention used here (odd days counted from the start of the Christian era), the remainder maps to:

0 → Sunday  ·  1 → Monday  ·  2 → Tuesday  ·  3 → Wednesday  ·  4 → Thursday  ·  5 → Friday  ·  6 → Saturday

Sanity check you can always use: you know some anchor dates by heart. 15 August 1947 was a Friday; 26 January 1950 was a Thursday. If your method gives the right answer on an anchor, trust it on the question.

5Five worked questions — with the elimination said out loud

These are pitched at TALLENTEX level. Try each before opening the working. The elimination line is the part worth copying: on the day, ruling out two options is often faster than solving.

1Clock angle · ~40 seconds

What is the angle between the hour hand and the minute hand at 4:20?

10°
210°
320°
430°
Working

θ = |30H − 5.5M| with H = 4, M = 20:

30 × 4 = 120  ·  5.5 × 20 = 110  ·  |120 − 110| = 10°.

Sanity: at 4:20 the minute hand sits exactly on the 4, and the hour hand has crept one third of the way from 4 towards 5 — a third of 30° is 10°. The minute hand is behind it. 10°.

Elimination: option (1) 0° is what you get if you forget the hour hand creeps at all — the minute hand at 20 minutes sits exactly on the 4, so if the hour hand were still parked on the 4 the two would coincide. (They really do coincide, but not until 4:21 9⁄11.) Option (3) 20° takes the creep as 20° — reading "20 minutes" straight off as degrees instead of 0.5°/min × 20 = 10°. Option (4) 30° is a whole hour-mark, the gap between two adjacent numerals, not an answer to this. Only (2) survives.
2Ranking · ~50 seconds

In a row of 40 students, Ravi is 12th from the left end and Meena is 15th from the right end. How many students are seated between Ravi and Meena?

112
213
314
4Cannot be determined
Working

Step 1 — put both on the same ruler. Meena is 15th from the right, so from the left she is 40 − 15 + 1 = 26th.

Step 2 — the gap. Ravi is 12th, Meena 26th, both from the left. Between them sit positions 13 to 25, which is 26 − 12 − 1 = 13 students.

Answer: 13.

Elimination: option (3) 14 is the raw difference 26 − 12 — the classic off-by-one, and the most popular wrong answer. Option (1) 12 is the over-correction: there is a one-line shortcut here, 40 − 12 − 15 = 13 (chop 12 off the left end and 15 off the right, and what is left is the middle), and 12 is what you get by subtracting a further 1 out of habit. Option (4) is a decoy — the row length is given, so it is determined. Note the two −1s in this topic pull opposite ways: L + R − 1 adds back a double-counted person, while L₂ − L₁ − 1 removes an endpoint. Know which one you are using.
3Calendar · ~2 minutes

What day of the week was 15 August 1947?

1Thursday
2Friday
3Saturday
4Sunday
Working

Split the date into complete years before it plus days elapsed in 1947.

Complete years: 1946 = 1600 + 300 + 46.

  • 1600 years → 0 odd days (a multiple of 400).
  • 300 years → 1 odd day.
  • 46 years (1901–1946): leap years are 1904, 1908, … 1944 → (1944−1904)/4 + 1 = 11 of them, and 35 ordinary years. Odd days = 35×1 + 11×2 = 57; 57 ÷ 7 leaves 1.

Sub-total: 0 + 1 + 1 = 2 odd days.

Days in 1947 up to 15 August (1947 is not a leap year, so February has 28):

31 + 28 + 31 + 30 + 31 + 30 + 31 + 15 = 227 days. 227 ÷ 7 = 32 remainder 3.

Total odd days = 2 + 3 = 5 → Friday.

Elimination: this is one to check rather than eliminate — Independence Day 1947 being a Friday is a fact worth simply knowing, and it doubles as the anchor that proves your method works. If your arithmetic gives Thursday you dropped the +15 and stopped at the end of July. If it gives Saturday, you either counted 1947 itself among the complete years or treated it as a leap year and added a 29 February — both of those land on Saturday.
4Clock — coincidence · ~90 seconds

At what time between 3 o'clock and 4 o'clock will the two hands of a clock be together?

115 min past 3
216 4⁄11 min past 3
317 1⁄11 min past 3
418 min past 3
Working

Together means θ = 0, so 30H − 5.5M = 0 with H = 3:

90 = 5.5M → M = 90 / 5.5 = 180/11 = 16 4/11 minutes.

Answer: 16 4/11 minutes past 3.

The same result the other way: at 3:00 the minute hand is 90° behind the hour hand. It closes the gap at 5.5° per minute, so it needs 90 ÷ 5.5 minutes.

Elimination: option (1) 15 min is where the minute hand reaches the 3 — but by then the hour hand has moved on by 7.5°, so they are not together. That "obvious" answer is the trap in every coincidence question. Options with an eleventh in them are the plausible ones, because every coincidence time has a denominator of 11 — that alone kills (1) and (4) in two seconds.
5Statement item · ~90 seconds

Consider the following statements.

  1. The year 2100 will be a leap year.
  2. An ordinary year has 1 odd day.
  3. The hands of a clock are at right angles 22 times in 12 hours.

Which of the statements are correct?

1I and II only
2I and III only
3II and III only
4I, II and III
Working

I is false. 2100 is a century year, so it needs to be divisible by 400, not just 4. 2100 ÷ 400 is not a whole number → not a leap year.

II is true. 365 = 52 × 7 + 1.

III is true. 22 right angles in 12 hours (twice per lap of the relative motion, 11 laps).

So II and III → option (3).

Elimination: the whole item turns on statement I. Settle I first — it appears in options (1), (2) and (4), so proving it false deletes three of the four options at a stroke and you never need to check II or III at all. On any statement item, find the clause that appears in the most options and test that one first.
