1What a tissue is — and which words to learn

Start from nothing. You do not need to have read a biology book before this paragraph.

Every living thing is made of cells. A one-celled creature like an amoeba does everything with that one cell: eats, breathes, moves, gets rid of waste. A plant or an animal is made of billions of cells, and they have divided the work up. Cells of one kind do one job; cells of another kind do another. A group of cells that look alike and do the same job is a tissue.

The four words, in order

cell → tissue → organ → organ system → organism. Similar cells make a tissue; several tissues together make an organ (a leaf, a heart); organs working together make an organ system (the digestive system); the systems together make the whole living thing. Each step up, the division of labour gets sharper and the body works better.

That is the whole idea of this half of the unit: shape follows job. Whenever you are asked to name a tissue from a description, you are really being asked "what job would that shape be good at?"

2Plant tissues 1 — the cells that are still dividing

A plant grows in a completely different way from you. You got bigger all over. A plant only gets bigger in a few specific places, and everywhere else is finished.

An animal's cells divide more or less all over the body while it is growing, and then most of them stop. A plant keeps a few small patches of cells that never stop dividing, for its whole life. Those patches are called meristems (mer-ist-ems), and every new cell a plant ever makes comes out of one of them. There are three, and each one makes the plant bigger in a different direction.

  1. Apical meristem — at the very tip of every shoot and every root. It makes the plant longer: taller above ground, deeper below. This is why a nail hammered into a tree trunk at your height is still at your height twenty years later — the trunk did not stretch, the tip grew.
  2. Lateral meristem — a thin ring running all the way round inside the stem. It makes the stem fatter. Each year it lays down a ring of new wood, which is why a cut tree stump shows annual rings you can count to find its age.
  3. Intercalary meristem — at the nodes (the joints of the stem, where leaves come out) and at the base of leaves. It is why grass grows back after it has been mown, and why sugarcane and bamboo shoot up so fast. The tip was cut off, but this meristem is below the cut.

What a meristematic cell looks like — and why

Small · thin-walled · one big obvious nucleus (the cell's control centre, which holds its DNA) · packed full of cytoplasm (the jelly a cell's machinery sits in) · almost no vacuole · no gaps between neighbours. Every one of those is about being ready to divide. A vacuole is a bag of water for storage, and a cell about to split in half has no use for one; a thick wall would have to be rebuilt each time; a big nucleus means plenty of the machinery that copies DNA.

That is the standard question. "Which property lets meristematic cells divide repeatedly?" — the answer is always the thin-walled, dense-cytoplasm, large-nucleus combination, never "large vacuoles" and never "already specialised".

When a cell made by a meristem stops dividing and takes up a job instead — becoming a pipe, a store, a support — it has gone through differentiation, and it is now a permanent tissue. The rest of the plant half of this page is about the permanent tissues.

One exception worth knowing (it is in the new chapter)

Totipotency. In 1958 F. C. Steward took phloem cells out of a carrot root, put them in a nutrient liquid, and grew whole carrot plants from them. So a plant cell that has already specialised can go back to dividing and rebuild the entire organism. This is the basis of plant tissue culture, and it is a favourite "did you read the box" question.

3Plant tissues 2 — the skin, and the hole in it

Every mark Allen has set on plant surfaces comes from one small structure: the stoma.

The outermost layer of a plant is the epidermis — a single layer of flat, rectangular cells packed edge to edge with no gaps, covering the whole plant. It is waterproofed on the outside by a waxy coat called the cuticle (made of a substance called cutin). Desert plants have a very thick cuticle; a submerged water plant has almost none, because it has no water to lose.

The epidermis has two local modifications you must know:

  • Root hairs — in the root, epidermal cells push out long thin hairs. They do not do anything clever; they simply give a far larger surface area for soaking up water and minerals.
  • Stomata (singular: stoma) — in the leaf, the epidermis is pierced by tiny holes. A stoma is not a hole in a cell; it is the gap between two cells, called guard cells.

Why a guard cell can open and shut, when no other cell can

Two things make a guard cell different from every other epidermal cell:

  1. It is bean-shaped (in dicots — in grasses it is dumb-bell shaped), and two of them face each other with the hole in between.
  2. Its wall is not the same thickness all round: the wall facing the pore is thick and stiff, the outer wall is thin and stretchy.

So when water flows in and the cell swells, only the thin outer wall can stretch. The cell is forced to bow outwards like a banana, dragging the thick inner wall with it — and the hole between the pair opens. Lose the water and they go limp and fall together: shut. A cell with an even wall would just swell into a ball and no hole would appear.

Guard cells also contain chloroplasts — the little green structures that do photosynthesis, the process by which a plant builds its own food out of carbon dioxide and water using sunlight. No other epidermal cell has any.

Three things go through that hole: carbon dioxide in (for photosynthesis), oxygen out, and water vapour out. That last one has a name — transpiration — and it is not a waste of water. As water evaporates out of the leaf, it pulls the whole thread of water in the xylem up behind it, like sucking a straw. That pull is called the transpiration pull, and it is most of what lifts water from the roots of a tall tree. Section 5 uses it.

Cork — the older plant's skin

As a stem gets old, a strip of cells just under the epidermis starts dividing again (this is a lateral meristem, the cork cambium) and makes cork: cells that are dead, packed with no gaps, and filled with a waxy substance (suberin, in the old book's wording) that stops water and gases getting through. This is the bark of a tree. Dead, waterproof and protective — those three words are the whole answer for cork.

4Plant tissues 3 — the three simple tissues

Simple means "made of only one kind of cell". Complex (section 5) means "made of several kinds working as a team". There are exactly three simple ones, and they differ in one thing only: how thick the wall is, and where.

TissueWallAlive?What it doesWhere you meet it
ParenchymaThin, all round; cells loosely packed with air gaps between themlivingFilling and packing; stores foodThe soft inside of almost any stem, root, leaf or fruit
CollenchymaThickened only at the corners, with pectin (the stuff that sets jam — bendy, like rubber)livingSupport that still bends without snappingThe stalk of a leaf; a young stem; the strings in a celery stick
SclerenchymaVery thick all round, with lignin — the hard stuff wood is made ofdeadHard, rigid support and armourThe husk of a coconut; a seed coat; the gritty bits in a pear

The intuition that answers all of these

A fresh twig bends; a dry twig snaps. Same reason: the bendy support is collenchyma, whose thickening is pectin, and pectin needs water. The coconut husk is hard because it is sclerenchyma, whose thickening is lignin, and lignin is hard whether wet or dry — which is also why those cells are dead. A wall thick enough to be that strong seals the cell off, and the cell inside dies. That single sentence explains every "is it alive?" answer in this whole unit.

Two old names that the new chapter drops

Parenchyma with chloroplasts, doing photosynthesis, used to be called chlorenchyma. Parenchyma with big air spaces, letting a water plant float, used to be called aerenchyma. The 2026 chapter describes both but names neither. Recognise the words; you will not be asked to define them.

5Plant tissues 4 — the two pipelines (the marks live here)

Six of the twelve questions Allen has set on this unit are about these two. Know this section properly and you have the unit.

A plant has two problems that both need moving things a long way. Water and minerals are in the soil, at the bottom, and are needed in the leaves, at the top. Food is made in the leaves, at the top, and is needed everywhere — including the roots, at the bottom. So it runs two separate pipelines in opposite directions: xylem (say "zy-lem") carries water up, phloem (say "flo-em") carries food wherever it is wanted.

Both are complex tissues: each is a team of four different cell types, not one.

The mirror-image fact Allen likes

Xylem: four parts, three dead, one living (the parenchyma). Phloem: four parts, three living, one dead (the fibre). If you remember nothing else about these two tissues, remember that they are each other's mirror image — it answers a whole family of "which of these is dead / living" questions in one step.

The 2026 chapter states the xylem half outright ("xylem parenchyma are the only living component of xylem"). It does not spell out that the phloem fibre is dead — but a fibre is sclerenchyma, and sclerenchyma is dead, so it follows. The older print said it plainly, and Allen's items assume it.

Why xylem needs no energy and phloem does

  1. Xylem is a passive pipe. Water evaporates out of the leaf through the stomata (transpiration). That leaves a gap at the top of the water thread, and because water molecules stick to each other, the whole thread is dragged up behind it — the transpiration pull. Nothing is pumped; the sun does the work. This is why the tubes can be dead, hollow and empty: a dead cell makes a better pipe than a living one, because there is nothing inside to get in the way.
  2. The old name for water climbing the xylem is the ascent of sap — that exact phrase is the stem of 2024-U Q40.
  3. Phloem is an active pump. The sugar it carries is sucrose — not glucose, not starch — and it is loaded into the sieve tube using ATP (the cell's energy currency) by the companion cell at the source — wherever sugar is being made or released, usually a leaf. Loading sugar in makes that spot very concentrated, so water follows it in by osmosis, the pressure there rises, and the sugary solution is pushed along the tube to wherever the pressure is lower — the sink, wherever sugar is being used or stored: a root, a fruit, a growing bud.
  4. So the direction changes with the season. In summer a leaf is a source and the root is a sink; in spring a sprouting potato is a source and the new shoot is the sink. That is why phloem is called two-way and xylem one-way. Moving food about in the phloem has its own name: translocation.

Three traps that have actually been set

1. "Source to sink", never "sink to source". 2024-U Q41 offered the statement "phloem helps in translocation of food from sink to the source" — backwards, and therefore wrong. Sugar goes from where it is made to where it is used, like money from a wage to a shop.

0. The sugar is sucrose. Allen has asked this twice — 2024-A Q40 and Sample Q36, the same sentence word for word, both keyed (3) sucrose — which makes it the most-repeated Biology question in the whole cached set. The distractors are glucose, starch and fructose. Glucose is what photosynthesis makes and what a cell burns; sucrose is the form it is packed into for the journey; starch is the form it is stored in once it arrives. Four marks for one word.

2. Pinus and Cycas have no vessels. 2024-A Q41 asks what carries minerals up in those two plants, and the answer is tracheids alone — not vessels, which those plants do not possess. Both are gymnosperms — plants that bear seeds but never flower (Pinus is a conifer, Cycas a cycad). When the same idea was asked without naming a plant, in 2024-U Q40, the answer was "tracheids and vessels". Same biology, two different keys, decided entirely by whether the stem names a plant. Read the stem for a plant name.

3. A sieve plate is perforated, not dissolved. The vessel's end wall is gone; the sieve tube's end wall is still there, with holes in it. An option that swaps those two around is a classic distractor.

6Animal tissues 1 — epithelium, the lining

Animals have four tissue types in total: epithelial, connective, muscular, nervous. That is the whole list, and this section takes the first.

Epithelium is the wrapping paper of the body. It covers every outside surface (your skin) and lines every inside surface (your mouth, your gut, your lungs, the inside of a blood vessel). Its cells are packed tightly together with almost nothing between them — that is the defining feature, and it is what makes it a barrier: germs cannot slip between the cells, and water cannot leak out.

What changes from place to place is the shape and the number of layers, and each variation is a different job:

The reasoning question they set on this

"Why is the epithelium lining an animal's internal organs usually only one or a few cells thick?" — so that materials can cross it quickly. Thin means fast. It is the same argument as the wall of an air sac in the lung, and the wrong answers offered in NCERT's own version are "for maximum strength", "to store food" and "to reduce friction" — the first of which is what the thick, many-layered kind is for.

7Animal tissues 2 — connective tissue, and what blood really is

The one surprise in the whole unit: blood is a tissue, and it is in the same family as bone.

A connective tissue joins, supports or separates other tissues. Its defining feature is odd: the cells are few and far apart, and most of the tissue is the non-living stuff between them, called the matrix. Everything about a connective tissue follows from what its matrix is made of.

TenDon / liGament — the single most-swapped pair in biology

Tendon = muscle to bone. Ligament = bone to bone. The new chapter's own exercise deliberately offers "tendons connect bone to bone" as a false statement to catch you. A memory hook that works: "liGament — Glues bone to bone", both with a G.

Blood, part by part

Spin a sample of blood or leave it to stand and it separates into two layers: about 55% plasma on top (the liquid matrix) and about 45% formed elements below (the cells and cell fragments).

  • RBC — red blood cell. Carries oxygen, stuck to an iron-containing protein called haemoglobin, which is what makes blood red. It is dented on both faces and has no nucleus, which leaves more room for haemoglobin. Lives about 4 months, then is replaced.
  • WBC — white blood cell. Fights infection. It has a big, lumpy nucleus. WBCs gather at an infected cut — which is why the place goes red and swollen and sometimes produces pus.
  • Platelet. Not a whole cell, just a fragment. It makes blood clot, plugging a leak. Its old name, which Allen used in 2017-E Q34, is thrombocyte.

Three old-print names you may still meet

Areolar tissue — the soft packing that fills space between organs and under the skin. Adipose tissue — fat-storing cells below the skin and around organs; it also insulates. Lymph — a colourless fluid that leaks out of the blood into the tissues and is drained back to the bloodstream by the lymphatic system. That last one is what 2017-E Q34 was testing: the lymphatic system's job is "to drain back the tissue fluid to the circulatory system". None of the three appears in the 2026 chapter.

How to tell them apart in one line each

Many nuclei → skeletal. Branched → cardiac. No stripes at all → smooth. Those three tests separate all three, whatever else the question says. The favourite assertion–reason pair: "cardiac muscle contracts continuously without fatigue" is TRUE, and the reason given — "it has many mitochondria and a rich blood supply" — is also true and is the explanation.

Nervous tissue

Muscles do not decide anything. They are told what to do by nervous tissue, whose cells are called neurons (or nerve cells). A neuron is built to carry a message a long way in one direction, and it is the longest cell in your body — a single one can reach from your spinal cord to your toe.

  1. Dendrites — short branches that receive the signal from other cells. Message IN.
  2. Cell body — holds the nucleus and runs the cell.
  3. Axon — one long fibre that carries the signal away.
  4. Axon terminals — the endings that pass the message on to the next cell. Message OUT.

The brain is the control centre; nerves are the wiring. "The structural and functional unit of the nervous system" is the neuron — that exact sentence is Sample Q45, and the trap option is nephron, which is the unit of the kidney. One letter apart, completely different organ.

9Bones and joints — the part that is new since April 2026

None of this was in the old Tissues chapter, and no cached paper has asked it yet. It is in the new chapter with the title Allen prints, so learn it — it is short.

The musculoskeletal system is bones + muscles + joints + cartilage + tendons + ligaments, working together and taking their orders from the nervous system. The chain of a single movement:

  1. The nervous system sends the order.
  2. A muscle contracts — it gets shorter and pulls.
  3. The tendon passes that pull to the bone.
  4. The bone swings at a joint — and a ligament stops it swinging too far.

A joint is simply a place where two or more bones meet. A joint cannot move itself; muscles move it. What a joint decides is how far and in which directions the movement is allowed, and that depends entirely on its shape.

HingeShaped so it can only fold in one plane, like a door hingeOne plane only: bend and straightenElbow; knee (where a small bone, the kneecap, guards it)

The skeletal system, in numbers worth remembering

  • The adult human skeleton is about 12–15% of body weight.
  • The vertebral column (backbone) is a stack of small bones called vertebrae, with a disc of cartilage between each pair — that cushion is what lets you bend and twist without damaging the spinal cord inside.
  • You have 12 pairs of ribs. They join the spine at the back and the breastbone (sternum) at the front, through flexible cartilage — which is exactly why a rib cage that looks rigid can still expand and shrink every time you breathe.
  • The skull is a hard case of flat bones with fixed joints, protecting the brain, eyes and ears.

10Improvement in Food Resources 1 — crops

A completely different topic from everything above. It is not hard; it is a list. Read it twice, and learn the numbers and the pairs, because that is all Allen has ever asked.

India has to feed a very large number of people from a fixed amount of land, so the yield per hectare has to keep rising. There are exactly three stages to the chapter, and every fact fits into one of them:

  1. Crop variety improvement — make a better plant.
  2. Crop production management — feed it and water it properly.
  3. Crop protection management — stop weeds, pests and diseases taking it.

1 · Variety improvement

Mostly done by hybridisation — crossing two plants with different good qualities so the offspring has both. Crossing two varieties of the same crop is intervarietal; two different but related species, interspecific; two different genera, intergeneric. The other route is putting a single desired gene into the plant, which gives a genetically modified crop.

What they breed for: higher yield · better quality · resistance to disease and insects (biotic) and to drought, salt, heat or waterlogging (abiotic) · a shorter time to maturity, so more crops fit in a year · wider adaptability, so one variety grows in many climates.

Kharif and Rabi — the two Indian growing seasons

Kharif — sown with the monsoon, June to October. Paddy (rice), maize, soyabean, cotton, pigeon pea, green gram, black gram.
Rabi — the winter crop, November to April. Wheat, gram, peas, mustard, linseed.
Hook: Rabi and Rice both start with R, and rice is not rabi — so if you can remember that one deliberate mismatch, you have the pair.

2 · Production management: feeding the crop

A plant needs 16 nutrients to stay healthy. Where each comes from is a favourite question, because the arithmetic is the answer:

The soil's 13 split into two groups by how much is needed, not by how important they are — a micronutrient is every bit as essential, just needed in a tiny quantity:

  • 6 macronutrients: nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium, sulphur (N, P, K, Ca, Mg, S).
  • 7 micronutrients: iron, manganese, boron, zinc, copper, molybdenum, chlorine (Fe, Mn, B, Zn, Cu, Mo, Cl).

You top the soil up in one of two ways, and the contrast between them is the examinable bit:

ManureFertiliser
What it isRotted animal dung and plant wasteChemicals made in a factory, supplying N, P, K
NutrientsLow concentrationHigh concentration
Organic matterLots — it adds humusNone
Effect on the soilImproves its structure: sandy soil holds water better, clay soil drains betterGives a big yield now, but long heavy use destroys soil fertility — the organic matter and the soil organisms go

Three kinds of manure have their own names: compost (farm and household waste rotted in a pit); vermicompost (the same, but sped up by earthworms); green manure (a quick crop such as sun hemp or guar is grown and then ploughed straight back into the field before it matures). Organic farming means using little or no chemical fertiliser and pesticide, relying instead on manure, recycled farm waste, natural pest control and clever cropping.

The three cropping patterns — do not mix these up

  • Mixed cropping — two or more crops grown on the same field at the same time, mixed up with no pattern (wheat + gram). Insurance: if one fails, the other still gives something.
  • Intercropping — two or more crops at the same time, but in a definite pattern of rows (a few rows of soyabean, then a row of maize). The two have different needs, so they do not compete, and both can be harvested separately.
  • Crop rotation — one after another on the same field, in a planned order. Different crops draw different nutrients, so the soil is not stripped of the same one every season.

The one-word test: mixed = same time, no pattern; inter = same time, in rows; rotation = different times.

Irrigation water comes from wells (dug wells and tube wells), canals, river lift systems and tanks; and is conserved by rainwater harvesting and watershed management.

3 · Protection management: keeping the crop

  • Weeds — unwanted plants that steal food, space, light and water from the crop. The three NCERT names: Xanthium (gokhroo), Parthenium (gajar ghas), Cyperinus rotundus (motha).
  • Insect pests attack in exactly three ways: they cut the root, stem or leaf; they suck the cell sap; or they bore into the stem and fruit.
  • Diseases are caused by bacteria, fungi and viruses.
  • Control is by pesticides — herbicides for weeds, insecticides for insects, fungicides for fungus — but these poison soil and water, so the preferred methods are preventive: a proper seed bed, timely sowing, intercropping, crop rotation, resistant varieties and summer ploughing.

Losses in storage — the biotic / abiotic split

Biotic (living causes): insects, rodents, fungi, mites, bacteria.
Abiotic (non-living causes): the wrong moisture and the wrong temperature.
They cause weight loss, poor germination, discolouration and loss of quality. Prevented by cleaning the produce, drying it properly (in the sun, then in shade) and fumigating the store.

11Improvement in Food Resources 2 — animals (both sample questions came from here)

Cattle, poultry, fish, bees. Learn it as four lists of pairs — because both times Allen has asked, the question was "which pairing is wrong?".

Cattle farming

Cattle are kept for two different purposes: milch animals give milk, draught animals do farm work. The lactation period is the stretch of time after a cow calves during which she gives milk — so a longer lactation period means more milk per calf.

Indigenous / Indian (Bos indicus)Exotic / foreign (Bos taurus)
BreedsSahiwal, Red SindhiJersey, Brown Swiss
Their strengthResistance to disease — they cope with Indian conditionsLong lactation period — they give milk for longer

Cross-breeding puts the two together: the foreign cow's long lactation with the Indian cow's disease resistance. Buffaloes are a different animal (Bos bubalis) with their own breeds — Murrah and Mehsana. Their feed is of two kinds: roughage, which is mostly fibre, and concentrates, which are rich in protein.

Sample Q40, taken apart

Four pairs; find the wrong ones. (a) Apis mellifera : little bee — WRONG, mellifera is the Italian bee; the little bee is A. florea. (b) Sahiwal : shows excellent resistance — right, it is indigenous. (c) Jersey : has a long lactation period — right, it is exotic. (d) Murrah : a high-yielding variety of cow — WRONG, Murrah is a buffalo. So the answer is a and d, option (4). Notice that neither wrong pair needed any understanding — just the list.

Poultry farming

Two words, and they are the whole topic: a layer is kept for eggs, a broiler is kept for meat. Broilers are given feed rich in protein with enough fat and plenty of vitamins A and K, and are ready in about six weeks. Indian breeds (such as Aseel) are crossed with exotic ones (such as Leghorn) to get more and better chicks, small size, tolerance of high temperature and low maintenance.

Fish farming

Fish come from two places: capture fishing (catching wild fish) and culture fishing (farming them). Water is either marine (sea: pomfret, mackerel, tuna, sardines, Bombay duck) or inland (fresh water and brackish water, where the culture fishing is done).

The best-known technique is composite fish culture — and it is a genuinely clever idea:

The six fish and their feeding levels

  • Catla and Silver carp — surface feeders
  • Rohu — middle zone (column) feeder
  • Mrigal and Common carp — bottom feeders
  • Grass carp — eats the weeds

Because each species eats at a different depth, five or six of them share one pond without competing, and every scrap of food in the water gets used. Sample Q44's wrong pairing was "Catla – column feeder"; Catla is a surface feeder. That same question offered "Silver carp – surface feeder" as one of its correct pairings, so know the silver carp too. The one real problem with the method is the supply of seed (young fish): most of these species breed only during the monsoon, so farmers use hormonal stimulation to breed them all year round.

Bee-keeping (apiculture)

Kept for honey and for wax. Four species are named, and this is a pure memory list:

SpeciesCommon nameNote
Apis cerana indicaIndian beeThe common local one
Apis dorsataRock beeLocal

Apis melliferaItalian beeBrought in for commercial honey: collects a lot, stings less, breeds fast, stays put in its hive

Pasturage is the flowers available to the bees to collect nectar and pollen from. It decides both the quantity and the taste of the honey — which is why honey is sold as "mustard honey" or "litchi honey".

13Glossary — every word the real papers use

If a word in a question stops you, it is almost certainly here. Words marked ◆ are in the older print only — the 2026 chapter has dropped them. Three of them have actually turned up in Allen's cached papers (guard cell, thrombocyte / lymphatic system, vascular bundle); the rest are there so an older-style question cannot stop you.

cellThe smallest unit a living thing is built from.
nucleusThe control centre inside a cell; it holds the DNA. "Has it got one?" separates a sieve tube from a companion cell, and skeletal muscle from cardiac.
cytoplasmThe jelly filling a cell, where its machinery sits.
chloroplastThe green structure inside a plant cell that does photosynthesis. Guard cells have them; other epidermal cells do not.
photosynthesisHow a plant makes its own food (a sugar) out of carbon dioxide and water, using the energy in sunlight.
osmosisWater moving on its own towards wherever a solution is more concentrated.
sucroseThe sugar the phloem actually carries — table sugar. Glucose is what is made and burnt; starch is how it is stored.
tissueA group of similar cells doing the same job.
meristemA patch of plant cells that never stops dividing.
differentiationA new cell losing the power to divide and taking up a job instead.
permanent tissueAny tissue whose cells have finished differentiating.
simple / complex tissueMade of one cell type / of several working together.
epidermisThe plant's outermost single layer of cells.
cuticleThe waxy waterproof coat (of cutin) over the epidermis.
stoma (plural stomata)A pore in the leaf epidermis, between two guard cells.
guard cell ◆ One of the two bean-shaped cells that open and shut a stoma.
transpirationWater vapour escaping from a plant, mostly through the stomata.
transpiration pullThe upward drag on the water thread in the xylem caused by that escape.
cork / suberinDead waterproof cells forming bark; suberin is the waxy substance in them.
pectinThe bendy thickening at the corners of a collenchyma cell.
ligninThe hard thickening that makes sclerenchyma rigid — and kills the cell.
tracheidA narrow, dead, tapered xylem tube; water seeps between them through pits.
vesselA wide, dead xylem pipe whose end walls have dissolved away.
sieve tubeThe living phloem tube whose end walls are perforated.
sieve plateThat perforated end wall.
companion cellThe nucleated cell beside a sieve tube that runs it.
ascent of sapThe old name for water climbing the xylem.
translocationThe movement of food through the phloem.
source / sinkWhere sugar is made or released / where it is used or stored.
vascular bundle ◆ Xylem and phloem running together as one strand. The new chapter calls it vascular tissue; 2024-A Q41 says "vascular bundle".
chlorenchyma / aerenchyma ◆ Parenchyma with chloroplasts / with big air spaces.
totipotencyA specialised plant cell's power to regrow a whole plant.
epitheliumThe tightly packed tissue that covers and lines.
squamous ◆ Flat, scale-like epithelium — for fast exchange.
stratified squamous ◆ The many-layered kind — for protection.
cuboidal / columnarCube-shaped / tall pillar-shaped epithelium. Both words are still in the 2026 chapter's own table.
ciliated / glandular ◆ Epithelium bearing cilia / making a secretion.
matrixThe non-living material between the cells of a connective tissue.
plasmaBlood's liquid matrix — about 55% of blood.
haemoglobinThe iron-containing protein in RBCs that carries oxygen.
thrombocyte ◆ A platelet — the fragment that clots blood.
lymph / lymphatic system ◆ Fluid that leaks from blood into the tissues, and the system that drains it back.

14Seven worked questions — at TALLENTEX pitch

Try each before opening the working, and give yourself 90 seconds — the real paper's average. The elimination reasoning is written out because on the day, ruling two options out is worth almost as much as knowing the answer.

12024-A Q41 machine, carried one step on · ~60 seconds

A student traces the path water takes from root to leaf in a pine (Pinus) and in a mango tree. Which statement is correct?

1In both, water moves only through vessels.
2In Pinus it moves through sieve tubes; in mango, through vessels.
3In Pinus, through tracheids only; in mango, through both tracheids and vessels.
4In both, water moves only through tracheids.
Working

Step 1 — which pipeline? Water and minerals means xylem, always. Phloem carries food.

Step 2 — which plants are named? Pinus and Cycas are not flowering plants, and they have no vessels at all — their xylem is tracheids, fibres and parenchyma. A mango is a flowering plant, so it has both tracheids and vessels.

Step 3. Tracheids only, versus tracheids and vessels — option (3).

Where each wrong option comes from. (2) puts water in the sieve tube, which is phloem and carries food — cross it out before reading the rest of the line. (1) gives Pinus vessels it does not have. (4) takes the mango's vessels away. Elimination. One glance at "sieve tube" kills (2). Then the only question left is whether Pinus has vessels — and if you know that one fact, (1) and (4) both fall. That single fact is worth four marks here, and it was worth four marks in 2024-A.
22018-S Q33 machine, one statement added · ~90 seconds

Consider these statements about the epidermis of a leaf:

  1. Guard cells contain chloroplasts; the other epidermal cells do not.
  2. The wall of a guard cell is equally thick all the way round.
  3. A stoma opens when its guard cells take in water and swell.
  4. Transpiration and gaseous exchange happen mainly through the stomata.
1I, III and IV
2I, II and III
3II, III and IV
4All four
Working

Take them one at a time — that is the whole skill in a statement question.

I — true. Chloroplasts in the guard cells only; that is one of the two things that make them special.

II — false. The wall is deliberately uneven: thick on the pore side, thin on the outside. If it were even, the cell would swell into a ball and no pore would open. This is the one statement being tested; everything else is padding.

III — true. Water in → turgid → they bow apart → open.

IV — true. Both gases and water vapour use the stomata.

So I, III and IV — option (1).

Elimination. The moment you have judged II false, every option containing II is dead — that removes (2), (3) and (4) in one stroke and you never have to check the rest. In a four-statement item, always look for the one statement that appears in three options; judging it correctly usually finishes the question.
3Counting item — the shape Allen likes best · ~75 seconds

How many of the following are dead while doing their job?

tracheid · sieve tube · xylem fibre · companion cell · phloem fibre · xylem parenchyma · vessel · collenchyma

1three
2five
3six
4four
Working

Go along the list and mark each one:

Dead: tracheid ✓ · xylem fibre ✓ · phloem fibre ✓ · vessel ✓ — that is four.

Living: sieve tube (alive, though it has lost its nucleus) · companion cell · xylem parenchyma (the one living part of xylem) · collenchyma (thickened corners, still alive).

Option (4).

The shortcut. Use the mirror-image rule instead of the list: xylem is 3 dead + 1 living, phloem is 3 living + 1 dead. The list above contains three of xylem's four dead parts (tracheid, fibre, vessel) plus phloem's single dead part (its fibre) = 4, and collenchyma is the decoy — a thickened wall makes you think "dead", but only lignin does that, and collenchyma's thickening is pectin. (3) six is what you get if you also count the sieve tube and companion cell, the classic error of assuming that "no nucleus" means dead.
4Description → name, two steps · ~60 seconds

A tissue has very few cells and a great deal of material between them; that material is hard and rigid because it is packed with calcium and phosphorus compounds. The tissue is ______, and the structure that carries a muscle's pull to it is the ______.

1cartilage … ligament
2bone … tendon
3bone … ligament
4cartilage … tendon
Working

Step 1 — read the matrix. "Few cells, a lot of material between them" says connective tissue. "Hard and rigid, calcium and phosphorus" says bone — cartilage's matrix is soft and jelly-like, which is why your ear bends and your elbow does not.

Step 2 — which connector? Muscle to bone is a tendon. Bone to bone is a ligament.

Option (2).

Elimination. The two halves are independent, so each one halves the list: "bone" kills (1) and (4); "tendon" then kills (3). You do not need both facts to be certain — either one on its own takes you to a 50-50, which is worth guessing at +4/−1 (expected value +1.5 marks). liGament Glues bone to bone is the hook for the half people get wrong.
5Identify from a description · ~60 seconds

A muscle fibre seen under a microscope is cylindrical, branches and rejoins its neighbours, has a single nucleus and very faint stripes. The muscle it came from

1lies in the wall of the intestine and is not under your control.
2is attached to the skeleton and is under your control.
3lies in the heart and is not under your control.
4lies in the heart and is under your control.
Working

Step 1 — "branches" settles it. Only cardiac muscle branches. Skeletal fibres are long unbranched cylinders; smooth cells are separate spindles. So the muscle is in the heart — and cardiac muscle is found nowhere else in the body.

Step 2 — control. You cannot decide to stop your heart, so it is involuntary. Option (3).

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) intestine = smooth muscle: spindle-shaped, one nucleus, no stripes at all — it matches the nucleus but fails on branching and on the faint stripes. (2) skeletal: unbranched, and many nuclei per fibre. (4) gets the organ right and the control wrong — the trap for anyone who stops reading after the first half of the option. Read both halves of every option.
6Sample Q40 / Q44 machine, fresh pairs · ~75 seconds

Which of the following pairs are incorrectly matched?

  • (a) Apis dorsata — rock bee
  • (b) Mrigal — surface feeder
  • (c) Red Sindhi — exotic breed of cattle
  • (d) Broiler — poultry bird reared for meat
1a and c
2b and c
3b and d
4a and d
Working

(a) correct. A. dorsata really is the rock bee. (cerana indica = Indian, florea = little, mellifera = Italian.)

(b) WRONG. Mrigal is a bottom feeder, with the common carp. Catla is the surface one and Rohu the middle.

(c) WRONG. Red Sindhi is indigenous — an Indian breed, prized for disease resistance. The exotic breeds are Jersey and Brown Swiss.

(d) correct. Broiler = meat, layer = eggs.

b and c — option (2).

How this question type actually works. There is no reasoning in it at all, only recall — which is why it is worth learning the four lists as pairs rather than as prose. And notice the structure: every option names exactly two of the four, so finding one wrong pair with certainty already cuts the four options to two.
7The one piece of arithmetic in the topic · ~90 seconds

A crop needs sixteen nutrients. A farmer applies a fertiliser supplying only nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium. Of the thirteen nutrients that come from the soil, how many are not supplied by that fertiliser, and how many of those are macronutrients?

113 and 6
210 and 3
310 and 6
413 and 3
Working

Step 1 — the split. 16 in all: 2 from the air (carbon, oxygen), 1 from water (hydrogen), 13 from the soil.

Step 2 — subtract the fertiliser. N, P and K are three of the soil's thirteen, so 13 − 3 = 10 are left unsupplied.

Step 3 — how many of those ten are macronutrients? The six macros are N, P, K, Ca, Mg, S. Three of them (N, P, K) have been supplied, so 6 − 3 = 3 remain: calcium, magnesium and sulphur.

Option (2).

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) ignores the fertiliser entirely. (3) does the first subtraction and forgets the second. (4) does the second and forgets the first. The lesson. Every number in this topic is one subtraction — but the question asks for two of them, and three of the four options are there for people who did only one. When a stem asks two things, answer both before you look at the options.
