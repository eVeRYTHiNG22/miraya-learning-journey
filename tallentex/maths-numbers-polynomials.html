Real Numbers & Polynomials

Official topics: Real Numbers and Polynomials (both Class 10) — two topics, one card. Your IGCSE course already gives you prime factors, HCF and LCM, surds and factorising, so about half of this page is revision in NCERT's words. The other half is genuinely new: the zeroes of a polynomial and how they are tied to its coefficients, reading a polynomial off its graph, Euclid's division lemma and the remainder puzzles built on it — and a set of Class-9 identities that Allen assumes you own. A 35-minute read, then the paper.

1Prime factorisation is the machine — HCF, LCM, factors, coprimes

NCERT opens the chapter with one theorem and then builds everything on it. Every HCF, LCM and "how many factors" question in this unit is that theorem applied once.

Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic — NCERT wording

Every composite number can be expressed (factorised) as a product of primes, and this factorisation is unique, apart from the order in which the prime factors occur.

So 756 = 2² × 3³ × 7 and nothing else — however you draw the tree, the same primes with the same powers come out at the leaves. A prime has exactly two factors (1 and itself); 1 is neither prime nor composite; 2 is the only even prime.

HCF and LCM — lowest powers, highest powers

Line up the factorisations under matching primes. The HCF takes the lower power of each prime that appears in both; the LCM takes the higher power of every prime that appears in either.

In words: the largest tile, rod or measure that fits two lengths exactly is their HCF, and the number of tiles is (length ÷ HCF) × (width ÷ HCF); the first moment two repeating events coincide again is their LCM.

The product rule — and its one condition

For two numbers, HCF × LCM = a × b. NCERT states it; the reason is in the powers — each prime's lower power and higher power multiply to the same thing as its two original powers. So if you know any three of HCF, LCM, a, b, you have the fourth: HCF 12, LCM 360, one number 72 → the other is 12 × 360 ÷ 72 = 60.

It fails for three numbers. 6, 10, 15: HCF 1, LCM 30, product 900 ≠ 30. Allen's stems say "two numbers" when they mean the rule to apply; if three numbers appear, go back to the powers.

Four sanity checks that kill wrong options

  1. HCF ≤ the smaller number; LCM ≥ the larger. An "LCM" smaller than one of the numbers is a mis-read.
  2. The HCF divides the LCM. An option pair that fails this is not a pair.
  3. The bigger number is the LCM only if the smaller divides it. LCM(84, 36) is not 84, because 36 does not go into 84. It is 2² × 3² × 7 = 252.
  4. Raise the power already there, don't multiply it in again. LCM(2 × 3, 3²) = 2 × 3² = 18, not 2 × 3 × 9 = 54.

Counting factors — an Allen-module rule, not in NCERT

If N = pa qb rc, a factor of N is pi qj rk with each exponent running from 0 up to its maximum — so there are (a + 1)(b + 1)(c + 1) factors. 300 = 2² × 3 × 5² has 3 × 2 × 3 = 18 factors (including 1 and 300).

Factors with a property — restrict the exponents. Odd factors of 300: the 2 must be absent (i = 0), so 1 × 2 × 3 = 6. Factors that are multiples of 10: at least one 2 and at least one 5, so i ∈ {1, 2}, j ∈ {0, 1}, k ∈ {1, 2} → 2 × 2 × 2 = 8. The trap is counting the forbidden exponent value in one prime while correctly excluding it in another.

Coprime (relatively prime) — and the coprime split

Definition

Two numbers are coprime (relatively prime) when their HCF is 1 — they share no prime factor. They need not be prime themselves: 8 and 15 are coprime; 51 and 85 are not (both have 17).

Testing a pair fast: factorise the smaller and test its primes against the larger. 51 = 3 × 17 and 85 = 5 × 17 → share 17. 26 = 2 × 13, 65 = 5 × 13 → share 13. 57 = 3 × 19, 76 = 4 × 19 → share 19. 29 is prime and 75 = 3 × 5² → coprime. Allen writes the shared prime in the teens so a glance says "no common factor" — factorise, do not glance.

The coprime split. "The product of two whole numbers is 1000 and neither is a multiple of 10." 1000 = 2³ × 5³. A multiple of 10 contains both a 2 and a 5, so all the 2s must go to one number and all the 5s to the other: 8 × 125, sum 133. (1 × 1000 fails because 1000 is itself a multiple of 10.) The same move answers "split N into two coprime factors": each prime's whole power goes to one side or the other.

2Remainders — Euclid's lemma and the three question shapes

The rationalised NCERT print dropped Euclid's division lemma from Class 10, but Allen's remainder items are built on it. Learn the lemma, then the three shapes the stems take.

Euclid's division lemma (removed from the rationalised print; Allen still asks it)

Given positive integers a and b, there exist unique whole numbers q and r such that a = bq + r, 0 ≤ r < b. In words: dividend = divisor × quotient + remainder, and the remainder is strictly less than the divisor. "n leaves remainder 7 on division by 15" is the sentence n = 15q + 7.

Euclid's algorithm finds an HCF without factorising — useful when the numbers are large or awkward. Divide the larger by the smaller, then the divisor by the remainder, until the remainder is 0; the last non-zero remainder is the HCF. NCERT's own example, 12576 and 4052:

The idea underneath is the one worth remembering: HCF(a, b) = HCF(b, a − b) — the HCF of two numbers divides their difference. That single fact is shape C below.

Read whether the unknown is the dividend (A: "a number which when divided by …") or the divisor (B: "a number that divides …"). Allen's option set often contains the other shape's answer.

Shape A worked: the smallest number leaving remainder 5, 9, 12 on division by 8, 12, 15 is LCM(8, 12, 15) − 3 = 120 − 3 = 117. Check: 117 = 14 × 8 + 5 = 9 × 12 + 9 = 7 × 15 + 12. ✓ For "the largest four-digit / smallest five-digit such number", find the nearest multiple of the LCM first, then shift by r.

Remainder arithmetic — the lemma as a calculator

Write the number by the lemma, then simplify inside the multiple. "n leaves remainder 7 on division by 15; what does 2n + 9 leave on division by 5?" — n = 15q + 7, so 2n + 9 = 30q + 23. 30q is a multiple of 5; 23 = 4 × 5 + 3 → remainder 3. (Check with n = 7: 2 × 7 + 9 = 23 = 20 + 3. ✓) Two things go wrong here: dividing 23 by the original 15 instead of the 5 the question names, and forgetting that the remainder must be smaller than the divisor — a "remainder 17 on division by 7" is not a remainder.

What a square (or a cube) can leave — the table Sample Q47 tests

A remainder question about a² is answered by squaring each possible remainder of a. Mod 6: a ∈ {0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5} gives a² ∈ {0, 1, 4, 9, 16, 25} → remainders {0, 1, 3, 4}. So a square can never leave 2 or 5 on division by 6. The table for the divisors Allen uses:

Digit sums, repeated digits, and a gauge that counts in base 9

Divisibility by 3 and 9 is a digit-sum test. A number written with the same digit d repeated k times has digit sum k · d. So every three-digit repdigit 111, 222, …, 999 is a multiple of 3 (digit sum 3d), while a two-digit or four-digit one needs d ∈ {3, 6, 9}. Counting "repdigits between 10 and 2023 divisible by 3": two-digit 33, 66, 99 (3) + all nine three-digit ones (9) + 1111 (sum 4, no) = 12. The usual slip is testing the three-digit ones one at a time and "finding" only three. For divisibility by 9 a three-digit repdigit needs 3d to be a multiple of 9 → d ∈ {3, 6, 9}.

A display that skips a digit is counting in a smaller base. If a gauge never shows the digit 5 — it goes …3, 4, 6, 7… — it has only nine symbols, so each place is worth 9 times the one to its right, not 10. Map each shown digit to its rank (0–4 stay, 6→5, 7→6, 8→7, 9→8), then expand in powers of 9.

3Irrational numbers and surds — the rules, then the two machines

NCERT's section is "Revisiting Irrational Numbers": two theorems, one proof, and the closure rules. Allen's items are surd arithmetic from Class 9 — rationalising, nested roots, conjugates. Both halves here.

NCERT's two results

  • Theorem 1.2: Let p be a prime number. If p divides a², then p divides a (a a positive integer). This is what makes the next proof work.
  • Theorem 1.3: √2 is irrational. Suppose √2 = a/b in lowest terms. Then 2b² = a², so 2 divides a², so 2 divides a; write a = 2c, then 2b² = 4c², so b² = 2c² and 2 divides b too — contradicting "lowest terms". The same proof shows √p is irrational for every prime p; the same idea, applied to a prime with an odd exponent, shows √n is irrational whenever n is not a perfect square.

The statement traps Allen writes

  • "The product of two irrational numbers is always irrational / always rational." Both false — "either" is the only true word. One example of each kills the "always".
  • "There are only finitely many irrationals between two numbers." False: between any two reals there are infinitely many rationals and infinitely many irrationals (1 + √2/2, 1 + √2/3, … all lie between 1 and 2).
  • A decimal like 0.10110111… is "recurring". It is not — the blocks grow, so no fixed block repeats; it is irrational. Recurring means the same block for ever.
  • A square looks like a conjugate product. (√6 − 1)² = 7 − 2√6 is irrational; (√6 − 1)(√6 + 1) = 5 is rational. Check which one is on the page.

Machine 1 — rationalising the denominator

Class 9 Number Systems, and the opening item of both 2024 codes. Multiply top and bottom by the conjugate (the same two terms with the sign between them flipped), so the denominator becomes a difference of squares:

  • 1/(3 − √7) = (3 + √7)/(9 − 7) = (3 + √7)/2.
  • Three terms: group two of them as one. 1/(1 + √2 − √3) — treat (1 + √2) as a block and multiply by (1 + √2 + √3): the denominator becomes (1 + √2)² − 3 = (3 + 2√2) − 3 = 2√2. So the fraction is (1 + √2 + √3)/(2√2); one more step, multiply by √2/√2: (√2 + 2 + √6)/4. Two rationalisations, the second a single surd. Then read off the coefficients the stem asks for.

Simplify surds first (√50 = 5√2, √12 = 2√3) or the conjugate step leaves you with numbers you cannot combine. And keep signs when you read coefficients: in (2 + √2 − √6)/4 the coefficient of √6 is −¼.

Machine 2 — the square root of a surd

To simplify √(a + 2√b), find two numbers p and q with p + q = a and pq = b; then √(a + 2√b) = √p + √q (and √(a − 2√b) = √p − √q, larger root first), because (√p ± √q)² = p + q ± 2√(pq).

  • √(7 + 2√10): p + q = 7, pq = 10 → 5 and 2 → √5 + √2.
  • √(5 − 2√6): 3 and 2 → √3 − √2.
  • When the surd has no 2 in front, make one. 2 + √3 = (4 + 2√3)/2 = (√3 + 1)²/2, so √(2 + √3) = (√3 + 1)/√2. This is exactly the step 2024-A Q46 hides behind "x = 2 + √3, find √x".

Conjugate zeroes — the rule 2024-A Q55 turns on

If a polynomial has rational coefficients and p + √q is a zero (q not a perfect square), then p − √q is a zero too (p, q rational, √q irrational). So a cubic with rational coefficients and one zero 3 − √5 also has 3 + √5; their sum 6 and product 4 are rational, and the third zero follows from the product of all three (section 5). The condition matters: x − √2 has the zero √2 alone — its coefficients are not rational.

4Polynomials — the words, and reading one off its graph

NCERT 2.1–2.2. The vocabulary is short; the skill is the graph — where it cuts, where it touches, which way it opens, where it crosses the y-axis. Every one of those is a coefficient or a zero.

Definitions, NCERT wording

  • A polynomial in x is a sum of terms akxk with whole-number powers; the highest power is its degree. Degree 1 linear (ax + b), 2 quadratic (ax² + bx + c), 3 cubic (ax³ + bx² + cx + d), a ≠ 0 each time. √x + 1 and x + 1/x are not polynomials.
  • The value of p(x) at x = k is p(k). A real number k is a zero of p(x) if p(k) = 0.
  • A polynomial of degree n has at most n zeroes. A non-zero constant has none; the zero polynomial has every number as a zero.
  • Geometrically, the zeroes are the x-coordinates of the points where the graph meets the x-axis. A linear polynomial's graph is a straight line (one zero, −b/a); a quadratic's is a parabola, opening upward if a > 0, downward if a < 0.

Reading a, b and c off a parabola

  • a — the opening. Upward ⇒ a > 0; downward ⇒ a < 0.
  • c — the y-intercept, because p(0) = c. Above the origin ⇒ c > 0; below ⇒ c < 0.
  • b — from the axis of symmetry, the vertical line through the vertex, at x = −b/(2a). Vertex to the right of the y-axis ⇒ −b/(2a) > 0 ⇒ b has the opposite sign to a. Vertex to the left ⇒ b has the same sign as a. b's sign depends on a's — the slip is to read the vertex side as the sign of b directly.
  • Cross-check with the zeroes: sum = −b/a and product = c/a (section 5). Both zeroes positive ⇒ sum > 0 and product > 0.

Cubics — three crossings, or a crossing and a touch

Rebuilding a polynomial from its graph — the routine

  1. Write a factor (x − α) for every crossing and (x − α)² for every touch: p(x) = k(x − α)(x − β)…
  2. Find k from one more point — almost always the y-intercept, p(0). A parabola through (−3, 0), (2, 0) and (0, −12): p(x) = k(x + 3)(x − 2), p(0) = −6k = −12, k = 2, so p(x) = 2(x + 3)(x − 2) = 2x² + 2x − 12.
  3. Only then evaluate what is asked (p(1) = 2 × 4 × (−1) = −8), or expand to read a coefficient.

The two slips Allen prices in: forgetting k (taking k = 1 because the zeroes "looked enough"), and treating a touch as a single zero — each gives a listed option.

5Zeroes ↔ coefficients — the core of the chapter, and the symmetric-function machine

NCERT 2.3, and the part your IGCSE course does not have. Two relations for a quadratic, three for a cubic; every "find α² + β²" item is those relations plus one identity.

ax² + bx + c, zeroes α, β
α + β = −b/a · αβ = c/a. Divide by a first; the sum carries the minus, the product does not.

Forming: x² − Sx + P
A quadratic with zero-sum S and product P — or any non-zero multiple of it. Zeroes 2 ± √3 → S = 4, P = 1 → x² − 4x + 1.

Check the cubic relations on one you can factorise: x³ − 6x² + 11x − 6 = (x − 1)(x − 2)(x − 3): sum 6 = −(−6), pairs 2 + 6 + 3 = 11, product 6 = −(−6). ✓ The sign pattern is the whole trap in a cubic item — the product of the zeroes is −d/a, so for x³ − 7x + 2 it is −2, not 2.

  • α⁴ + β⁴ is two steps — α² + β² first, then square that and subtract 2P². Squaring S² straight to S⁴ skips a step; subtracting 2PS (the cube habit) instead of 2P² is the other listed option.

The cubic machine — and the e₁ = 0 shortcut Allen keeps recycling

Write e₁ = α + β + γ, e₂ = αβ + βγ + γα, e₃ = αβγ. Then

  • α² + β² + γ² = e₁² − 2e₂, and 1/α + 1/β + 1/γ = e₂/e₃ (put over the common denominator αβγ).
  • (α + β)(β + γ)(γ + α) = e₁e₂ − e₃.
  • The recycled item: "α, β, γ are the zeroes of x³ − 7x + 2; find 1/(α + β) + 1/(β + γ) + 1/(γ + α)." There is no x² term, so e₁ = 0 and α + β = −γ, β + γ = −α, γ + α = −β. The sum is −(1/α + 1/β + 1/γ) = −e₂/e₃ = −(−7)/(−2) = −7/2. Two signs to keep: e₂ = c/a = −7 and e₃ = −d/a = −2. The options offered were 0, 7/2, −7/2 and 2/7 — three of them are numbers you write on the way.

Unknown coefficients — three routes

  1. One zero is given: substitute. If 2 is a zero of x² + kx + 6, then 4 + 2k + 6 = 0, k = −5; the other zero is then 6/2 = 3 from the product.
  2. Two zeroes of a cubic are given: the third comes from the product −d/a, then the other two relations give the unknown coefficients. Or substitute both zeroes and solve the pair of linear equations — either way both zeroes are needed; one substitution alone leaves a and b unseparated.
  3. A quadratic factor of a quartic is given: write the quartic as (the factor) × (x² + ux + v), fix v from the constant term, then compare the remaining coefficients. x² − x + 1 a factor of x⁴ + px² + q: the constant forces v = q, and matching x³ and x gives u = 1, then q = 1 and p = 1 — i.e. x⁴ + x² + 1 = (x² − x + 1)(x² + x + 1). Compare the lowest-power coefficients first; they carry the fewest unknowns.

Power sums — beyond NCERT, asked as 2024-U Q47

Given a + b + c, a² + b² + c² and a³ + b³ + c³, find a⁴ + b⁴ + c⁴. Two routes; both are Class-9 identities.

  1. Get e₂ and e₃: e₂ = (e₁² − Σa²)/2, and from a³ + b³ + c³ − 3abc = e₁(Σa² − e₂) read off e₃ = abc.
  2. Then square Σa²: (Σa²)² = Σa⁴ + 2Σa²b², with Σa²b² = e₂² − 2e₁e₃. Solve for Σa⁴.

The one-line shortcut (Newton's identity) is Σa⁴ = e₁Σa³ − e₂Σa² + e₃e₁; it gives the same number and saves a minute if you trust it. Worked example 2 below runs both routes on the same data.

6Class-9 tools Allen assumes — factor theorem, identities, GCD × LCM

None of this is in the Class 10 chapter; all of it appears in the real items. Your IGCSE course has the factor theorem and the square identities; the cube identities and the three-variable one are new.

Remainder and factor theorems

Remainder theorem: dividing p(x) by (x − a) leaves the remainder p(a). x³ − 2x + 5 divided by (x + 2): p(−2) = −8 + 4 + 5 = 1. Factor theorem: (x − a) is a factor of p(x) if and only if p(a) = 0 — the same sentence as "a is a zero". To test (2x − 1), use a = ½.

(a ± b)² = a² ± 2ab + b²
a² − b² = (a + b)(a − b) · (x + p)(x + q) = x² + (p + q)x + pq
(a + b + c)² = Σa² + 2Σab
So Σa² = e₁² − 2e₂ — the same line as section 5.
(a ± b)³ = a³ ± b³ ± 3ab(a ± b)
Hence a³ + b³ = (a + b)³ − 3ab(a + b) and a³ − b³ = (a − b)³ + 3ab(a − b).
a³ ± b³ = (a ± b)(a² ∓ ab + b²)
1 + 8y³ = (1 + 2y)(1 − 2y + 4y²). The middle sign is the opposite of the outer one; the last term is +.
a³ + b³ + c³ − 3abc = (a + b + c)(a² + b² + c² − ab − bc − ca)
If a + b + c = 0 then a³ + b³ + c³ = 3abc. (−5)³ + 2³ + 3³ = −90 = 3(−5)(2)(3). ✓

How the identities are disguised on the paper

  • "2a + b = 12 and ab = 15, find 8a³ + b³." Read 8a³ as (2a)³; then (2a)³ + b³ = (2a + b)³ − 3(2a)(b)(2a + b) = 1728 − 3 × 30 × 12 = 648. The product in the identity is (2a)·b = 30, not ab = 15 — using 15 gives 1188, which is on the option list.
  • A three-cube expression with a plus in front of the triple product — X³ + Y³ − Z³ + 3XYZ — is X³ + Y³ + (−Z)³ − 3XY(−Z), so it factorises as (X + Y − Z)(…). Read the signs off, add the three brackets: (a + 2b) + (2a − c) − (a + 2c) = 2a + 2b − 3c.
  • "Factorise x + 8xy³" — take out x first, then 1 + 8y³ is a sum of cubes. Two of the four options have the middle sign of the quadratic factor wrong.
  • A functional substitution — "f(x) − m = x" just means f(t) = t + m for every t. So f(x − m) = x and f(x − 3) = x − 3 + m; substitute mechanically and the x² and m terms cancel, leaving a constant.

GCD × LCM for polynomials

Exactly the number rule: for two polynomials, GCD × LCM = p(x) × q(x). Given GCD x − 1, LCM x⁶ − 1 and one polynomial x³ − 1, the other is (x − 1)(x⁶ − 1)/(x³ − 1). Since x⁶ − 1 = (x³ − 1)(x³ + 1), that is (x − 1)(x³ + 1) = x⁴ − x³ + x − 1. The GCD of two polynomials is the product of their common factors to the lower power; the LCM takes every factor to the higher power — the same lower/higher rule as section 1.

4Graph → polynomial → five statements · ~2½ minutes

The graph of a cubic polynomial p(x) is shown. It crosses the x-axis at x = −2, touches it at x = 1, and cuts the y-axis at 4.

Consider the statements:

  1. The sum of the zeroes of p(x), a repeated zero counted twice, is 0.
  2. The coefficient of x² in p(x) is 0.
  3. p(x) has three distinct zeroes.
  4. p(−1) = 8.
  5. The product of the zeroes of p(x) is −2.

How many of the statements are correct?

1One
2Three
3Four
4Five
Working

Step 1 — rebuild. A crossing at −2 and a touch at 1: p(x) = k(x + 2)(x − 1)². The y-intercept fixes k: p(0) = k × 2 × 1 = 2k = 4, so k = 2 and p(x) = 2(x + 2)(x − 1)² = 2x³ − 6x + 4.

Step 2 — judge each clause. I: zeroes −2, 1, 1 → sum 0 ✓ (and −b/a = 0/2 ✓). II: the expansion has no x² term ✓ — the same fact as I. III: ✗ — 1 is a repeated zero, so only two distinct zeroes. IV: p(−1) = 2(1)(4) = 8 ✓. V: (−2)(1)(1) = −2 ✓ (and −d/a = −4/2 = −2 ✓).

Four statements hold. Option (3).

Where each wrong option comes from. (2) Three — k forgotten: p(x) = (x + 2)(x − 1)² = x³ − 3x + 2 gives p(−1) = 4, so IV fails while I, II and V still hold. (4) Five — the repeated zero counted as "three zeroes" and III passed. (1) One — the touch read as an ordinary crossing: p(x) = k(x + 2)(x − 1), k = −2, and then only V survives (sum −1, an x² term of −2, two zeroes, p(−1) = 4). Elimination. I and II say the same thing, so they stand or fall together; III is the one clause written to be false, which rules out Five at once. Decide IV — that is where k earns its place — and you are done.
