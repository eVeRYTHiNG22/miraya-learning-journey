TALLENTEX 2027 · Mental Ability · Crash notes

Syllogism, Puzzles & Seating

Official topics: Syllogism, Puzzle Test and Sitting Arrangement. None of this is in your IGCSE course and none of it needs any maths. Each one is a method you run on the booklet — circles for a syllogism, a ring of seats, a grid of ticks and crosses — because every question is built so that doing it in your head drops exactly one link. A 30-minute read, then Paper 1.

What the paper actually asks here

Questions 61–80 of all five cached papers — the four real sittings and Allen's official sample — were read page by page, the 2017 and 2018 booklets rendered from their image scans (a text search returns nothing for them), and every item from these three topics re-solved against Allen's own key sheets. What that tells you:

  1. Two to four of the twenty Mental Ability questions a sitting — 8 to 16 marks — and both 2024 papers put them at the very end — Q78–80 in 2024-A, Q79–80 in 2024-U — where you arrive with the least clock. The 2017 and 2018 papers spread them through Q66–73 instead. The last question of both 2024 papers, and of the sample, is a syllogism.
  2. Syllogism is the big one: eight of the seventeen. Three of the eight key to an "either … or …" pair (2017-E Q70, 2024-A Q80, Sample Q80) and three more dangle "either/or" as a wrong option (2017-E Q73, 2018-S Q70, 2024-U Q80) — and the two 2024/Sample items that key to a pair also offer fake pairs among their wrong options. So six of the eight turn on knowing exactly which two conclusions make a complementary pair (section 1.4). One asks a "… is a possibility" conclusion (2017-E Q73); one plants a conclusion about a group that appears in neither statement (2024-U Q80); one runs four statements at once (2018-S Q73).
  3. Sitting Arrangement is five items: three circular (5, 6 and 7 seats), one row of six, and one line-up of eight Kho-Kho players whose shape the stem never states. None has a figure — you draw the ring. Two are the "which statement is false?" form (2017-E Q66, 2024-A Q79), which means solving the whole table and then testing four statements; one offers "can't be determined" as an option (2018-S Q66) and the arrangement is in fact unique.
  4. Puzzle Test is four items and every one is a matching table — people against professions and cars (2017-E Q71), teachers against subjects with a count at the end (2018-S Q71), professors against courses in two roles (2024-A Q78), kids against colours with the gender words doing half the work (Sample Q78). No floors, no days of the week: those forms appear in Paper 1 because other exams use them, but Allen has not, in five papers.
  5. A defect you should know about. 2017-E Q66 has no arrangement in which only one statement is false: in every arrangement that obeys its clues, statement (3) is false and so is at least one other (under the natural reading, (1) and (3) both), and the key credits (3). When two statements look false, re-check your left/right once; if they stay false, pick either and move on — it is one question, and time is what is being tested.
  6. Nothing here needs a formula and nothing needs a calculator. Every mark is for reading exactly: "second to the left", "not between", "either … or", "is a possibility", "which is false".

Budget accordingly. An 8–16-mark unit. The method for each of the three topics fits on one screen; what takes practice is running it in under two minutes on the booklet, with no second sheet of paper.

1Syllogism — draw the least-assuming picture

Allen's syllogisms are the bank-exam kind: two to four statements, two to four conclusions, "take the statements to be true even if they seem to be at variance with commonly known facts". One skill is being tested — telling what must be true from what merely could be.

1.1 The four sentences and their pictures

Definitions

  • All A are B — every A is a B: circle A inside circle B. It also makes "Some A are B" and "Some B are A" true — a pen inside the box means the box holds at least that pen.
  • No A is B — the circles never touch. It reads the same both ways: No B is A.
  • Some A are B — the lens where the circles cross is not empty. "Some" means at least some, possibly all. It says nothing about the A's outside the lens — so it does not mean "some A are not B".
  • Some A are not B — the part of A outside B is not empty. Says nothing about the lens — so it does not mean "some A are B".

The books letter them A (All), E (No), I (Some) and O (Some … not); the fact bank uses those letters.

1.2 Three rules of reading

  1. Take the statements as true, however silly. "All fruits are leaves" is true for the next ninety seconds. Real-world knowledge about fruit is exactly what the question is baiting.
  2. A conclusion may turn a statement around only when the statement allows it. "No A is B" ⇒ "No B is A". "Some A are B" ⇒ "Some B are A". "All A are B" ⇒ "Some B are A" — never "All B are A". "Some A are not B" turns around into nothing at all. A conclusion that only restates a statement, or turns it around legally, follows — Sample Q68's second conclusion was exactly that (2018-S Q70's keyed conclusion needed a two-link chain first, and then a turn-round).
  3. A conclusion that names a group in neither statement never follows. 2024-U Q80's first conclusion was about a group no statement mentioned; the key was "neither". Scan the nouns before you scan the logic.

1.3 Chaining two statements — the six links

Two statements chain only when they share a term, and the shared term must sit at the end of one and the start of the other — align them that way first ("All A are B · Some B are C"), turning a statement around by rule 2 if you need to — turn the "Some" or the "No", never the "All" (an "All" turned round weakens to a "Some" and the chain loses information). Then only six combinations give anything:

First statementSecond statementConclusion
All A are BAll B are CAll A are C (and so Some A are C, Some C are A)
All A are BNo B is CNo A is C
Some A are BAll B are CSome A are C
Some A are BNo B is CSome A are not C
No A is BAll B are CSome C are not A — note the turn-round
No A is BSome B are CSome C are not A — note the turn-round
Some + Some · No + No · All + Some (in that order) · anything with "Some … not" firstnothing follows

Three or four statements chain the same way, two at a time. A conclusion about the two outer terms of a chain is definite only when every link between them is definite: "All A are B" and "Some B are C" do not put a single A among the C's — the B's that are C may all be non-A. (The third conclusion of 2018-S Q73 dies exactly there.)

The three slips that lose the most marks

  • "Some" read as "some but not all". "Some flowers are red" allows every flower to be red. So "Some flowers are not red" does not follow from it.
  • "All A are B" read backwards. Every kite is a bird; not every bird is a kite.
  • Pushing a "No" down a chain that entered through "Some". "Some cups are steel · All steel things are magnetic · No magnetic thing is cheap" forbids "cheap" only to the cups that are steel. The other cups are free — so "No cup is cheap" does not follow.

1.4 "Either I or II follows" — the complementary pair

When two conclusions each fail on their own but between them cover every possibility, the answer is "either … or …". Three pairs qualify, and the two conclusions must be about the same two groups in the same roles:

PairWhy one of them must be true
Some A are B · No A is Beither the circles touch or they do not
All A are B · Some A are not Beither every A is inside B or at least one is outside
Some A are B · Some A are not Bthere is at least one A; it is either inside B or outside

Because "Some A are B" and "No A is B" both turn around (rule 2), a "Some" or a "No" half may be written either way round: "Some B are A · No A is B" is still the first pair — that is how 2024-A Q80 wrote its keyed pair ("No cloth is a bag" / "Some bags are clothes"; Sample Q80's pair was the plain form). "All A are B" and "Some A are not B" do not turn around, so those halves must keep their subject: "All A are B · Some B are not A" is not a pair.

Two checks before you write "either/or": (i) neither conclusion follows by itself — if one does, the answer is that one, not the pair; (ii) both conclusions use only the two groups in question. 2024-U Q80 offered "either I or II" where conclusion I was about a group in neither statement — a fake pair; the answer was "neither".

How Allen writes the option — and the distractor

The keyed forms in the five papers: "Only either I or III follows" (2017-E Q70, 2024-A Q80) and "Either 1 or 3 follows and 2 follows" (Sample Q80). The wrong options carry the same word "either" with the wrong numbers — 2024-A Q80 offers "Only either I or IV" and "Only either I or II" beside the real pair, Sample Q80 offers "Either 1 or 2 and 3" and "Either 1 or 2 and 4" — plus "None of these" / "Neither", and in 2017-E Q70 each half alone ("Only I follows", "Only III follows"). So the check is never whether an option says "either"; it is which two numbers it pairs, and whether those two are a genuine pair from the table above.

1.5 "… is a possibility"

A conclusion such as "All wheels being flowers is a possibility" asks a different question — not "must it be true?" but "could it be true?" It follows if you can draw one picture in which every statement and the possibility are true together. Test it by trying to draw it; if nothing forbids the picture, it follows. 2017-E Q73 keyed one such conclusion as following. (If the statements already force it, that is a certainty rather than a possibility — Allen has not asked that corner in five papers, and the books disagree about it; do not lose sleep.)

1.6 The ninety-second routine

  1. Nouns first. Underline every group named in a conclusion; a group missing from the statements kills that conclusion at once.
  2. Align and chain. Rewrite the statements so the shared terms meet; apply the six links.
  3. Draw the least-assuming picture in the margin — small, two or three circles.
  4. Test each conclusion against the picture. For every "follows", ask "could I redraw the picture legally so that it breaks?" If yes, it does not follow.
  5. Pair up the leftovers. A complementary pair among the conclusions that failed → either/or.
  6. Only now read the options — every word of them.

2Sitting Arrangement — draw the ring, fix one seat, count the right way

Five of the seventeen items, none with a figure. The whole skill is a convention (which way is "left" for someone facing the centre?), a vocabulary ("second to the right", "between", "opposite") and a routine that reaches one arrangement in under two minutes.

2.1 The convention — a fact about hands, not a rule Allen chose

Stand at the top of the table facing the centre — facing down the page. Your right hand points to the page's left. From the top of a clock face, the page's left is towards 11 o'clock: anticlockwise. So for anyone facing the centre, right is anticlockwise and left is clockwise. It is where your hands are. (Facing away from the centre swaps both — Allen has not asked it in five papers, but read the first line of every stem.)

If a row question does not say which way people face (2024-U Q79 did not), take the natural reading — the way you would face to read the row, right = the page's right. Any answer about who is between whom, or how many sit between two people, is the same either way (2.5).

2.2 The vocabulary

PhraseMeans
"X is to the immediate right of Y" · "X is next to Y, on the right"one seat anticlockwise from Y (circle, facing the centre) — the same seat as "Y is to the immediate left of X"
"X is second to the left of Y"two seats clockwise from Y; nothing is implied about who sits between
"exactly two people sit between X and Y"X and Y are three seats apart — two branches, one each way round
"X sits between Y and Z"X is next to both — unless the stem says "somewhere between"
"X sits opposite Y"n/2 seats away; only possible when the seat count is even. In a circle of 8, opposite = 4th left = 4th right; a circle of 7 has no "opposite"
"X sits at one of the ends" (row)two cases — but a later clue such as "Y is third to the right of X" settles the end, because there must be room on that side
"not necessarily in the same order"the names are listed in no order; the listing is not a clue
"kth to the right" in a circle of nthe same seat as (n − k)th to the left: in a circle of 5, third right = second left

2.3 The routine

  1. Draw the ring (or the row) with the right number of seats, tick the facing, and write the seat count beside it.
  2. Put the first person named in a two-person clue at the top. Everything is relative; where you start does not matter.
  3. Place every clue that pins a seat outright — "second to the right of …", "third to the left of …", "between … and …".
  4. Then the loose clues — "exactly two between", "next to". They branch; write both branches if you must. The negative clues ("not next to", "not between") exist to kill one branch. Use every clue. A clue you did not use is either redundant — Allen's items often carry one — or the one that decides the answer, and you cannot tell which until you have used it.
  5. Read the question again before you count — left or right, second or third, "opposite", "between".
  6. If two arrangements survive and the answer differs between them, the answer is "cannot be determined". 2018-S Q66 offered it; the arrangement was unique and the option was a trap.

2.4 "Which of the following is false?"

Two of the five real items take this form. There is no shortcut: solve the whole table, then test all four statements and mark each ✓ or ✗ in the margin. Three will be true. The false one is nearly always a left/right swap or a count-by-one — "third" for "fourth", "immediate" for "second". Because you check four statements, budget two minutes for this form, not one.

Two look false

First re-check your convention on the pair — a left/right slip applied to the statements but not to the table (or the other way round) flips the statements that mention left or right. If they stay false after one re-check, the item is faulty (2017-E Q66 is): pick either and move on. It is four marks, and the clock is the real opponent.

2.5 Consistency — the one comfort

Run a whole question with left and right swapped and you get the mirror image of the table. Every question of the form "who is between", "who is opposite", "how many sit between", "who is second to the left of" has the same answer in the mirror. A wrong convention costs marks only on absolute questions — "who sits at the left end", "who faces east" — and on none of the five real items. What matters is consistency: never switch convention halfway through a question.

3Puzzle Test — the grid of ticks and crosses

Every real puzzle item is a matching table: people down the side, one or two attributes across, and four to eleven clues — most of them either a tick ("T is an air-hostess") or a cross ("M is not a doctor"), sometimes a pairing to tally ("A and B teach Hindi and English"). The exam is not whether you can do it; it is whether you can do it in two minutes with nowhere to write except the margin.

3.1 The grid

  1. Draw the grid before you read the clues properly — names down the side, the attribute across the top. Two attributes (city and instrument)? Two grids, or one grid with two answer columns.
  2. Ticks first. Every "X is Y" is a tick, and a tick empties its whole row and column with crosses.
  3. Crosses second. Every "X is not Y" is one cross.
  4. Link clues third. "The person from Chennai plays the veena" ties two attributes; it becomes a tick only once one of them is placed — note it beside the grid and come back to it.
  5. A row or column with one open cell is a tick. Repeat.
  6. Answer the question asked. Most stems want one cell; stop the moment it is forced.

3.2 The hidden clues

  • Gender words. "M's sister L", "while he is wearing brown", "his best friend P is a boy", "three of the seven are girls" — Sample Q78 turns on them: the two "sister" words are what identify the girls the question asks about, and "no girl is wearing black, yellow or brown" is what then places them. Every pronoun is a potential clue; underline them as you read.
  • Group-level clues. "None of the ladies is a consultant or a lawyer", "none of the ladies owns a Scorpio" (2017-E Q71) — one sentence, a cross in several rows. Apply them the moment you know who the ladies are.
  • Two-role clues. 2024-A Q78's professors each co-ordinate one course and support another, and "L and O have the same subjects" says only that they share the same pair of courses — that their roles are swapped is a deduction from another clue, not part of the sentence. Keep two columns — co-ordinates / supports — and read "has X as one of their subjects" as "X is in one of the two".
  • Counting puzzles. "A and B teach Hindi and English; C and D teach English and Geography …" (2018-S Q71) is a tally: a name-by-subject table, a ✓ for each pairing, then a count per row. Watch for a tie: in that item two teachers tied for the most and only one of them was among the options — the option list settles it. If your answer is not among the options, count once more; if the count stands, choose the option that shares the maximum.

3.3 The forms Allen has not asked (yet)

Floors of a building, days of the week, months — the "linear ordering" puzzles common in other exams. (Queue and row position questions — "Amrita is 10th from the front …" — do appear, but they are counting, not clue puzzles, and belong to the Ranking unit.) They are Sitting Arrangement in disguise: a row with numbered seats ("floor 1 is the ground floor") and clues like "only one person lives between K and L", "N lives immediately below L". Same routine as 2.3, with two extra checks: odd/even clues ("an even-numbered floor below Rekha") and midway questions (the floor midway between floors 2 and 6 is (2 + 6) ÷ 2 = 4). Paper 1 carries one of each so you have met them; treat them as a bonus, not as the core.

What the stem looks like

"Study the following information carefully and answer the question" · "Read the following information and answer the question based on it" — a wall of text with one question at the end. Read the question first (the last line), then the clues: you then know which cell you are hunting and can stop when it fills.

5Five worked questions — at TALLENTEX pitch

Try each before opening the working and give yourself two minutes — the real paper's average is ninety seconds, and these three archetypes sit at the end of the section where you will have less. The elimination reasoning is written out because ruling two options out is worth almost as much as solving.

1The Q80 machine — three statements, an either/or pair · ~2 minutes

Take the statements to be true even if they seem to be at variance with commonly known facts, and decide which of the conclusions logically follow.

Statements: All kites are birds. Some birds are eagles. No eagle is a fish.

Conclusions:

  1. Some birds are not fish.
  2. Some kites are eagles.
  3. No kite is a fish.
  4. Some kites are fish.
1Only I follows
2Only I and II follow
3Only I and either III or IV follow
4Only either III or IV follows
Working

Nouns: kites, birds, eagles, fish — all four are in the statements. Nothing dies on rule 3.

Chain: "Some birds are eagles · No eagle is a fish" is Some + No → Some birds are not fish. Conclusion I follows. "All kites are birds · Some birds are eagles" is All + Some → nothing: the eagle-birds need not include a single kite. Conclusion II does not follow.

Kites and fish: kites are linked to fish only through the eagle lens, and the kites may sit inside that lens or outside it — nothing definite. III fails alone and IV fails alone.

Pair them: "No kite is a fish" and "Some kites are fish" are the Some/No pair on the same two groups. Either III or IV follows. Answer (3).

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) misses the pair. (2) accepts II — "some birds are eagles" read as if it said all. (4) throws away I, the one definite conclusion, because "some … are not" sounds weak. It is as definite as any "No".
22024-A Q79's shape — seven round, which statement is false · ~2½ minutes

Seven friends — Aditi, Bhanu, Chetan, Divya, Eshan, Falak and Gauri — sit around a circular table at equal distances, all facing the centre. Aditi sits where the figure shows.

  • Chetan sits second to the right of Aditi.
  • Eshan sits third to the left of Chetan.
  • Only one person sits between Eshan and Bhanu.
  • Divya sits to the immediate right of Bhanu.
  • Gauri is not an immediate neighbour of Aditi.

Which of the following statements is false?

1Gauri sits second to the left of Divya
2Falak sits to the immediate right of Aditi
3Bhanu sits third to the right of Falak
4Divya sits second to the right of Eshan
Working

Number the seats 1–7 clockwise from Aditi (1). Facing the centre, left = clockwise = up the numbers, right = anticlockwise = down.

Chetan second to the right of Aditi → two seats anticlockwise → seat 6. Eshan third to the left of Chetan → three seats clockwise from 6 → 7, 1, 2 → seat 2. One person between Eshan and Bhanu → Bhanu at seat 4 or seat 7. Divya immediately right of Bhanu → one seat anticlockwise: Bhanu 4 → Divya 3 ✓; Bhanu 7 → Divya 6, which is Chetan ✗. So Bhanu 4, Divya 3. Seats 5 and 7 remain for Falak and Gauri; Gauri is not next to Aditi and seat 7 is → Gauri 5, Falak 7.

Test every statement. (1) Second to the left of Divya (3) → seat 5 → Gauri — true. (2) Immediate right of Aditi (1) → seat 7 → Falak — true. (3) Third to the right of Falak (7) → seat 4 → Bhanu — true. (4) Second to the right of Eshan (2) → seat 7 → Falak, not Divya. Divya is first to the left of Eshan. False — answer (4).

What to notice. The false statement is a left/right swap plus a count-by-one — the usual recipe. Mirror the convention and the whole table mirrors: all four statements keep their truth values, so a student who uses the wrong convention consistently still gets (4). The last clue decides the branch: skip it and Falak and Gauri are interchangeable, which makes (1), (2) and (3) uncertain too — four candidates for "false" is the sign that a clue was left unused.
3Sample Q78's shape — five people, two attributes, which statements are correct · ~2½ minutes

Five friends — Ira, Jai, Kunal, Lata and Mohan — come from five different cities (Agra, Bhopal, Chennai, Dehradun, Ernakulam) and each plays a different instrument (flute, guitar, sitar, tabla, veena).

  • The one from Chennai plays the veena.
  • Jai is from Bhopal and does not play the flute.
  • Lata plays the sitar.
  • Lata is not from Agra.
  • Ira plays the tabla.
  • Mohan is from Dehradun.

Which of the following statements are correct?

  1. Kunal plays the veena.
  2. Ira is from Ernakulam.
  3. Jai plays the guitar.
  4. Mohan plays the flute.
1I, III and IV only
2I and III only
3III and IV only
4All four
Working

Ticks first: Jai–Bhopal, Mohan–Dehradun, Lata–sitar, Ira–tabla. Cross out their rows and columns (the figure in 3.1 shows this grid a moment later, once the "is not" and link crosses are in).

Link clue: the Chennai person plays the veena. Ira (tabla) and Lata (sitar) cannot be that person; Jai and Mohan already have cities → Kunal is from Chennai and plays the veena. I is correct.

Cities left: Agra and Ernakulam for Ira and Lata; Lata is not from Agra → Lata Ernakulam, Ira Agra. II is false.

Instruments left: flute and guitar for Jai and Mohan; Jai does not play the flute → Jai guitar, Mohan flute. III and IV are correct.

PersonCityInstrument
IraAgratabla
JaiBhopalguitar
KunalChennaiveena

Answer (1).

Where each wrong option comes from. (2) stops before the last instrument column closes — Jai's guitar is found, Mohan's flute (the one open cell left) is never ticked. (3) applies the link clue only as crosses (Lata is not from Chennai, Jai does not play the veena) and never closes the Chennai column, so Kunal's veena looks unforced: a deduction is not a written fact, and the student who only ticks what is written misses it. (4) accepts II — "Lata is not from Agra" was never applied, so Ira in Ernakulam looked as good as Ira in Agra, and the tired reader ticks the first fit.
42024-U Q80's trap, turned round — a real pair, a turned-round statement, a possibility · ~2 minutes

Take the statements to be true and decide which of the conclusions logically follow.

Statements: Some doctors are singers. Some singers are dancers.

Conclusions:

  1. Some doctors are dancers.
  2. No doctor is a dancer.
  3. Some dancers are singers.
  4. All singers being doctors is a possibility.
1Only III and IV follow
2Only either I or II follows
3Either I or II, and III and IV follow
4Only I, III and IV follow
Working

Nouns: doctors, singers, dancers — all present; nothing dies on rule 3.

Chain: Some + Some → nothing definite about doctors and dancers. So I fails alone and II fails alone — but they are the Some/No pair on the same two groups: either I or II follows.

III turns "Some singers are dancers" around — follows (rule 2).

IV: can every singer be a doctor? Draw singers wholly inside doctors: "some doctors are singers" still holds, "some singers are dancers" still holds. Nothing forbids it — the possibility follows.

Answer (3).

Where each wrong option comes from. (2) misses that III and IV are definite — the student who reads "Some + Some = nothing" as "nothing at all follows". (1) misses the pair: I and II each fail, so both are dropped, and the either/or is never considered. (4) accepts I outright — Some + Some read as a chain. That is the single most common syllogism error, and 2024-U Q80's option (2) was built for it.
52024-U Q79's shape — a row, one person at an end · ~2 minutes

Seven friends P, Q, R, S, T, U and V sit in a row of seven chairs, all facing north.

  • R sits at one of the ends of the row.
  • T sits third to the right of R.
  • S sits second to the right of T.
  • U sits to the immediate left of S.
  • Q sits to the immediate left of V.

Who sits third to the left of S?

1Q
2T
3U
4V
Working

Facing north, right = east = the page's right. Number the chairs 1–7 from the left.

R is at an end and T is three chairs to R's right — so R must be at the left end, chair 1: from chair 7 there is nothing to the right. T = chair 4. S second to the right of T → chair 6. U immediately left of S → chair 5. Chairs 2, 3 and 7 remain for P, Q and V; Q immediately left of V needs two adjacent chairs → Q 2, V 3, and P takes chair 7.

Row: R · Q · V · T · U · S · P. Third to the left of S (chair 6) is chair 3 → V. Answer (4).

Where each wrong option comes from. (3) U is first to the left of S and (2) T is second — the count-by-one slips. (1) Q is fourth. The end clue fixes the arrangement, and it is the stem's way of telling you which end without saying so: put R at the right end and there is no chair for T. 2024-U Q79 did exactly this. And if you had faced the row south by mistake, the row mirrors and the third to the left of S is still V.
