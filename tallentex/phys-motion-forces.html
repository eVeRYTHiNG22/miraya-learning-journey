Motion & Forces

Official topics: Motion and Force and Laws of Motion (both Class 9) — two topics, one card. Your IGCSE course already covers nearly all of the physics here — the kinematics, F = ma and momentum (0625 sections 1.2, 1.5 and 1.6) — so this is a short page. What is new is the NCERT framing Allen writes in — the three equations of motion as formulas, Newton's three laws by number, inertia, action–reaction pairs, uniform circular motion — and the word traps the real papers set. A 25-minute read, then the paper.

What that tells you:

  1. Two to four of the fifteen Physics questions a sitting (three to five counting the free-fall and orbit items) — and both 2024 papers opened with it, in exactly the same four slots: Q1 a graph, Q3 a concept (circular motion, or an impossible curve), Q8 a Newton's-law statement, Q10 a one-formula number. The older papers and the sample open with sound or waves instead, so treat the pattern as recent, not eternal.
  2. Graph judgement is the signature. "Which of these velocity–time curves can be realised" (2017-E Q2), "which is not possible" (2024-A Q3) and "which graph will this set of forces produce" (2024-U Q1) — three of the sixteen. The rules are in section 2 and they are short: a vertical segment is impossible, a curve that turns back in time is impossible, crossing the time axis is fine.
  3. The numerical items are one formula each — v² = u² + 2as (2024-U Q10), v = u + at (Sample Q3), Pythagoras (2024-A Q10), FΔt = Δp (Sample Q12), area under an F–t graph (2018-S Q8), area under an a–t graph (2024-A Q1). None of the arithmetic is hard. The marks go on the wording: "retardation" is a negative acceleration, "displacement" is not distance, and "net change in momentum" means the area below the axis counts against you.
  4. The Newton's-law items are word traps, not physics traps. "No force is acting" versus "the resultant force is zero" (2024-A Q8); "F₁ and F₂ cancel each other" when they act on different bodies (2024-U Q8); friction on a walking foot pointing forward (2018-S Q12); a released stone going straight, not curving on (2024-U Q3, 2017-E Q11). Sections 4–7 name every one of them.
  5. Allen recycles the machine, not the numbers. 2024-U Q10 and Sample Q3 are the same uniform-retardation item; 2024-U Q3 and 2024-A Q7 are one circular-motion idea in two dresses; 2017-E Q8 and 2024-U Q8 are both third-law pictures; 2017-E Q2 and 2024-A Q3 are the same impossible-graph question seven years apart.

Budget accordingly. This is an 8–16-mark unit (12–20 counting the free-fall and orbit items) — among the largest in Physics — and you have done all the physics before. Spend the time on the traps and on the NCERT wording, not on re-learning kinematics.

1The words NCERT uses — and the four that cost marks

Your IGCSE course says resultant force and deceleration; NCERT says unbalanced force and negative acceleration; Allen's stems say retardation. Same physics, three sets of labels — and some of the labels are traps in their own right.

Where NCERT starts — at rest compared with what?

  • Motion is a change of position with time — and position only means something measured from a reference point, which NCERT calls the origin. Its example: "the school is 2 km north of the railway station" makes the station the reference point.
  • Rest and motion are relative. To the passengers in a moving bus the roadside trees seem to move backwards and their fellow passengers seem at rest; to someone standing on the road the bus and everyone in it are moving. (Activities 7.1 and 7.2 are this one idea: are the walls of your classroom at rest? Why does your stationary train seem to move when the one beside it starts?)
  • NCERT's number line (Fig. 7.1). An object goes from O out to A, 60 km away, and comes back to C, which is 25 km from O. Distance = 60 + 35 = 95 km; displacement = 25 km. Stop at B, 35 km from O, instead: distance 85 km, displacement 35 km. Go all the way back to O: distance 120 km, displacement zero. And from O to A alone the two are equal, 60 km each.
  • Magnitude is NCERT's word for the numerical value of a quantity. Distance has only a magnitude; displacement has a magnitude and a direction.
  • A car's odometer measures the distance it has travelled — never the displacement (Activity 7.4: the odometer shows 1850 km from Bhubaneshwar to New Delhi; the straight-line displacement on the map is shorter).
  • NCERT's table for uniform motion (Table 7.1, Activity 7.5). Read every 15 minutes, object A has gone 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 70 m — equal distances in equal times, uniform. Object B has gone 12, 19, 23, 35, 37, 41, 44 m — unequal steps, non-uniform.
  • Motion you infer, and motion you cannot feel. We infer that air is moving from dust and swaying leaves. The earth moves — sunrise, sunset and the seasons come from that — yet we do not feel it directly. Most motions are complex: along a straight line, round a circle, rotating, vibrating, or a mix; NCERT starts with the straight line.
  • Activities 7.3 and 7.6. Walk from one corner of a basketball court to the opposite corner along the sides: the distance is two sides, the displacement is the diagonal. And at a walking speed of about 4 km/h, the time you take to reach the bus stop tells you how far away it is.
  • Lightning first, thunder later (Activity 7.7). Light arrives almost at once, while sound travels at about 346 m/s in air — so the gap between flash and thunder, in seconds, times 346 m/s gives the distance to the lightning.
  • Think and Act. Erratic, uncontrolled motion — a flooded river, a hurricane, a tsunami — endangers us; controlled motion serves us, as in generating hydro-electric power.

Definitions — NCERT's, plus IGCSE's words scalar and vector

  • Distance — the length of the path actually travelled. A scalar; it never decreases.
  • Displacement — the shortest straight line from the starting point to the finishing point, with its direction. A vector. It can be zero (one full lap of a track) and its magnitude is never more than the distance.
  • Speed = distance ÷ time (scalar). Velocity = displacement ÷ time (vector). Average speed = total distance ÷ total time; average velocity = total displacement ÷ total time — and for uniform acceleration, average velocity = (u + v)/2.
  • Uniform motion — equal distances in equal intervals of time, however small the intervals. Non-uniform otherwise — NCERT's examples are a car on a crowded street and a person jogging in a park. Its summary puts it through velocity: uniform if the velocity is constant, non-uniform if it is changing.
  • Acceleration = change in velocity ÷ time, in m/s²; uniform when the velocity changes by equal amounts in equal intervals. A negative acceleration is what Allen's stems call retardation (IGCSE: deceleration).
  • Velocity can change through its speed, its direction, or both. NCERT's sign rule: acceleration is positive when it is in the direction of the velocity and negative when it is opposite to it. Its standard example of uniform acceleration is a freely falling body; its example of non-uniform acceleration is a car whose speed rises by unequal amounts in equal intervals. Activity 7.8 asks for everyday examples of acceleration along the motion, against it, uniform and non-uniform.
  • Units of speed: the SI unit is m s⁻¹; NCERT also lists cm s⁻¹ and km h⁻¹. Speed and velocity share the same units. A car that covers 100 km in 2 h has an average speed of 50 km/h — it need not have done 50 km/h all the time. (NCERT's Fig. 7.2 pairs a bowling speed of 143 km/h with a road signboard — numbers like these are speeds, not velocities: no direction is given.)

The four wording traps

  1. Distance ≠ displacement. 2024-A Q10: 16 cm east then 63 cm south, and 79 cm — the distance — sits there as option (2). The displacement is √(16² + 63²) = 65 cm.
  2. Retardation is quoted as a magnitude in words and as a negative number in the options. 2024-U Q10 lists all four options with a minus sign: a = (0² − 75²)/(2 × 1000) = −5625/2000 = −2.8125 m/s². Do not "correct" the sign away.
  3. km/h is not m/s. Multiply by 5/18. NCERT's favourite four: 36 km/h = 10 m/s, 54 km/h = 15 m/s, 72 km/h = 20 m/s, 90 km/h = 25 m/s. Expect an option built on the unconverted number.
  4. "Speed" and "velocity" are different words on purpose. A body on a circle at steady speed has a changing velocity (section 4); a body that returns to its start has an average velocity of zero however fast it went.

The tables NCERT draws its graphs from

  • Speed from a distance–time graph: pick two points on the line, v = (s₂ − s₁)/(t₂ − t₁) — the slope. A straight line means the distance is directly proportional to the time: uniform speed.
  • Table 7.2 — a car speeding up. At 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12 s it has gone 0, 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36 m. The steps keep growing, so the distance–time graph is a curve (Fig. 7.4): non-uniform speed.
  • Fig. 7.5 — uniform velocity. A car at a steady 40 km/h gives a velocity–time line parallel to the time axis; the distance covered between t₁ and t₂ is the area of the rectangle under it, 40 × (t₂ − t₁) km.
  • Table 7.3 — uniform acceleration. A car's speedometer read every 5 s shows 0, 9, 18, 27, 36, 45, 54 km/h (0, 2.5, 5.0, 7.5, 10.0, 12.5, 15.0 m/s): equal gains in equal times, so the velocity–time graph is a straight sloping line (Fig. 7.6). The distance is the area under it — NCERT splits it into a rectangle plus a triangle.
  • Fig. 7.7 shows two more velocity–time shapes: (a) a line falling from 20 m/s to zero in 6 s — velocity decreasing with time; (b) a zig-zag that rises, falls, rises and falls again — velocity changing non-uniformly. Both are possible motions.
  • Activity 7.9 — a train. Station A at 0 km (arrives 08:00, departs 08:15), B at 120 km (arrives 11:15, departs 11:30), C at 180 km (arrives 13:00, departs 13:15). On a distance–time graph that is a 15-minute flat step at each station — A, B and C — joined by sloping lines, and both slopes are 40 km/h (120 km in 3 h; 60 km in 1½ h).
  • Activity 7.10 — Feroz and Sania cycle the same 3.6 km to school. Feroz is at 1.0, 1.9, 2.8, 3.6 km after 5, 10, 15, 20 minutes; Sania at 0.8, 1.6, 2.3, 3.0, 3.6 km after 5, 10, 15, 20, 25 minutes. Feroz's line is the steeper one — he is faster and arrives 5 minutes earlier; neither line is perfectly straight, so neither motion is exactly uniform.

A constant force gives a straight v–t line. 2024-U Q1 drew five forces on a mass on a frictionless floor: 5 N right against 3 N + 2 N left (net zero), 7 N up the page against 4 N down (net 3 N). A net force that does not change gives an acceleration that does not change, and a constant acceleration is a straight sloping line on a v–t graph — the key was the straight line, and the three curves were there for anyone who thinks "several forces" means "complicated motion".

The two shapes that cannot exist beyond NCERT

A vertical segment would mean the velocity changed with no time passing — an infinite acceleration, needing an infinite force. A curve that turns back in time would give the body two velocities at one instant, and time does not run backwards. A closed loop (2024-A Q3's circle) breaks both rules at once. The same two rules hold for a distance–time or displacement–time graph: a vertical segment would mean an infinite speed, and the graph cannot turn back in time. One more: a distance–time graph can never slope downwards, because distance never decreases; a displacement–time graph can. On a velocity–time graph everything else is allowed: crossing the time axis just means the body reversed (a ball thrown up); a sharp corner is a sudden change of acceleration, which a force can produce instantly (a brake pressed); a horizontal line below the axis is a steady backward speed.

Read "nearly vertical" as vertical

2017-E Q2's panel (iii) is a smooth hump whose drop is drawn almost vertical — steep enough that the printed key treats it as a jump and rules the panel out, keeping only (ii) and (iv). Allen's figures are hand-drawn: a segment that looks vertical is meant to be vertical. If you find yourself arguing "but it slopes a little", you are answering a question the setter did not ask.

The a–t graph — the 2024-A opener

Area under an acceleration–time graph is the change in velocity. 2024-A Q1 shaded a 5 × 5 square minus a quarter circle on an a–t graph of a stopping car: Δv = 25(1 − π/4) m/s over 5 s. The key, 250(1 − π/4) N, is m × Δv ÷ 5 s — the average force on the 50 kg driver over the stop. The stem never said "average" and its hint stopped at the area, so m × area = 1250(1 − π/4), which is not an option, was the dead end. Clean version of the same machine: an a–t graph that is a straight line from −2 m/s² at t = 0 to 0 at t = 8 s has area ½ × 8 × (−2) = −8 m/s; a train doing 20 m/s at t = 0 is doing 12 m/s at t = 8 s, and if its mass is 2 × 10⁵ kg the average braking force was 2 × 10⁵ × 8 / 8 = 2 × 10⁵ N.

3The three equations of motion — and the three shortcuts Allen expects

IGCSE let you do all of this from graphs. NCERT names the three equations and Allen expects you to pick the one that skips the quantity you are not given.

v = u + at
No distance in it. Sample Q3: 100 − 5 × 7 = 65 m/s.
s = ut + ½at²
No final velocity in it. From rest: s = ½at².
v² = u² + 2as
No time in it. 2024-U Q10: 0 = 75² + 2a(1000) → a = −2.8125 m/s².
u, v, a, s, t
Take the direction of u as positive. Retardation → a negative. "Comes to rest" → v = 0. "Starts from rest" → u = 0.

What NCERT calls them — worth knowing for a match-the-following. v = u + at is the velocity–time relation; s = ut + ½at² is the position–time relation; and the third, which NCERT prints as 2as = v² − u², is the position–velocity relation — it is what you get by eliminating t from the first two. All three hold only for uniform acceleration in a straight line; s is the distance covered in time t. NCERT says they can be derived graphically but, in this print, does not derive them.

Three shortcuts

  1. Average velocity for uniform acceleration is (u + v)/2, so s = (u + v)t/2. "Stops in 600 m from 30 m/s — how long?" is 600 ÷ 15 = 40 s in one line, no acceleration needed.
  2. Stopping (v = 0): distance s = u²/2a, time t = u/a. Double the speed → four times the stopping distance.
  3. Free fall from rest (u = 0, a = g): v = gt, h = ½gt², v² = 2gh, so t = √(2h/g). Time goes as √h: from 2h the fall takes √2 times as long (2018-S Q2, key 1 : √2), and, with no air resistance, it is the same for every mass — the 20 kg iron sphere and the 12 kg aluminium one share their acceleration and their velocity at every height (Sample Q11). Thrown straight up, a body takes as long to come down as to go up, and comes back at the speed it was thrown — the motion is symmetric.

Beyond NCERT, inside Allen's scope

Distance covered in the nth second: sn = u + a(2n − 1)/2 — the difference between the whole-journey distances at n and at n − 1. Not in the Class 9 text; Allen's Physics subject paper on the hub uses it. If you forget it, work out ut + ½at² at n and at n − 1 seconds and subtract.

g: use the value the question gives (Sample Q13 gives 9.8); when none is given, 10 m/s² — and check that the options come out clean with it.

4Uniform circular motion — steady speed, changing velocity

Two of the twenty items (2024-U Q3, 2024-A Q7) plus the switched-off satellite (2017-E Q11) live here. The physics is one sentence; the traps are in the vocabulary.

NCERT wording

When a body moves in a circular path with uniform speed, its motion is called uniform circular motion. The direction of its velocity changes continuously, so the velocity changes, so it is an accelerated motion even though the speed is constant. Speed on a circle of radius r, one revolution in time t: v = 2πr / t.

How NCERT gets there. An athlete runs at steady speed round a rectangular track: he changes direction 4 times a lap. Make the track a hexagon: 6 times. A regular octagon: 8 times. Keep adding sides and each side shrinks towards a point while the track approaches a circle — on which the direction changes at every point. Steady speed, endlessly changing direction: that is why circular motion is accelerated.

NCERT's examples of uniform circular motion: the motion of the moon and of the earth, a satellite in a circular orbit, a cyclist on a circular track at constant speed. Its Activity 7.11 is the stone on a thread: let go, and it leaves along the tangent — which is exactly how an athlete throws a hammer or a discus.

  • The force that keeps a body on the circle acts towards the centre — centripetal, "centre-seeking" (2024-A Q7's X). The velocity is along the tangent (its Y). A cornering car, a stone on a string, the Moon round the Earth: in each, the net force points to the centre. NCERT names the centripetal force in its Gravitation chapter, not here beyond NCERT.
  • Released, the body goes straight along the tangent at the speed it had — that is the first law (section 5), and it is why a hammer-thrower lets go at the right instant and why sparks fly off a grinding wheel in straight lines.
  • beyond NCERT "Centrifugal force" is not a force on the body. The outward throw you feel on a turn is your inertia carrying you straight while the vehicle turns underneath you. 2024-A Q7 offered it in two of its four options as bait.

Assertion–Reason: judge each sentence on its own

2024-U Q3 — Assertion: a stone whirled on a thread moves in a straight line when released (true). Reason: "along the circular path the direction of motion remains the same at every point" (false — it changes at every point; that is the whole point of the chapter). So option (3), A true, R false. Students who agree with the assertion and stop reading tick (1).

5Force, balanced and unbalanced — inertia and the first law

NCERT's chapter opens with balanced versus unbalanced forces, then states the first law and names inertia. Allen's Q8-type items test whether you can tell "no force" from "no net force".

NCERT wording

  • A force is a push or a pull. It can change the speed of a body, its direction of motion, or its shape and size. SI unit: the newton (N).
  • Balanced forces add to zero: they do not change the state of rest or of motion (they may change the shape — the two sides of a stretched spring). An unbalanced force (net force, resultant force) produces an acceleration in its own direction.
  • First law of motion: an object remains in a state of rest, or of uniform motion in a straight line, unless compelled to change that state by an applied force — an unbalanced one, as the paragraph before it in NCERT makes clear.
  • Inertia is the natural tendency of an object to resist a change in its state of rest or of uniform motion. Mass is the measure of inertia: the heavier the body, the more force it takes to change its motion.

NCERT's three pictures of balanced and unbalanced forces

  • A wooden block with a string on each side (Fig. 8.3). Pull one string and it moves that way; pull both equally and it stays put — balanced forces. Pull one harder and it moves towards the greater force — an unbalanced force.
  • Children pushing a box on a rough floor (Fig. 8.4). A small push: the box does not move, because friction acts opposite to the push and balances it. A harder push: still balanced. Harder still: the push becomes bigger than the friction, the forces are unbalanced, and the box moves. Friction arises between two surfaces in contact.
  • A bicycle slows down when you stop pedalling — friction again. It looks as if an object needs a force to keep moving; NCERT calls that "quite incorrect". With balanced forces an object moves at uniform velocity; an unbalanced force changes its speed or direction for as long as it acts; remove it and the object keeps the velocity it has reached.

NCERT's opening remark is a good statement-question line: no one has seen, tasted or felt a force — we only ever see or feel its effect (a spring stretching, a rubber ball squashed oblong, a trolley set moving).

Galileo's marble — where the first law came from

For centuries people believed that rest is the "natural state" of an object — a ball given a small hit does not roll for ever. Galileo and Newton showed otherwise.

A marble rolling down an inclined plane speeds up; rolling up one it slows down. Galileo put two frictionless planes face to face: released on one, the marble climbs the other to the same height. Make the second plane less steep and the marble has to travel further to reach that height. Make it horizontal and the marble would travel for ever, trying to reach a height it can never regain. So no net force is needed to keep something moving at constant speed — only to change its motion. In practice friction stops the marble; a smooth marble, a smooth plane and a lubricant reduce it.

Newton built his three laws on this. NCERT's name for the first: the law of inertia.

Galileo Galilei (1564–1642), born in Pisa on 15 February 1564. His father, Vincenzo, wanted him to be a doctor; he enrolled for a medical degree at Pisa in 1581 but never finished it, drawn to mathematics instead. His first book, The Little Balance (1586), described Archimedes' way of finding relative densities with a balance. In De Motu (1589) he wrote on falling bodies, slowing them down with inclined planes, and in 1592 he became professor of mathematics at Padua. From inclined planes and the pendulum he found that for a uniformly accelerated body the distance is proportional to the square of the time. He built telescopes much better than others of his day and designed the first pendulum clock (around 1640). In Starry Messenger he reported mountains on the Moon, the Milky Way as tiny stars and four small bodies orbiting Jupiter; he wrote on floating bodies and on sunspots; and from Saturn and Venus he argued that all the planets orbit the Sun, not the earth.

NCERT's four inertia demonstrations. A sharp horizontal hit on the bottom coin of a pile of carom coins knocks only that coin out; the rest drop straight down (Activity 8.1). A five-rupee coin on a card over a tumbler falls into the glass when the card is flicked away (Activity 8.2) — and a one-rupee coin needs a gentler flick, because it has less mass and so less inertia. Water in a tumbler on a tray spills when you turn round quickly (Activity 8.3). And the groove in a saucer is there so the cup does not topple on a sudden jerk. NCERT's pairs for comparing inertia: an empty box and a box full of books, a football and a stone of the same size, a cart and a train — the more massive one is always harder to start or stop. And NCERT's point about safety belts: when a car brakes suddenly, they exert a force on your body that slows its forward motion — instead of the panel in front.

"No force" is never the answer for a body at constant velocity

2024-A Q8: a train moving east at a steady 20 m/s. Forces act on it all the time — its weight, the track pushing up, the engine pulling, friction and air dragging back — and they add to zero. The key is (4) the resultant force is zero; "(1) no force is acting" is the bait, and so are the two options that put a net force east or west. Same idea in 2017-E Q12's region C: a constant speed means the water's upward forces — drag and upthrust together — exactly equal the weight, not that the forces have gone.

6The second law — momentum, F = ma, impulse

NCERT states the second law through momentum, then derives F = ma from it. Allen asks it both ways, and draws the F–t graph that IGCSE never drew.

NCERT wording

  • Momentum p = mv — mass × velocity; a vector along the velocity; SI unit kg m/s.
  • Second law of motion: the rate of change of momentum of a body is proportional to the applied unbalanced force, and takes place in the direction of the force. F = (mv − mu)/t = ma. One newton is the force that gives 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m/s²: 1 N = 1 kg m/s².
  • Impulse (Allen's word — NCERT Class 9 writes the equation without naming it): F × t = mv − mu = Δp. Unit N s, which is the same thing as kg m/s. Sample Q12: 980 N × 0.1 s = 98 N s.

How NCERT builds the second law

  • Why mass and velocity go together. A table-tennis ball that hits a player does not hurt; a fast cricket ball does. A parked truck is harmless; a truck moving at only 5 m/s can kill. A bullet has a tiny mass and a huge velocity. The "impact" depends on mass and velocity together — the product is the momentum, a quantity Newton introduced.
  • Why time matters. A car with a dead battery needs a speed of 1 m/s to start. One sudden shove hardly moves it; a steady push kept up for some time gets it there. So the change of momentum depends on the force and on how long it acts.
  • From the law to F = ma. The rate of change of momentum is m(v − u)/t = ma, so the law says F ∝ ma, that is F = kma. The unit of force is then chosen so that k = 1: one unit of force is the force that gives a mass of 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m s⁻². That unit is the newton, 1 N = 1 kg m s⁻², and the law becomes F = ma.
  • The first law is inside the second. Write it as Ft = mv − mu. If F = 0 then v = u for any time t: a moving object keeps its velocity, and one at rest (u = 0) stays at rest.

The same Δp, spread over a longer time, needs a smaller force. That one sentence explains the cricketer drawing his hands back while catching, the high-jump pit's padding, a car's crumple zone, the bent knees of a landing gymnast. Turned round — a karate chop, a hammer — a short time means a large force.

Conservation of momentum

If no external unbalanced force acts on a system, its total momentum does not change: m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂. Starting from rest, the two parts move off with equal and opposite momenta — a gun and its bullet (mgunvgun = mbulletvbullet), a rocket and its exhaust, and the balloon car of 2017-E Q8. It is also the whole answer to Sample Q5: a child stuck on a frictionless floor cannot push on the floor sideways, so running, rolling and jumping do nothing — but throwing mass away (spitting, coughing, tossing a shoe) sends the child the other way.

Scope note

beyond NCERT The rationalised NCERT print has no conservation-of-momentum section — yet its own Exercise 14 (an object that collides with a block and sticks to it) cannot be done without the idea. Allen has not dropped it either: the sample paper's Q5 is a conservation item, and the Physics subject paper on the hub asks a recoil sum. Learn it as part of the third law (next section), which is where NCERT used to derive it from.

Δp carries direction

A ball of mass m hitting a wall at speed v and bouncing straight back at v has Δp = (−mv) − (+mv) = −2mv — magnitude 2mv, not zero, not mv. Take the incoming direction as positive, give the return velocity a minus sign, then subtract final minus initial. The forgotten sign turns 2mv into 0 and is the single most common momentum error on this page.

7The third law — pairs act on different bodies

Four of the sixteen items are third-law pictures or statements. Every one of them turns on the same clause: the two forces of a pair act on two different objects.

NCERT wording

Third law of motion: to every action there is an equal and opposite reaction — and the two act on two different objects. They are equal in magnitude, opposite in direction, simultaneous, and beyond NCERT of the same kind (both contact forces, or both gravitational — the law holds for non-contact forces too, 2018-S Q12).

NCERT's pictures of the third law

  • Two spring balances hooked together (Fig. 8.10), one fixed to a wall. Pull the free one: both show the same reading. The force of A on B equals the force of B on A, in the opposite direction. Either may be called the action and the other the reaction.
  • The two forces arise together. NCERT's wording is that the second object instantaneously exerts a force back — there is no delay, and neither comes "first".
  • Equal forces, unequal accelerations. A gun pushes the bullet forward and the bullet pushes the gun back with the same force — but the gun has far more mass, so its acceleration (the recoil) is far smaller. Equal forces do not mean equal effects.
  • The sailor and the boat (Fig. 8.12): as the sailor jumps forward out of a rowing boat, the boat moves backwards.
  • Two footballers colliding both feel hurt — each exerts a force on the other.
  • Activity 8.4: two children on separate carts throw a heavy sand bag to each other. The thrower's cart rolls backwards with every throw. Put two children on one cart and the same throw gives that cart a smaller acceleration — the second law showing through.
  • Find the pair by swapping the nouns. "Hands push wall" ↔ "wall pushes hands". "Earth pulls book down" ↔ "book pulls Earth up". If the swap gives the same object, it is not a pair.
  • Weight and the table's push on a resting book are not a pair. Both act on the book; they are equal because the book is not accelerating (first law), not because of the third law. The partner of the table's push is the book's push on the table.
  • 2024-U Q8: Rohan pushes a wall; F₁ is the force on the wall, F₂ the force on Rohan. Key (4): equal and opposite. Option (1), "F₁ and F₂ cancel each other", is the bait — forces on different bodies cannot cancel; the wall does not move because the ground holds it.
  • Walking: the foot pushes the ground backward; the ground's friction pushes the foot forward. So friction on a walker's feet points the way they are walking — 2018-S Q12's "walking south, friction acts north" is the incorrect statement. Rowing (oar pushes water back), swimming, a rocket, a recoiling gun: the same sentence.

The rest of 2018-S Q12, plus one more friction fact

Its other two true statements: the normal force always acts perpendicular to the surfaces in contact, and friction is a dissipative force — it turns kinetic energy into heat. (Its third, that the third law holds for non-contact forces, is in the box above.) One more that Allen asks: static friction adjusts — it matches your push up to a limit, and the object moves only once the push exceeds it, so "friction always equals the applied force" is false: it does while the body stays at rest or slides at constant velocity, but not while it accelerates. NCERT's summary says "force of friction always opposes motion of objects"; more exactly, friction opposes the slipping of the two surfaces against each other, or their tendency to slip — which is why, on a walking foot (which would slip backwards), it points forward.

9Five worked questions — at TALLENTEX pitch

Try each before opening the working and give yourself 90 seconds — the real paper's average. The elimination reasoning is written out because on the day, ruling two options out is worth almost as much as solving.

12024-U Q1 archetype, carried one step on · ~90 seconds

A block of mass 2 kg lies on a frictionless floor and is pulled by the five horizontal forces shown, viewed from above. It starts from rest. After 3 s its speed and the distance it has moved are

16 m/s and 9 m
26 m/s and 18 m
312 m/s and 18 m
412 m/s and 36 m
Working

Step 1 — net force, one axis at a time. Along x: 5 − 3 − 2 = 0. Along y: 10 − 6 = 4 N, up the page. A constant 4 N, so the motion is uniformly accelerated along +y.

Step 2 — a = F/m = 4/2 = 2 m/s².

Step 3 — v = u + at = 0 + 2 × 3 = 6 m/s; s = ut + ½at² = ½ × 2 × 3² = 9 m. Option (1).

Where each wrong option comes from. (2) 6 m/s, 18 m — s = at², the ½ dropped. (3) 12 m/s, 18 m — the 4 N net force used as the acceleration; the mass was never divided in, then s = ½ × 4 × 9 = 18. (4) 12 m/s, 36 m — both slips at once. Elimination. 4 N on 2 kg cannot give 4 m/s², so (3) and (4) go before any arithmetic; between (1) and (2) it is only whether you remembered the ½.
2The 2024-U Q10 / Sample Q3 machine, with the km/h step · ~90 seconds

An auto-rickshaw travelling at 54 km/h on the Outer Ring Road brakes with a uniform retardation of 2.5 m/s². The distance it travels before stopping, and the time it takes, are

145 m and 3 s
245 m and 6 s
390 m and 6 s
4583 m and 21.6 s
Working

Step 1 — units. 54 km/h × 5/18 = 15 m/s.

Step 2 — distance, no time given so v² = u² + 2as with v = 0 and a = −2.5: 0 = 225 − 5s → s = 45 m.

Step 3 — time, v = u + at: 0 = 15 − 2.5t → t = 6 s. Or the shortcut: average velocity while braking is 7.5 m/s, and 45 ÷ 7.5 = 6 s. Option (2).

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) 45 m, 3 s — t = s/u = 45/15: treats the braking auto as if it kept 15 m/s throughout; while braking its average is half that. (3) 90 m, 6 s — the 2 in 2as forgotten, s = u²/a. (4) 583 m, 21.6 s — 54 used as if it were m/s: 54²/5 = 583.2 and 54/2.5 = 21.6. The tell: an auto does not need half a kilometre to stop. Elimination. Any option built on 54 as a speed in m/s is out at sight; then s = u²/2a decides 45 against 90, and only (1) and (2) remain.
3Multi-statement, five clauses · 2024-U Q8, 2024-A Q8 and 2018-S Q12 in one item · ~2 minutes

Consider the following statements.

  1. For a book lying at rest on a table, the weight of the book and the upward force of the table on the book form an action–reaction pair.
  2. When a stationary bus starts suddenly, the passengers fall backward because of the inertia of rest.
  3. A train moving along a straight track at a constant 20 m/s has no force acting on it.
  4. The action and reaction of Newton's third law always act on two different bodies.
  5. A fielder draws his hands back while catching a fast ball so that the ball's momentum is brought to zero over a longer time and the force on his hands is smaller.

Which of the statements are correct?

1I, II, IV and V only
2II, III, IV and V only
3II, IV and V only
4I, IV and V only
Working

I — false. Both forces act on the book; a third-law pair never acts on one body. The partner of the Earth's pull on the book is the book's pull on the Earth; the partner of the table's push on the book is the book's push on the table.

II — true. The lower body moves off with the bus; the upper body's inertia of rest keeps it where it was, so the passenger tips backward.

III — false. Weight, the track's push, the engine's pull, friction — plenty of forces act; they add to zero.

IV — true. By definition.

V — true. F = Δp/Δt: same Δp, longer Δt, smaller F.

So II, IV and V — option (3).

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) accepts I — the commonest third-law error in the chapter: equal-and-opposite is necessary for a pair but not sufficient; the two must be on different bodies. (2) accepts III — the "no force" trap of 2024-A Q8. (4) accepts I and rejects II — two slips at once: the pair error again, plus muddling inertia of rest (lurch backward at a start) with inertia of motion (lurch forward at a stop). Elimination. I and III are each a named trap on this page; any option containing either goes. That leaves (3) on its own — you never need to judge V.

The graph shows the force acting on a 4 kg trolley along a straight track. At t = 0 the trolley is already moving at 3 m/s in the direction in which the positive force acts. Its velocity at t = 8 s is

15 m/s
28 m/s
310 m/s

Working

5Assertion–Reason · the 2024-U Q3 / 2024-A Q7 idea · ~60 seconds

Assertion (A): When a car takes a sharp left turn at speed, the passengers are pushed to the right by an outward (centrifugal) force acting on them.
Reason (R): A body continues in its state of rest or of uniform motion in a straight line unless an unbalanced external force acts on it.

1Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A
2Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A
3A is true but R is false
4A is false but R is true
Working

R is the first law of motion, in substance — true.

A — no outward force acts on the passenger. The passenger's body keeps moving straight (inertia of direction) while the seat and the door turn left underneath it, so the door ends up pressing the passenger leftward. The feeling of being thrown right is the absence of a force, not the presence of one. A is false. Option (4).

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) is the everyday belief, and it is why 2024-A Q7 offered "centrifugal force" in two of its four options. A stone on a string, an orbiting Moon and a cornering car each have a net force pointing to the centre. (2) and (3) need A to be true. Elimination. R is the first law, so (3) is gone at once; the whole question is whether "a centrifugal force acting on them" is physics. It is not.
