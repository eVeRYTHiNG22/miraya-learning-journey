Linear Equations, Coordinates & Statistics

Official topics: Pair of Linear Equations in Two Variables (Class 10), Coordinate Geometry (Class 10) and Statistics (Class 9) — three topics, one card. Your IGCSE course already solves simultaneous equations, finds lengths and midpoints, and works means and histograms, so most of this page is NCERT's framing plus the five ways Allen dresses each topic up. New to you: the ratio test for how many solutions a pair has, the section formula (a point dividing a segment in a given ratio) and the centroid, and the unequal-width histogram. Every part of the three NCERT chapters is here too — the box under the paper table says where. About a 45-minute read, then the paper.

What that tells you:

  1. Two or three of the fifteen Maths questions a sitting — 2017-E two, 2018-S two, 2024-U three, 2024-A three, the sample two — so 8–12 marks. Both 2024 codes put the Statistics item at Q50, the coordinate-geometry item at Q54 and the linear-equations item at Q57 — three fixed slots in the recent papers.
  2. Only one of the twelve carries a figure — 2024-U Q54's rectangle on axes, where the figure is the data (four labelled vertices). No pair of lines is ever drawn for you, no histogram, no frequency polygon. The graphical half of all three NCERT chapters was never asked in five papers; the arithmetic half was asked every time.
  3. Statistics is never a table. No grouped data, no median or mode read off a list, no histogram — all five items are mean or range arithmetic wrapped in a puzzle: a sum that must divide exactly, a replaced student, the averages of overlapping subsets, an ordering clause that fixes which end x is on. The rationalised Class 9 print dropped mean, median and mode altogether; Allen asks them anyway, so section 4 carries them.
  4. "How many solutions" beats "what is the solution". Only one of the five linear-equation items is a plain solve (Sample Q46), and even that is the add-and-subtract trick rather than elimination. The others are a word problem, a consistency test with three unknowns, a system whose answer is a formula, and a check of four candidate plans against two constraints. Section 2 has all five machines.
  5. Coordinate geometry appeared twice in five papers, both times in 2024 — a rectangle on axes whose unknown vertices fall out of "opposite sides are equal and parallel" (code U), and a centroid from three midpoints (code A), which the rationalised NCERT chapter never states. Know the four formulas cold (distance, section, midpoint, centroid) and expect one item a sitting; the hub's Maths subject paper carries three.
  6. Three of the twelve are fastest by testing the options — 2024-U Q57 (substitute each formula pair), 2024-A Q57 (check each plan against the hours) and 2017-E Q58 (which option makes the division exact). When the options are candidates, verifying beats solving.

Budget accordingly. An 8–12-mark unit whose items run 45–120 s each once the machine is recognised. The marks are lost on the translation — a sentence into an equation, an average into a sum, "descending" into "x is the largest" — not on the arithmetic that follows.

1Pair of linear equations — one line each, three ways two lines can meet

NCERT writes every equation as a₁x + b₁y + c₁ = 0 and a₂x + b₂y + c₂ = 0. Each is a straight line; a solution is a point on both. So the whole chapter is one picture: two lines cross once, never, or lie on top of each other.

The ratio test — NCERT's table, one line

Compare the coefficients of the two equations, both written in the same form:

RatiosLinesSolutionsNCERT's word
a₁/a₂ ≠ b₁/b₂intersectexactly oneconsistent
a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ ≠ c₁/c₂parallelnoneinconsistent
a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ = c₁/c₂coincidentinfinitely manydependent (consistent)

Try it: 2x + 3y = 9 against 4x + 6y = 18 — ratios ½, ½, ½ → coincident. Against 4x + 6y = 20 — ½, ½, 9/20 → parallel, no solution. Against 4x − 6y = 20 — ½ ≠ −½ → one solution, and only then is it worth solving.

Fractions can hide a coincident pair. NCERT's Example 2 sets 5x − 8y + 1 = 0 against 3x − (24/5)y + 3/5 = 0: multiply the second by 5/3 and it is the first.

Trap — the c-ratio only means something in one form

NCERT's c is the constant in ax + by + c = 0, so 2x + 3y = 9 has c = −9. If you keep both equations as "= constant" instead, the test still works — what breaks it is comparing one equation's "+ c = 0" constant with the other's "= c" constant. Pick one form for both, then compare. The a and b ratios do not care which form you use.

The parameter question — "for which k are there infinitely many / no solutions?" cut from NCERT

Set up the ratio chain and solve it in two halves. For infinitely many solutions of x + (k + 1)y = 5 and (k + 1)x + 9y = 8k − 1:

  • a = b half: 1/(k + 1) = (k + 1)/9 → (k + 1)² = 9 → k + 1 = ±3 → k = 2 or k = −4.
  • c half: the constants' ratio must match the (now equal) coefficient ratio too. k = 2: a-ratio 1/3 and c-ratio 5/15 = 1/3 ✓. k = −4: 1/(−3) but 5/(−33) ✗. So k = 2 only.

The wrong option Allen always plants is "both values" — the answer to the first half alone. For no solution you want the first half to hold and the second to fail, so k = −4 would be the no-solution answer here. Read which of the two the stem asks.

Solving — NCERT keeps two algebraic methods; both are the ones you already use

Substitution (express one unknown from the easier equation, put it in the other) and elimination (multiply one or both equations so a coefficient matches, then add or subtract). With fractions or decimals, multiply through first. The answer need not be a whole number: NCERT's first pair solved by substitution (Example 4), 7x − 15y = 2 with x + 2y = 3, gives x = 49/29, y = 19/29.

When the variable disappears. If substituting or eliminating leaves a statement with no variable in it, read it. A true one (18 = 18) means the two equations are the same line — infinitely many solutions. A false one (0 = 9, or −4 = 0) means parallel lines — no solution. NCERT's two rails, x + 2y − 4 = 0 and 2x + 4y − 12 = 0, never cross for exactly this reason.

The cross-multiplication method and "equations reducible to a pair of linear equations" were removed from the chapter in 2022 and are not asked in the five papers. Under the clock, elimination on whichever unknown has the smaller lowest common multiple, and always substitute the answer back into the equation you did not use for the last step.

Solving by graph — NCERT's other method

For each equation find two solutions (the intercepts are easiest: put x = 0, then y = 0), plot them and draw the line. Where the lines cross is the solution; parallel lines mean no solution; one line on top of the other means infinitely many. NCERT's Example 1: x + 3y = 6 passes through (0, 2) and (6, 0), and 2x − 3y = 12 through (0, −4) and (3, −2) — and through (6, 0) as well. So x = 6, y = 0. The weakness of a graph is a solution that is not a whole number, such as (−1.75, 3.3): it cannot be read off reliably, which is why the algebra exists.

Writing a partner to order (Exercise 3.1 Q6). Given 2x + 3y − 8 = 0: keep the 2 and the 3 and change the constant for a parallel line (2x + 3y − 12 = 0); multiply the whole equation for the same line (4x + 6y − 16 = 0); change the ratio of the two coefficients for a line that crosses it (3x + 2y − 7 = 0).

2The five linear-equation machines the real papers use

Five items in five papers, five different machines. Recognise which one you are in before you write a line — three of them are not "solve the pair".

A · Swapped coefficients → add and subtract (Sample Q46) beyond NCERT

When the x-coefficient of one equation is the y-coefficient of the other, adding the two gives (a + b)(x + y) and subtracting gives (a − b)(x − y). The pair itself is NCERT's — Exercise 3.1 Q1(ii), 5 pencils and 7 pens for ₹50 against 7 pencils and 5 pens for ₹46, is one (₹3 and ₹5); the add-and-subtract trick is not. Fresh numbers: 73x + 29y = 175 and 29x + 73y = 131.

  • Add: 102(x + y) = 306 → x + y = 3.
  • Subtract: 44(x − y) = 44 → x − y = 1.
  • So x = 2, y = 1 — but Allen usually stops at x − y or x + y and offers "cannot be determined" as bait. It always can: two independent equations in two unknowns determine both.

B · A story → two equations (2017-E Q47)

Name the two unknowns, translate each sentence into one equation, solve, then answer the question that was actually asked. The 2017 item: tables t, chairs c. "Two chairs at each table leaves one chair over" → c = 2t + 1. "Three at each table leaves one table with no chairs" → the chairs fill t − 1 tables: c = 3(t − 1). So 2t + 1 = 3t − 3 → t = 4, c = 9, and the question wanted the sum: 13. Neither 4 nor 9 was an option — the wrong options (12, 7, 17) all imitate a sum, so finding t and stopping earns nothing.

C · "How many solutions?" with three unknowns (2018-S Q47) beyond NCERT

The 2018 item gave three equations in h, w and d and asked for the number of solutions. Do not solve — scale each equation to look like the first: 2h + 4w + 6d = 170 is exactly 2 × (h + 2w + 3d = 85), so it says nothing new; 5h + 10w + 15d = 450 divided by 5 is h + 2w + 3d = 90, which contradicts the 85. Same left-hand side, different right-hand side ⇒ no solution, whatever the number of unknowns. Had it been 425 instead of 450, all three would say the same thing — one equation, three unknowns, infinitely many. This is the ratio test in a bigger coat.

D · Coefficients that are letters → verify the options (2024-U Q57) cut from NCERT

ax + by = a + b and bx + ay = ab(a + b)/(a² − ab + b²), options given as formulas. Two tells: the denominator a² − ab + b² is the partner of (a + b) in a³ + b³ = (a + b)(a² − ab + b²), and the options are candidates. So substitute rather than solve: with x = a²/D and y = b²/D (D = a² − ab + b²), the first equation gives (a³ + b³)/D = a + b ✓ and the second gives (a²b + ab²)/D = ab(a + b)/D ✓. One option, two ticks, done in 40 seconds; eliminating by hand is three minutes of algebra with the same answer. The reversed pair (x = b²/D, y = a²/D) fails the first equation — that is the planted distractor.

E · Four plans, two constraints (2024-A Q57) beyond NCERT

Hours per bag on machines A and B, hours available, profit per bag, and four production plans: "which meets the constraints and maximises profit?" Each option is a point (standard, deluxe). Step 1 — kill the infeasible. Plan (50, 100) needs 6·50 + 10·100 = 1300 h on B against 1250: out. Plan (60, 90) needs 1260: out. Step 2 — compare profit among the survivors only. (75, 80) earns 20·75 + 30·80 = ₹3900; (100, 60) earns ₹3800. So (75, 80). The trap: the plan with the largest profit of all four (₹4000 for (50, 100)) is the one that breaks a machine — profit is compared after the feasibility cut, never before.

The 60-second triage for a linear-equations item

  1. Coefficients swapped? → add and subtract (A).
  2. Asked how many solutions, or for a parameter? → ratio test, scale to compare (§1, C).
  3. Options are formulas or plans? → substitute or check; do not solve (D, E).
  4. A story? → two unknowns, one equation per sentence, and re-read what is asked (B).

3Coordinate geometry — four formulas and the tricks built on them

The rationalised NCERT Class 10 chapter is two formulas long: distance and section (with the midpoint as its special case). Allen's items add the centroid and, at subject level, an area. There is no gradient and no y = mx + c here — that is your IGCSE chapter, and it is still legal to use, but nothing on Allen's 2027 list needs it.

Two words NCERT uses: the x-coordinate of a point is its abscissa (its distance from the y-axis) and the y-coordinate is its ordinate (its distance from the x-axis). A point on the x-axis is (x, 0); on the y-axis, (0, y).

AB = √[(x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²]
Distance. From the origin: √(x² + y²). Compare squared distances and take one root at the end.
P = ( (m x₂ + n x₁)/(m + n), (m y₂ + n y₁)/(m + n) )
Section — P divides AB in the ratio m : n from A. The m multiplies the far point B.
M = ( (x₁ + x₂)/2, (y₁ + y₂)/2 )
Midpoint = section with m = n. Parallelogram: the diagonals share a midpoint.
G = ( (x₁ + x₂ + x₃)/3, (y₁ + y₂ + y₃)/3 )
Centroid cut from NCERT — only in an optional exercise of the pre-2022 chapter; Allen assumes it (2024-A Q54). Divides each median 2 : 1 from the vertex.

Three traps in the section formula

  • Which point does m multiply? The far one. P divides AB "in the ratio 2 : 3" means AP : PB = 2 : 3, so x = (2x₂ + 3x₁)/5. Swap them and you get the point that divides it 3 : 2 — always an option.
  • "In what ratio does the x-axis divide the join of A and B?" Put P = (x, 0), call the ratio k : 1, and set the y-coordinate to zero: for A(1, −5), B(−4, 5) (Exercise 7.2 Q5), (5k − 5)/(k + 1) = 0 → k = 1 → 1 : 1, and then the point is x = (1·(−4) + 1·1)/2 = −3/2, i.e. (−3/2, 0) — here the axis cuts the segment in half. A point on the x-axis is (x, 0); on the y-axis it is (0, y). Mixing these two is the commonest slip in the chapter.
  • A negative k means P is outside the segment (external division), which the chapter does not use. In an "in what ratio" question it signals a slip: check that you set the right coordinate to zero (y for the x-axis, x for the y-axis) and the signs. Swapping A and B does not make k negative — it only turns k into 1/k.

More jobs from NCERT's examples and Exercise 7.2

  • In what ratio does a given point divide AB? Call the ratio k : 1 and use one coordinate. For (−4, 6) on the join of A(−6, 10) and B(3, −8): (3k − 6)/(k + 1) = −4 → k = 2/7, so 2 : 7 (Example 7).
  • Trisection. The two points that cut AB into three equal parts divide it 1 : 2 and 2 : 1. For A(2, −2) and B(−7, 4) they are (−1, 0) and (−4, 2) (Example 8) — and the second is also the mid-point of the first and B. Four equal parts are the ratios 1 : 3, 1 : 1 and 3 : 1.
  • A fraction of the way. "AP = 3/7 of AB" means AP : PB = 3 : 4 — the other part is what is left of 7 (Exercise 7.2 Q8).
  • Two flags on a ground ruled into 1 m lines (Exercise 7.2 Q3). A green flag a quarter of the way along the 100 m side, on line 2, is at (2, 25); a red flag a fifth of the way along, on line 8, is at (8, 20). They are √(6² + 5²) = √61 m apart, and a blue flag halfway between them goes at (5, 22.5) — on line 5, 22.5 m along.
  • The other end of a diameter. The centre is the mid-point, so the missing end = 2 × centre − known end. Centre (2, −3) and one end (1, 4) → the other end is (3, −10) (Exercise 7.2 Q7).
  • The area of a rhombus is ½ × the product of its diagonals, and each diagonal is one distance. Vertices (3, 0), (4, 5), (−1, 4), (−2, −1) in order: diagonals √32 = 4√2 and √72 = 6√2, area ½ × 4√2 × 6√2 = 24 (Exercise 7.2 Q10).

Distance — and never taking the root early

"A point on the x-axis equidistant from (2, −5) and (−2, 9)": the point is (x, 0), so (x − 2)² + 25 = (x + 2)² + 81. The x² cancels: −4x + 29 = 4x + 85 → −8x = 56 → x = −7, the point (−7, 0). No root was ever taken. The same habit settles "which of these points is nearest the origin" (compare x² + y²) and "is the triangle right-angled" (check a² + b² = c² on the three squared lengths).

Collinear points: A, B, C are in a line when AB + BC = AC with B between. For (1, 2), (3, 6), (5, 10): AB = √20 = 2√5, BC = 2√5, AC = √80 = 4√5 ✓. The faster test is the area below coming out as zero.

What NCERT uses the distance formula for

  • Do three points make a triangle, and of what kind? They do unless the two shorter distances only add up to the longest (then they are collinear). Then compare the squares: for (3, 2), (−2, −3) and (2, 3) they are 50, 52 and 2, and 50 + 2 = 52 — a right-angled triangle (Example 1). Two equal distances make it isosceles.
  • A relation between x and y. A point (x, y) equidistant from A and B: set the two squared distances equal. The x² and y² cancel and a straight line is left — the perpendicular bisector of AB. From (7, 1) and (3, 5): x − y = 2 (Example 4).
  • An unknown coordinate. P(2, −3), Q(10, y) and PQ = 10: 64 + (y + 3)² = 100, so y + 3 = ±6 and y = 3 or −9 (Exercise 7.1 Q8). A squared bracket gives two answers; keep both.

Midpoints — the parallelogram trick and the medial triangle

Parallelogram ABCD (in order): its diagonals bisect each other, so midpoint of AC = midpoint of BD. For A(−2, 1), B(a, 0), C(4, b), D(1, 2): x gives (−2 + 4)/2 = (a + 1)/2 → a = 1; y gives (1 + b)/2 = (0 + 2)/2 → b = 1. The unknowns come out one coordinate at a time — never set up four distances. This is 2024-U Q54: a rectangle ABCD on axes with A(9, 2), B(15, b), C(a, 13), D(5, 5) — mid AC = mid BD gives (9 + a)/2 = (15 + 5)/2 → a = 11 and (2 + 13)/2 = (b + 5)/2 → b = 10, so a − b = 1. Equivalently, the vector from A to B equals the vector from D to C: (6, b − 2) = (a − 5, 8). The right angle was never used.

From midpoints back to vertices beyond NCERT

If D, E, F are the midpoints of BC, CA, AB, then A = E + F − D (add the two midpoints on A's sides, subtract the one opposite), and likewise B = F + D − E, C = D + E − F — coordinate by coordinate. Check with the figure: E + F − D = (4 − 1 − 2, 3 + 3 + 1) = (1, 7) = A ✓. And a side is twice the midline opposite it: BC = 2·EF. Either route answers "find the vertices / a side" in under a minute.

Area of a triangle — dropped from the print, kept here for two reasons cut from NCERT

For vertices (x₁, y₁), (x₂, y₂), (x₃, y₃): Area = ½ |x₁(y₂ − y₃) + x₂(y₃ − y₁) + x₃(y₁ − y₂)|. For (1, −1), (−4, 6), (−3, −5): ½ |1(6 + 5) + (−4)(−5 + 1) + (−3)(−1 − 6)| = ½ |11 + 16 + 21| = 24. 4Statistics — what NCERT Class 9 keeps, and what Allen asks anyway

The rationalised Class 9 chapter is graphs only: bar graph, histogram (including unequal class widths) and frequency polygon. Mean, median and mode were cut from it. Every Allen Statistics item in five papers was a mean or a range — so this section carries both halves, and marks which is which.

In the print — the three pictures

PictureForThe rule that gets tested
Bar graphcategories (months, subjects)bars of equal width with gaps; height = frequency. Mean from a bar graph = Σ(value × height) ÷ Σ(heights).
Histogramcontinuous classes (0–10, 10–20 …)no gaps; area ∝ frequency. Equal widths: height = frequency. Unequal widths: height = frequency × (smallest width ÷ this width) — what NCERT calls the number "per 10 marks interval". If the first class does not start at 0, the axis carries a kink (a break).
Frequency polygonthe same classesplot (class mark, frequency), join with straight lines, and close it to the axis at an imagined empty class on each end. Class mark = (lower + upper)/2. Its area equals the histogram's. Used when the data is continuous and very large, and for comparing two sets of data on one graph.

Four things NCERT's examples and exercise turn on

  • Make the classes continuous first. Classes written 118–126, 127–135, … leave gaps of 1. Move every limit by half the gap: 117.5–126.5, 126.5–135.5, … and only then draw the histogram or the polygon (Exercise 12.1 Q4 and Q7).
  • A class is not a value. If the class 145–153 holds the most leaves, you may not conclude that most leaves are 153 mm long — only that the commonest lengths lie somewhere in that class (Exercise 12.1 Q4).
  • "More than" adds classes. Lamps lasting more than 700 hours are every class from 700 up: 74 + 62 + 48 = 184 (Exercise 12.1 Q5).
  • NCERT's own unequal-width table (Example 3) has 7 students in 0–20 and 8 in 70–100 beside classes 10 marks wide. Their bars are drawn 7 × 10/20 = 3.5 and 8 × 10/30 ≈ 2.67 high. The first picture NCERT shows, with bars 7 and 8 high, is the wrong one.

When the first class starts at 0 there is no room for the imagined empty class before it: the polygon is drawn towards that class's mid-point and stops where it meets the vertical axis (Example 4).

Reading and drawing the other two pictures — NCERT's Examples 1, 2 and 5

  • Reading a bar graph (Example 1). One axis names the categories; each bar's height is a value. "How many students were born in November?" is one bar read against the scale — 4; "in which month were the most born?" is the tallest bar — August.
  • Drawing one (Example 2, Exercise 12.1 Q1–3). Bars of equal width with equal gaps, one per category; choose a scale that fits the largest value — 75 seats in Q3, so one unit for every 10 seats will do — and draw each bar to its value. The questions after it are read off the picture: the party with the most seats is the tallest bar, A with 75 (Q3); the largest cause in Q1 is reproductive health conditions, 31.8%.
  • A frequency polygon without a histogram (Example 5, the figure above): class marks across, frequencies up, join the points, and close it at the class marks of an empty class on each side.
  • Two polygons on one graph compare two sets (Exercise 12.1 Q6 and Q7). Section A's marks peak in 20–30 (17 students), section B's in 10–20 (19 students), so section A did better.

Not in the print, asked every year — the four numbers cut from NCERT

Mean = Σx / n
From a frequency table: Σfx / Σf. The moment a mean is stated, write the sum: Σx = n × mean.
Median = middle of the sorted list
n odd: the ((n + 1)/2)th value. n even: the average of the two middle values — 3, 7, 8, 12, 15, 20 → (8 + 12)/2 = 10.
Mode = most frequent value
2, 3, 3, 5, 5, 5, 8 → 5. A list can have two modes or none; the mode is a value, not a frequency.
Range = largest − smallest
One subtraction — the whole trap is which value is the largest when one of them is x (2024-U Q50).

The five statistics machines the real papers use beyond NCERT

A · Mean ⇄ sum (2018-S Q58, and the "corrected mean"). "The mean of x, x + 2, x + 7, x + 11 and 2x is 16" → the sum is 80 → 6x + 20 = 80 → x = 10 — and the question asked for 2x, 20. The corrected-mean version: "the mean of 10 readings was 25; one reading of 51 had been copied as 15" → the sum 250 was short by 36 → 286/10 = 28.6. Fix the sum, then divide again.

B · One in, one out (Sample Q59). The mean moves by (in − out)/n: 30 students, 71.5 kg out, 53.5 kg in → the sum drops by 18 → the mean drops by 18/30 = 0.6 → 62.5 − 0.6 = 61.9. No need to compute 1875 at all. Removing an observation with nothing added: new mean = (sum − x)/(n − 1) — the n changes too.

C · Averages of overlapping groups (2024-A Q50). Turn every average statement into a sum statement, then subtract. "Average of the first three of five numbers is 10 less than the average of the last three" → (a + b + c) = (c + d + e) − 30 → the shared c cancels: a + b = d + e − 30 = 42 − 30 = 12. The stem's "odd numbers, ascending" was decoration — the arithmetic never used it.

D · A range with an ordering clause (2024-U Q50). "In descending order x, 76, …, 61; range 39." The word descending makes x the largest, so x − 61 = 39 → x = 100 — not 76 − 39 = 37, which would put x at the wrong end. Then the tail: digit sum of 100 is 1, √1 = 1. One of the four options was 100 — x itself, offered to anyone who stopped at the first rung.

E · A whole-number condition on the sum (2017-E Q58). 15 students average 12 books → 180 books. Two students got k each; the other 13 average a whole number → (180 − 2k)/13 must be an integer. Test the options: k = 25 gives 130/13 = 10 ✓; 11, 15, 20 give 158, 150, 140, none divisible by 13. The options are the method — do not solve a congruence.

Six facts that shortcut the puzzles beyond NCERT

  • Mean of 1, 2, …, n is (n + 1)/2; of any evenly spaced list (an AP) the mean is the middle term (or the average of the two middle terms) — so mean = median there.
  • Add k to every observation: mean, median and mode all rise by k; the range does not change.
  • Multiply every observation by k: mean, median, mode and range all multiply by k (range by |k|).
  • Two groups combined: mean = (n₁m₁ + n₂m₂)/(n₁ + n₂) — never the average of the two means unless n₁ = n₂.
  • The median is unmoved by how extreme the end values are; the mean is not. "Which changes if the largest value is doubled?" → the mean (and the range), not the median — nor the mode, unless that largest value was itself the mode.
  • Class mark of 20–30 is 25; class width is 10; the "upper limit of the class 30–40" is 40 even though 40 is counted in the next class. (Class marks, widths and limits are in the print; the rule that a boundary value goes into the next class was in the section NCERT cut.)

Hand arithmetic without a calculator

Dividing by 30: divide by 3, then move the point — 1857 ÷ 3 = 619, so 1857 ÷ 30 = 61.9. Dividing by 13 or 7: check the options rather than divide. A mean stated to two decimals for n = 20 or n = 25 comes from a whole-number sum (16.35 × 20 = 327; 12.36 × 25 = 309) — so if a mean of your own comes out with three decimals, re-add the list.

1Sample Q46 machine, carried one step on · ~60 seconds

If 47x + 31y = 63 and 31x + 47y = 15, then x² − y² equals

1−3
21

44
Working

Step 1 — swapped coefficients, so add and subtract. Add: 78(x + y) = 78 → x + y = 1. Subtract (first minus second): 16(x − y) = 48 → x − y = 3.

Step 2 — the thing asked is a product of exactly those two. x² − y² = (x + y)(x − y) = 1 × 3 = 3. Option (3). (x = 2, y = −1 if you want them — but you never needed them.)

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) −3 — subtracting the other way round (second minus first gives y − x = −3) and forgetting that the product then needs (x + y)(y − x) = −(x² − y²). (2) 1 — stopping at x + y. (4) 4 — x², having solved fully and then read the question as x² alone. Elimination. x + y = 1 comes in ten seconds and kills nothing on its own, but x − y = 3 with it gives integer x, y at once — any option that is not a small integer product is suspect. Two of the four options differ only by sign: decide the sign from which equation you subtracted.
2The parameter question, with two parameters · ~90 seconds cut from NCERT

The pair 2x + 5y = 9 and (a + b)x + (2a − b)y = 27 has infinitely many solutions. The value of a − b is

1−1
26
37
48
Working

Step 1 — infinitely many ⇒ all three ratios equal. (a + b)/2 = (2a − b)/5 = 27/9 = 3. The constants give the common ratio straight away, which is the setter's kindness: a + b = 6 and 2a − b = 15.

Step 2 — a new pair, in a and b. Add: 3a = 21 → a = 7; then b = −1. So a − b = 7 − (−1) = 8. Option (4).

Where each wrong option comes from. (2) 6 — a + b, the first ratio's right-hand side, read as the answer. (3) 7 — a alone. (1) −1 — b alone. Elimination. Three of the four options are intermediate values in the honest working — the classic "stopped early" ladder. A negative b is the setter's twist: a − b then exceeds a, so once b < 0 is seen, every option ≤ a is out. Check the ratio if unsure: (7 − 1)/2 = 3 and (14 + 1)/5 = 3 ✓.

Working

Fast route — the midline. EF joins the midpoints of CA and AB, so EF is parallel to BC and half of it. EF = √[(5 − 2)² + (0 − 4)²] = √(9 + 16) = 5, so BC = 10. Option (3). Thirty seconds.

4A mean and a median together · ~2 minutes

Five distinct positive integers have mean 12 and median 15. The largest possible value of the greatest of them is

126
228
330
442
Working

Step 1 — both statements as constraints on a sorted list a < b < c < d < e. Mean 12 → the sum is 60. Median 15 → c = 15, so a and b are below 15 and d, e above it.

Step 2 — to make e as large as possible, make everything else as small as possible. a = 1, b = 2, d = 16 (the smallest distinct integer above 15). Then e = 60 − 1 − 2 − 15 − 16 = 26. Option (1). Check: 1, 2, 15, 16, 26 → sum 60 ✓, middle value 15 ✓, all distinct ✓.

Where each wrong option comes from. (4) 42 — 60 − 1 − 2 − 15, forgetting that d exists at all. (2) 28 — allowing repeats: 1, 1, 15, 15, 28 (the stem said distinct). (3) 30 — 60 − 15 − 15, treating the median as if it were two of the numbers. Elimination. e must leave room for four other numbers, three of them at least 1, 2, 15 and one more above 15 — so e ≤ 60 − 18 − 16 = 26. Anything above 26 is impossible, which kills three options at once without finding the list.
5The unequal-width histogram, NCERT's signature item · ~90 seconds

A histogram is drawn for the classes 0–10 (frequency 8), 10–30 (14), 30–40 (9) and 40–60 (12). The tallest and the shortest bars belong, respectively, to the classes

110–30 and 0–10
230–40 and 40–60
340–60 and 10–30
410–30 and 40–60
Working

Step 1 — the widths are not equal, so heights are not frequencies. Widths 10, 20, 10, 20; the smallest is 10. Adjusted heights: 8 × 10/10 = 8; 14 × 10/20 = 7; 9 × 10/10 = 9; 12 × 10/20 = 6.

Step 2 — read the extremes off the adjusted list 8, 7, 9, 6: tallest is 30–40 (9), shortest is 40–60 (6). Option (2). The class with the largest frequency, 10–30, is neither — that is the whole point of the adjustment.

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) 10–30 and 0–10 — reading raw frequencies (14 tallest, 8 shortest), no adjustment. (3) 40–60 and 10–30 — halving 10–30 but forgetting 40–60: 8, 7, 9, 12. (4) 10–30 and 40–60 — halving 40–60 but forgetting 10–30: 8, 14, 9, 6. Elimination. Both 20-wide classes must be halved, so neither can be tallest unless its frequency is more than twice 9 — 14 and 12 are not. Of the two 10-wide classes, 30–40 (9) beats 0–10 (8), so 30–40 is the tallest; the shortest is the least of 8, 7 and 6 — 40–60.
