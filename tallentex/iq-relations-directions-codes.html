Relations, Directions & Codes

Official topics: Blood Relation, Direction Sense, Coding-Decoding and Alphabet Test. None of them needs any maths beyond counting. They need a method you draw on the booklet (a family tree, a small grid, the alphabet with numbers under it), because every question is built so that doing it in your head drops one link.

What the paper actually asks here

All five cached papers (2017-E, 2018-S, 2024-U, 2024-A and the sample) were read question by question and each item solved and checked against the official key. This unit is about 4 of the 20 Mental Ability questions in every paper, so about 16 marks.

  • Blood Relation, in all five papers (8 questions). Most often a spoken riddle: "Pointing to a person in the photograph, a lady said…". Also a bare chain ("If X is brother of son of Y's son…") and symbol-coded relations ("P × Q means P is the father of Q … which of the following represents S is the niece of T?").
  • Direction Sense, in all five (6 questions). A walk with turns, then the shortest distance home. The positions of several points, then where one is from another (one version asks it for a man facing north). A string of left and right turns, then the direction someone ends up facing.
  • Coding-Decoding, in all five (5 questions, and the key to one was later scrapped). The rule changes every time: letter shifts that alternate +1 / −3, letters numbered from Z instead of from A, the product of the word lengths, matching sentences to scrambled codes. 2024-U also had a letter analogy built on a fixed shift of 8.
  • Alphabet Test, once (2024-A). The alphabet is rearranged first (vowels moved to the end), then there's a nested position: "the 12th letter to the left of the 17th letter to the right of the 20th letter from the right end".

None of these questions had a figure. They are all plain text, so you draw the tree, the grid or the numbered alphabet yourself. Allen also recycles: the points-and-a-man-facing-north question in 2024-A (Q69) is word for word the sample's Q74.

Your teacher found the real paper harder than the sample. Many real items chain two steps. You follow the turns and then use Pythagoras (2024-U). You rearrange the alphabet and then count a nested position in the new series (2024-A). You translate a symbol chain and then check every link and gender (2017-E). Get the method below down to zero effort so your attention is free for the second step.

1Blood Relation: draw the tree, never hold it in your head

Every blood-relation question is a chain of links like "father of", "son of" and "wife of". Mistakes come from dropping a link, from guessing a gender, or from answering about the wrong person. A four-symbol family tree prevents all three.

The method, in three moves

  1. Start from the person you know, usually the speaker, and add one link at a time. With 's ("my father's son's mother") that means reading left to right. With of ("the son of the brother of my mother") it means reading right to left, because the person you know comes last.
  2. Draw each person once, with generations on separate levels, and mark + / − only when a word proves it.
  3. Read the answer the right way round. "How is A related to B?" means "A is B's ___".

The five traps the papers set

  • The speaker can be the person described. Meera's "my father's only son" is her brother. If a boy says exactly the same words, the only son is himself, and the boy in the photo is his son. In the same way, "my mother's only daughter" said by a girl is the girl herself. When the chain comes back to the speaker's own generation, ask "could this be me?"
  • A name never gives a gender. Only a relation word does. son, brother, father, husband, uncle, nephew are male. daughter, sister, mother, wife, aunt, niece are female. child, sibling, parent, spouse, cousin say nothing. "Kiran is Ravi's parent" does not make Kiran a father or a mother, so a statement that depends on it is not definitely true. A pronoun in the question ("he recalled") does count.
  • "Only" is doing work. "The only son of my father's mother" is your father. Drop the word "only" and he might be an uncle. A plain "my grandmother" is ambiguous, but the 2024-A key reads it as the father's mother. Follow that unless the question says maternal.
  • Answering about the wrong person. If you work out that A is B's nephew, then B is A's uncle or aunt. The two answers are mirror words, and expect both among the options.
  • Maternal or paternal. The side is the parent the link passes through: mother's brother is the maternal uncle, father's brother the paternal uncle. Both are often options.

Coded relations: translate every symbol, then draw

Suppose P # Q means P is the mother of Q, P @ Q means P is the brother of Q, P % Q means P is the father of Q, and P $ Q means P is the wife of Q. Which expression means A is the maternal uncle of D?

A maternal uncle is the brother of the mother, so you need exactly "A brother of someone, and that someone mother of D":

  • A @ B # D: A is the brother of B, and B is the mother of D. ✓ Maternal uncle.
  • A @ B % D: B is the father of D, which makes A the paternal uncle.
  • A % B @ D: A is the father of B, and B is the brother of D. That makes A D's father.
  • B # A @ D: A is the brother of D. They are siblings, not uncle and nephew.

A gender word fixes the gender of the person it names. A husband or wife word also fixes the spouse. A @ B (A is the brother of B) makes A male and says nothing about B. A $ B (A is the wife of B) makes A female and B male, because a wife's spouse is a husband. Watch which way the definition runs, too. The sample paper wrote "P − Q means Q is father of P", where the gendered person is the second one. Questions that ask "which is the niece" or "which is the sister" are won or lost on this.

2Direction Sense: put the walk on a grid

Turns cause the mistakes, and a right turn is always clockwise, whichever way you are facing. Convert every turn into a compass direction as you go, write each leg as a move east–west or north–south, and add them up at the end.

Right = clockwise · Left = anticlockwise
Facing N: right is E. Facing E: right is S. Facing S: right is W. Facing W: right is N. Left is always the opposite of right.
East +x · North +y
Add up the legs. Final (x, y): both positive is NE, x positive and y negative is SE, both negative is SW, x negative and y positive is NW. If one of them is 0, the answer is due N, S, E or W.
Shortest = √(x² + y²)
The numbers are chosen to come out whole, so learn the triples: 3-4-5 · 6-8-10 · 5-12-13 · 9-12-15 · 8-15-17 · 7-24-25.
Turning on the spot: add angles
Take N = 0°, E = 90°, S = 180°, W = 270°. A clockwise turn adds, an anticlockwise turn subtracts, then take the result mod 360°. A clockwise turn of 90° is a right turn.

"Where is A with respect to B?" Stand at B, look at A

P is due north of Q, and R is due west of Q. Where is P with respect to R? Put Q at the origin: P = (0, +1) and R = (−1, 0). Going from R to P is east and north, so P is north-east of R. The reverse, R with respect to P, is south-west, and that is likely to be an option too.

Read "X is north-east of Y" exactly as written: stand at Y and X lies north-east. Never flip it. When the question puts a person facing a direction at B, answer in compass terms if the options are compass directions (in both real versions they were, so the facing was a red herring). Convert to left or right only when the options use those words. For a man facing north, west is his left.

No starting direction given? ("He walks 10 km straight, then turns right…") Assume he starts facing north. The shortest distance does not depend on that choice. A right angle as a clue: if D is north of A and ∠CDA = 90°, then DC runs due east–west, so D and C are level.

Shadows: the sun tells you where you are facing

  • The sun rises in the east, so in the morning shadows fall towards the west.
  • It sets in the west, so in the evening shadows fall towards the east.
  • Facing south swaps left and right compared with the page: your right is west. So if your shadow falls to your right in the morning (towards the west), you are facing south.
  • If you face your shadow in the morning, you are facing west and your right hand points north. This is a common variant in practice books. None of the five cached papers used it, but it belongs to the same official topic.

A twisted signpost: find the twist, then undo it

A signpost has been turned on its pole, so every arm points the wrong way by the same angle in the same sense. One arm tells you the twist. Say the arm marked NORTH actually points east. That is a turn of 90° clockwise, so every arm points 90° clockwise of its label: the arm marked EAST points south, SOUTH points west, and WEST points north.

To travel west, go back 90° anticlockwise from where you want to go: follow the arm marked SOUTH. Two slips to avoid: turning the wrong way (that gives NORTH), and following the arm labelled WEST (that takes you north).

Where the marks go

  • A turn is relative, a direction is absolute. "Turns left" while facing south means walking east. Write the compass letter on each leg as you read it.
  • Shortest distance is not distance walked. The total of the legs is usually among the options.
  • Adding the legs instead of using Pythagoras. 12 km east and 5 km north is 13 km from the start, not 17.
  • Reversing "with respect to". Where the finish is from the start and where the start is from the finish are opposite directions, and expect both among the options.

3Coding-Decoding: find the rule, then prove it on every letter

You are shown a word and its code, and you must code a new word by the same rule. The whole skill is writing the letter numbers down and testing the rule on every position. A rule that fits the first two letters and fails the third is the distractor.

After Z comes A
Shifts wrap around. Y + 3 = B, A − 3 = X. To go back 3 from A, count Z, Y, X. Going forward to D by mistake is the standard slip.
From Z: 27 − n
"Letters numbered backwards" means Z = 1 … A = 26, which is the same as 27 − the usual position. Using 26 − n puts every letter one out.
Test simple → complex
For a word-number code try: sum of positions → sum from Z → average of positions (FHG → 7) → doubled or squared positions (C → 6 or 9) → number of letters (added, or multiplied across words). Ignore hyphens and spaces. Accept a rule only when it fits every example given.

Sentence codes: match by the shared word, never by position

sun is hot → ka la ma  ·  sun rises early → ta ka pa  ·  hot tea is good → la ra ma sa

  • Sentences 1 and 2 share only sun, and their codes share only ka, so sun = ka. Sun is the first word of both sentences, but ka is the first code in one and the second in the other, because the order of the code words is scrambled.
  • Sentences 1 and 3 share is and hot, and their codes share la and ma. Each of is and hot is la or ma, but these three sentences cannot tell you which. When "cannot be determined" is an option, this is the reason it can be correct.
  • Elimination pins a word down. Add a fourth sentence: early morning walk → pa zo ni. It shares only early with sentence 2 and only pa with its code, so early = pa. Sentence 2 now has one word and one code left, so rises = ta.

Substitution: the answer is the code word

"If white is called black, black is called red, red is called yellow and yellow is called green, what is the colour of blood?" Blood is red, and red is called yellow, so the answer is yellow. "Red" is the trap: it is the real colour, but the question asks what it is called in the code.

Matrix codes: row first, then column

The letters sit in a grid with numbered rows and columns:

12345
1ABCDE
2FGHIJ
3KLMNO
4PQRST
5UVWXY

Each letter is written as its row number followed by its column number. So G is 22, O is row 3, column 5, giving 35, and T is 45. GOT = 22 35 45. The trap option reads column first (22 53 54). When a question gives two grids, find the letter in either one, and still write row, then column.

4Alphabet Test: number the series, then do plain arithmetic

Every alphabet-test question becomes addition and subtraction once each letter has a number. What makes the hard versions hard is changing the series first (reversed, vowels removed, halves swapped). After that change, the old positions are wrong.

k-th from right = N − k + 1
In the full alphabet N = 26, so k-th from the right is 27 − k (7th from the right is the 20th letter, T). In a shortened series, use its own N.
k to the right: p + k · to the left: p − k
Here p is a position counted from the left. Moving right increases it. Convert every "from the right" position into "from the left" before adding.
Nested? Start at the end
In "the 5th letter to the left of the 9th letter from the right", the letter you can find directly is named last. Find it, then work back towards the start of the sentence.
Letters between = difference − 1
Between D (4) and J (10) there are 10 − 4 − 1 = 5 letters (E F G H I), not 6. The same off-by-one as people in a row.
Midway letter = average
The letter exactly midway between D (4) and R (18) is at (4 + 18) ÷ 2 = 11, K. If the two positions add to an odd number, no single letter is midway.
Dictionary order
Compare words letter by letter from the left. The first letter that differs decides. If one word runs out first (CAR, CARE), the shorter one comes first.

Rearranged series: rewrite and renumber before anything else

If the vowels are removed, 21 letters remain: B C D F G H J K L M N P Q R S T V W X Y Z, numbered 1 to 21. B is now 1st and Z is 21st. The question is now about this row, so "from the right end" uses N = 21, and "the 12th letter" means the 12th consonant, P. Write the new row out on the booklet and number it. Doing it in your head is where the letter slips by one.

Letter pairs: "as many letters between them in the word as in the alphabet"

A pair qualifies when the gap between the two letters' positions in the word equals the gap between their positions in the alphabet. Take DECIMAL (D4 E5 C3 I9 M13 A1 L12):

  • D, E: next to each other in the word, and next to each other in the alphabet (4, 5). ✓
  • E … A: 4 places apart in the word (2nd and 6th), and E5 to A1 is 4 apart. ✓ This pair runs backwards (E comes after A), and it still counts.
  • I … L: 3 places apart in the word (4th and 7th), and I9 to L12 is 3 apart. ✓

Answer: 3 pairs. Unless the question says "in the same order as in the alphabet", count pairs in both directions. The forward-only count, 2 here, is the planted wrong option.

5Five worked questions, with the elimination spelled out

These are pitched at TALLENTEX level: two steps each. Try each one before opening the working. The elimination lines are worth studying most, because every wrong option below is what a real slip produces.

1Blood Relation · ~60 seconds

Pointing to a boy, Asha says, "He is the son of the only child of my father's wife." How is the boy related to Asha?

1Brother
2Nephew
3Son
4Grandson
Working

"of" chains are read from the right, starting with the person you know.

My father's wife is Asha's mother. Her only child must be Asha herself, because Asha is her child, so if there were anyone else she would not be the only one. The boy is the son of Asha.

Answer: Son, option (3).

Elimination: (2) Nephew comes from treating "the only child of my father's wife" as a sibling of Asha, whose son would be her nephew. Forgetting that the speaker can be that only child is the trap this item is built on. The answer does not depend on Asha's gender. (1) Brother skips a link: it is "son of my father's wife", with the only child dropped. (4) Grandson is right about the wrong person: the boy is the grandson of Asha's mother, but the question asks how he is related to Asha.
2Direction Sense · ~75 seconds

Meera starts from her school and walks 9 km north. She turns right and walks 5 km, turns right again and walks 4 km, then turns left and walks 7 km. How far is she from the school, and in which direction?

113 km north-east
213 km south-west
317 km north-east
425 km north-east
Working

Step 1: turn each turn into a direction. She starts facing north. Turning right gives east. Turning right again gives south. Turning left while facing south gives east.

Step 2: add the legs. North–south: +9 − 4 = 5 km north. East–west: 5 + 7 = 12 km east.

Step 3: distance and direction. √(12² + 5²) = √169 = 13 km. Both components are positive (east and north), so she is north-east of the school.

Elimination: (4) 25 km is 9 + 5 + 4 + 7, the distance walked, not the distance from school. (3) 17 km is 12 + 5: the legs added instead of using Pythagoras. (2) has the right distance but the reversed direction. South-west is where the school is from Meera, not where she is from the school. The 5-12-13 triple settles the distance in two seconds, and after that you only have to check the direction.
3Coding-Decoding · ~70 seconds

In a certain code, TIGER is written as UFHBS. How is PANEL written in that code?

1QDOBM
2QXOBM
3QXOHM
4OXMBK
Working

Step 1: find the rule on every letter. T→U is +1, I→F is −3, G→H is +1, E→B is −3 and R→S is +1. The shifts alternate +1, −3.

Step 2: apply it to PANEL. P+1 = Q. A−3 wraps round to Z, Y, X. N+1 = O. E−3 = B. L+1 = M.

Answer: QXOBM, option (2).

Elimination: (1) QDOBM moves A forward to D. Going backwards past A is the wrap-around slip, and it is the most tempting wrong option. (3) QXOHM uses +3 instead of −3 on the E, a sign slip on the fourth letter. (4) OXMBK uses −1 instead of +1 on the first, third and fifth letters, which is what happens when you work out T→U as a step backwards. Every wrong option matches the right code in at least two letters, so check every position, not just the first.
4Alphabet Test · ~80 seconds

If all the vowels are removed from the English alphabet, which letter will be the 4th to the right of the 11th letter from the right end?

1R
2S
3T
4Y
Working

Step 1: rewrite the series. Removing A, E, I, O, U leaves 21 letters: B C D F G H J K L M N P Q R S T V W X Y Z.

Step 2: the letter named last. The 11th from the right of 21 letters is position 21 − 11 + 1 = 11 from the left, which is N.

Step 3: 4 to the right. Position 11 + 4 = 15: P (12), Q (13), R (14), S (15).

Answer: S, option (2).

Elimination: (3) T is the answer in the full alphabet (11th from the right is P, and 4 on is T), where the rearrangement has been ignored. (4) Y uses the full-alphabet formula 27 − 11 = 16 on the 21-letter row, which gives T as the start and Y as the answer. (1) R drops the +1 and takes position 21 − 11 = 10 (M), which also puts the answer one letter early.
5Statement item · ~90 seconds

Ravi is the son of Kiran. Kiran's only sister is Neha. Neha's husband is Arun. Consider the following statements.

  1. Arun is Ravi's uncle.
  2. Kiran is Ravi's father.
  3. Neha is Ravi's aunt.
  4. Ravi is Arun's nephew.

Which of the statements are definitely true?

1I and III only
2II and III only
3I, II, III and IV
4I, III and IV only
Working

II cannot be decided. "Ravi is the son of Kiran" makes Ravi male and says nothing about Kiran, who may be his father or his mother. The name does not settle it (in India Kiran is used for both), so II is not definitely true.

III is true. Neha is the sister of Ravi's parent, and "sister" makes her female, so she is his aunt.

I is true. An aunt's husband is an uncle, and "husband" makes Arun male.

IV is true. Arun is Ravi's uncle, and Ravi is male ("son"), so Ravi is Arun's nephew.

So I, III and IV, which is option (4).

Elimination: settle II first, because it is the only statement whose truth depends on something missing, and it appears in two options. Once II is out, (2) and (3) are gone, which leaves (1) or (4). Those differ only on IV, and "son" answers that at once. (3) is the answer you give if you read Kiran as a man's name. (2) keeps II and also rejects I and IV, which is what happens if you think an aunt's husband is not really an uncle. (1) drops IV, which is the slip of reading it the wrong way round, as "Arun is Ravi's nephew".
