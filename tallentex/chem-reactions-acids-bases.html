Chemical Reactions; Acids, Bases & Salts

Official topics: Chemical Reactions and Equations and Acids, Bases and Salts (both Class 10) — two topics, one card, and the largest chemistry unit on the paper: 5 to 9 of the 15 Chemistry questions in every sitting. Your IGCSE course already has most of the chemistry — balancing, redox, indicators, neutralisation, hydrated salts, rusting. What it does not have is the NCERT framing Allen writes from: the five reaction types by their NCERT names, NCERT's own demonstration reactions with their colours and smells, phenolphthalein and turmeric, the pH scale as numbers you calculate with, and the four "salts of common salt" — bleaching powder, baking soda, washing soda, plaster of Paris. A 35-minute read, then the paper.

  2. A balancing item in three of the five papers (2017-E Q30, 2024-U Q25, 2024-A Q29; none in 2018-S or the sample), always asked as "the values of x, y, z" or a ratio of coefficients — never "balance this".   3. Heating lead nitrate is Allen's favourite reaction — asked three times (2017-E Q25 and Q30, 2024-U Q17): white crystals, a yellow residue, brown fumes.   4. The salt tree is in every paper. Brine electrolysis (2017-E Q22, 2024-U Q23), gypsum and plaster of Paris (2018-S Q28, Sample Q19), baking soda → washing soda (2024-A Q21), water of crystallisation (2024-U Q22).   5.   6.   7. Figures are apparatus, not geometry — a test tube over a burner, four rusting nails, an electrolytic cell, a process flow — five of the 34 items. The figure is never where the difficulty is; the difficulty is the chain of names it stands for (X is heated to give Y, which with Z gives …).

Budget accordingly. These are 30–60-second recall items dressed as chains: a 20–36-mark unit where the marks are lost to one unchecked cell or one colour misremembered, not to anything you cannot do. Everything below is organised by what Allen actually writes.

1Reading and balancing an equation — the "x, y, z" machine

Three of the five papers have an item that hands you an unbalanced equation with letters for coefficients. The chemistry is Class 9 (the law of conservation of mass); the marks go to a routine done without a slip.

Definitions, NCERT's way

A chemical equation writes the reactants on the left and the products on the right of an arrow. It is balanced when every element has the same number of atoms on both sides — because mass can neither be created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction. A skeletal equation is the unbalanced one. State symbols: (s) (l) (g) (aq); conditions go above the arrow — Δ or "heat", sunlight, electricity, a catalyst. A coefficient (the 2 in 2H2O) multiplies the whole formula; a subscript (the 2 in H2O) is part of the formula and must never be changed to balance an equation.

The hit-and-trial routine (NCERT's own example)

Fe + H2O → Fe3O4 + H2 (skeletal)
① Draw boxes round every formula; count atoms: Fe 1 | 3, H 2 | 2, O 1 | 4.
② Start with the element that appears in the fewest formulas or the most complex formula — here O in Fe3O4. Put 4 in front of H2O.
③ That makes H 8 on the left → 4H2 on the right. ④ Fe: 3 on the right → 3Fe on the left.
⑤ Recount everything. ⑥ Add states and the condition.
3Fe(s) + 4H2O(g) → Fe3O4(s) + 4H2(g)

Two habits that make the exam version fast: balance a polyatomic ion as one unit when it appears intact on both sides (count "SO4" groups, not S and O separately), and leave hydrogen and oxygen for last unless one of them sits in the most complex formula (as O does in Fe3O4 above) — they appear in the most formulas, so they usually fall into place once the rest is fixed. If a coefficient comes out as a fraction, multiply the whole equation through to clear it.

How Allen asks it — three real shapes

  • "The values of x, y, z" (2024-U Q25): 3BaCl2 + Al2(SO4)3 → 2AlCl3 + 3BaSO4. Count sulphate groups (3 → z = 3), then Ba (x = 3), then Al (y = 2), then check Cl: 6 = 6. The distractors each pass one check and fail another — (2, 2, 3) balances Al and sulphate but not Ba or Cl; (2, 3, 3) balances only sulphate; (3, 3, 2) balances nothing. Only (3, 2, 3) survives the chloride check.
  • A polyatomic organic ion (2024-A Q29): 6C2H4O2 + 2Fe → 2Fe(C2H3O2)3 + 3H2. The acetate group C2H3O2 is the unit: each iron(III) acetate holds three of them, so two products need six acetic acids; six acids give six H atoms that are not in acetate → 3H2. The trap is the hydrogen: each acid molecule has one H outside the acetate group, and only those six H make the H2 — option (3) 6, 2, 2, 2 gives 24 H on the left against 22 on the right.
  • A ratio of coefficients (2017-E Q30): 2Pb(NO3)2 → 2PbO + 4NO2 + O2, asked as P × R ÷ (Q + Z). Write the 1s. Q = 1 (the subscript in PbO), Z = 1 (the coefficient of O2): 2 × 4 ÷ (1 + 1) = 4. The item is only hard if you forget that an unwritten coefficient is 1, not 0.

Traps

  • A "balanced" option that changed a subscript (H2O → H2O2) is always wrong, however neatly the atoms count.
  • Smallest whole numbers. 4Fe + 6H2O → … is balanced but not the answer; halve it.
  • Nitrate decompositions produce O2 as well as NO2 — an option with only NO2 cannot balance oxygen.

Two words NCERT defines and Allen can ask

Oxidising agent — the substance that gives oxygen (or takes hydrogen) and is itself reduced: CuO in CuO + H2 → Cu + H2O. Reducing agent — the substance that takes the oxygen and is itself oxidised: H2 there, C in ZnO + C → Zn + CO. The agent is always named after what it does to the other substance, which is the opposite of what happens to it.

Beyond NCERT — redox in an ionic equation (2018-S Q30)

Allen gave MnO4− + SO32− + H+ → SO42− + Mn2+ + H2O (unbalanced, as the booklet prints it) and asked what is oxidised and what reduced. NCERT's oxygen rule still answers it: sulphite SO32− gained an oxygen to become sulphate → oxidised; permanganate MnO4− lost all four oxygens to become Mn2+ → reduced. H+ became H2O — it neither gained nor lost (its oxidation number stays +1), so any option that says "H+ is oxidised" or "reduced" is wrong. The IGCSE electron view (oxidation = loss of electrons, rise in oxidation number: Mn +7 → +2 reduced, S +4 → +6 oxidised) gives the same answer; use whichever is faster, and remember permanganate is the classic oxidising agent.

The "which type" traps, from the real items

  • Both labels can be true. BaCl2 + (NH4)2SO4 → BaSO4↓ + 2NH4Cl is a double displacement and a precipitation (2018-S Q25 keys the option with both). It is not a displacement — nothing is an element — and not a combination.
  • A displacement only happens downhill. 2AgNO3 + Cu → Cu(NO3)2 + 2Ag happens (Cu is above Ag); Cl2 displaces iodine from KI (a more reactive non-metal); BaSO4 + 2NaCl → BaCl2 + Na2SO4 cannot happen — BaSO4 is the insoluble product, and a double displacement does not run backwards out of a precipitate (2018-S Q29).
  • "Which is a chemical change" (2017-E Q29): a new substance must form. Sublimation, dissolving, evaporation, melting are physical; burning, rusting, a cracker bursting, cooking, digestion, milk souring are chemical.
  • Electrolysis of water is a decomposition (an electrolytic one) — Sample Q30 pairs it with "electric decomposition", not with combination. And the two gases come in the ratio H2 : O2 = 2 : 1 by volume, hydrogen at the cathode.

Traps in the colour items

  • Brown gas = NO2, not NO. 2024-U Q17's distractors swap in NO and PbI2; the yellow solid + brown gas pair is PbO + NO2, nothing else on this list.
  • Ferrous sulphate has two stages (2024-A Q30): gentle heat only drives off the water of crystallisation — the residue is anhydrous FeSO4, not yet Fe2O3; strong heat then decomposes it. An option that has the reddish-brown residue in stage I is wrong even though the colour is right.
  • "Burning sulphur" is the smell of SO2 — the same gas the 2024-A Q20 spatula item makes by burning sulphur itself. Its solution is acidic (sulphurous acid), so it reddens moist blue litmus and leaves dry litmus alone (section 5).
  • Quicklime vs slaked lime vs lime water vs limestone: CaO · Ca(OH)2 · Ca(OH)2(aq) · CaCO3. Heating limestone gives quicklime; quicklime + water gives slaked lime (hot, fizzing — combination, exothermic); slaked lime painted on a wall takes CO2 from the air over two to three days and turns back into limestone, which is why whitewash "dries" to a shiny finish (2017-E Q23 — the answer is CO2, not oxygen).

4Heat, rust, rancid fat — the effects of reactions

Four short NCERT paragraphs, each of which has produced a real question.

Exothermic and endothermic

Exothermic reactions give out heat: burning natural gas, respiration (glucose + O2 → CO2 + H2O + energy), the decomposition of vegetable matter into compost, quicklime + water, and — NCERT's line in the acids chapter — dissolving an acid or a base in water. Endothermic reactions take heat in: every thermal decomposition, and photosynthesis.

Beyond NCERT — the heat of dissolving a salt (2024-U Q30)

Allen asked which of four dissolvings are endothermic. The ones worth knowing: ammonium salts (NH4NO3, NH4Cl, (NH4)2SO4) dissolve with cooling — the instant cold pack; glucose and most sugars also cool water slightly; anhydrous CuSO4, NaOH, CaO and concentrated H2SO4 dissolve with heating. The tell for CuSO4 is that it is re-hydrating — the reverse of the endothermic heating in section 3, so it must be exothermic.

Corrosion

A metal attacked by air, moisture or acids around it: iron rusts (a reddish-brown flaky coat — hydrated iron(III) oxide), silver turns black (silver sulphide Ag2S, from sulphur compounds in the air), copper turns green (basic copper carbonate, from moist CO2). Rusting needs both air and water — the NCERT three-test-tube activity (from the Metals chapter) became 2024-A Q28, with a fourth tube added.

Reading a rusting figure (2024-A Q28)

Ask two questions of each tube: is there water? and is there air? Boiled distilled water under a layer of oil — water but no air (the boiling drove out the dissolved air, the oil keeps it out) → no rust. Anhydrous calcium chloride with a cotton plug — air but no water (the CaCl2 dries it) → no rust. Water open to the air → rust. Salt solution → rust, and faster — the dissolved ions speed up the reaction (Allen's fourth tube) "faster": beyond NCERT. The item's trick is a tube of boiled water without the oil: air dissolves back in, so it rusts.

Preventing rust is the Metals unit's business (painting, oiling, greasing, galvanising with zinc, chrome plating, anodising, alloying — Sample Q27's answer is Zn, the galvanising metal). Silver's black coat is NCERT's Ag2S; Sample Q28's "toothpaste" mechanism is not NCERT chemistry — if the item is recycled, the key credits the hydrogen-peroxide option (1).

Rancidity

Fats and oils are oxidised on standing; the food smells and tastes bad. Prevention: antioxidants added to fatty foods, airtight containers, refrigeration, and flushing packets with nitrogen — NCERT's chips-packet example. Rancidity is oxidation, so it is a chemical change; a question that calls it "physical" or "reduction" is wrong.

5Acids and bases — indicators and the six reactions

NCERT's chapter is built on a set of activities; Allen's items are those activities with one cell changed. Learn the observations as a table, then the six reaction shapes.

Definitions, NCERT's way

Acids taste sour, turn blue litmus red, and produce H+ ions in water (carried as hydronium, H3O+). Bases taste bitter, feel soapy, turn red litmus blue, and produce OH− ions in water. A base that dissolves in water is an alkali (NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)2); all alkalis are bases, not all bases are alkalis. An indicator changes colour (or smell — an olfactory indicator) in acid and in base.

Two neighbours from the Metals chapter that Allen files here

Amphoteric oxides (Al2O3, ZnO) react with both acids and bases to give salt + water: Al2O3 + 2NaOH → 2NaAlO2 + H2O (sodium aluminate) — so aluminium oxide with NaOH gives "salt and water", not hydrogen and not "no reaction" (2024-U Q20). And water itself is the non-metal oxide of 2018-S Q21: it acts as a proton donor and acceptor Brønsted view, beyond NCERT, reacts with acidic oxides (CO2 → carbonic acid) and basic oxides (CaO → slaked lime), oxidises red-hot iron as steam (3Fe + 4H2O → Fe3O4 + 4H2), and pure water is a poor conductor. SO2, NO2 and CO2 each fail at least one of the four clauses — CO2 does not react with acidic oxides, and none of the three is a proton donor and acceptor that reacts with both kinds of oxide.

What all acids have in common — and why dry gases do nothing

An acid is an acid only in water. HCl gas contains hydrogen, but it does not give H+ until water separates the ions: HCl + H2O → H3O+ + Cl−. So dry HCl gas leaves dry blue litmus unchanged, and moist litmus goes red — NCERT's guard-tube activity, and exactly the 2024-A Q20 pattern with SO2. Glucose and alcohol contain hydrogen too but do not conduct and are not acids: the bulb in NCERT's circuit lights for HCl and H2SO4 solutions, not for glucose or alcohol. Bases give OH− the same way: NaOH(s) → Na+(aq) + OH−(aq).

Dilution — the rule and its reason

Dissolving an acid or a base in water is highly exothermic. Always add the acid to the water, slowly, with stirring — never water to concentrated acid: the small amount of water boils at once and the mixture spatters out of the glass. Dilution lowers the concentration of H+ (or OH−) per unit volume — which is what section 6's pH arithmetic measures.

6pH — the scale, the numbers, and the arithmetic Allen adds

IGCSE reads pH off universal indicator paper. Allen expects you to calculate with it — four of the 34 items are pH numbers.

The scale

pH ("p" from the German potenz, power) measures hydrogen-ion concentration on a scale from 0 to 14: 7 is neutral, below 7 acidic, above 7 basic. The further from 7, the stronger the acid or base — pH 0 is strongly acidic, not "no acid" (2018-S Q22; Sample Q29's statement "pH 0 is neutral" is false). Universal indicator is a mixture of indicators that shows a different colour at each pH. Strength is how completely an acid ionises: strong acids (HCl, H2SO4, HNO3) give many H+, weak acids (acetic, citric, carbonic) few; NaOH and KOH are strong bases. Strength is not concentration — a dilute strong acid and a concentrated weak acid can have the same pH.

You only need the order and the side of 7; the exact decimals are Allen's, not NCERT's (whose figure gives lemon 2.2, water 7, blood 7.4, milk of magnesia 10, NaOH 14 — recalled from the textbook, no copy is cached here).

pH in everyday life — what NCERT lists

  • Our bodies work between pH 7.0 and 7.8; acid rain is rain below pH 5.6, and it makes river water too acidic for aquatic life.
  • Soil: plants grow only in a particular pH range; farmers add quicklime, slaked lime or chalk to acidic soil liming: beyond NCERT.
  • Stomach: HCl helps digestion; excess causes indigestion, cured by antacids — mild bases such as milk of magnesia, Mg(OH)2, or baking soda.
  • Tooth decay starts when mouth pH falls below 5.5 — bacteria make acid from sugar; toothpaste is basic.
  • Bee sting: an acid → rub with baking soda. Nettle sting: methanoic acid → dock leaf, which grows beside the nettle.
  • Natural acids: vinegar — acetic; orange, lemon — citric; tamarind — tartaric; tomato — oxalic; sour milk (curd) — lactic; ant sting — methanoic.

Beyond NCERT — the pH arithmetic (2018-S Q23, Sample Q29)

pH = −log10[H+]: pH 2 means [H+] = 10−2 mol/L, pH 3 means 10−3. So diluting an acid tenfold raises its pH by 1 (pH 2 → 3 on tenfold dilution, 2018-S Q23), a hundredfold by 2; diluting a base tenfold lowers its pH by 1 (12 → 11). Dilution never crosses 7 — an acid diluted a million times creeps towards 7 from below, it does not become a base. For bases, pOH = −log10[OH−] and pH + pOH = 14: [OH−] = 10−2 gives pOH 2, pH 12 (Sample Q29 statement iii is true). In 2018-S Q23 the distractors were 102, 210 and "remains constant".

pH of a salt solution

A salt is neutral, acidic or basic according to its parents:

ParentsSolutionExamples
strong acid + strong baseneutral, pH 7NaCl, KCl, Na2SO4, KNO3
strong acid + weak baseacidic, pH < 7NH4Cl, (NH4)2SO4, CuSO4
weak acid + strong basebasic, pH > 7Na2CO3, NaHCO3, CH3COONa, NaF

Beyond NCERT — acid salts and basic salts (Sample Q29, 2024-A Q23)

An acid salt still carries a replaceable hydrogen — NaHSO4, NaHCO3, NaH2PO4. A basic salt still carries a hydroxide — Pb(OH)Cl, Mg(OH)Cl, basic copper carbonate. So "Pb(OH)Cl is an acid salt" is false (Sample Q29). NaHSO4 is the exception to the family rule above: its extra H+ makes its solution acidic although both parents are strong. 2024-A Q23 (NaF, NaClO4, NaHSO4 — which are basic?) keys "NaF only", and the parent rule gets there directly: NaF is basic (the salt of the weak acid HF), NaClO4 is neutral (perchloric acid is strong, like HCl), NaHSO4 is acidic. Read the subscript — without the 4, hypochlorite NaClO would be basic and the answer would change.

7Salts — the family, and the tree that grows from common salt

Six of the 34 items are one line each off this tree. Draw it once from memory before the paper.

Family of salts

Salts with the same cation or the same anion form a family: NaCl, Na2SO4, NaNO3 are the sodium salts; NaCl, KCl, CaCl2 are the chlorides. Common salt NaCl (rock salt is the dry deposit of ancient seas; sea water is the other source) is the raw material for everything below.

① Chlor-alkali process — brine electrolysed

2NaCl(aq) + 2H2O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + Cl2(g) + H2(g) electricity · Cl2 at the anode, H2 at the cathode, NaOH formed near the cathode

The name: chlor for chlorine, alkali for sodium hydroxide. The three products (2017-E Q22 — the option with sodium metal or with oxygen is wrong) and their uses:

ProductUses (NCERT's list)
H2fuels, margarine, ammonia for fertilisers
Cl2water treatment, swimming pools, PVC, disinfectants, CFCs, pesticides; with H2 → HCl (cleaning steel, medicines, cosmetics, ammonium chloride); with dry slaked lime → bleaching powder
NaOHde-greasing metals, soaps and detergents, paper making, artificial fibres

② Bleaching powder CaOCl2

Ca(OH)2 + Cl2 → CaOCl2 + H2O chlorine on dry slaked lime

Uses: bleaching cotton and linen in textiles, wood pulp in paper, washed clothes in the laundry; an oxidising agent in chemical industries; disinfecting drinking water. (2024-U Q23's "Hasenclever's plant" is simply the industrial vessel for this reaction — coaching-module trivia; the chemistry you need is "chlorine + slaked lime".)

③ Baking soda NaHCO3 — sodium hydrogencarbonate

NaCl + H2O + CO2 + NH3 → NH4Cl + NaHCO3 CO2 into ammoniacal brine — the Solvay process by name beyond NCERT (2024-A Q21, 2024-U Q23); NaHCO3 is the less soluble one and separates

A mild, non-corrosive base. Baking powder = baking soda + a mild edible acid (tartaric acid); on heating or wetting, NaHCO3 + H+ → CO2 + H2O + sodium salt of the acid, and the CO2 makes bread and cake rise (the tartaric acid also mops up the Na2CO3 that would taste bitter). Uses: antacid; soda–acid fire extinguisher. On heating alone: 2NaHCO3 → Na2CO3 + H2O + CO2 — and that is where it stops: the sodium carbonate left behind does not decompose further in a burner flame, so heating the residue again gives no more CO2. Baking soda has no water of crystallisation — 2024-U Q22's answer against blue vitriol, green vitriol and washing soda, which all do.

④ Washing soda Na2CO3·10H2O

2NaHCO3 → Na2CO3 + H2O + CO2 heat
Na2CO3 + 10H2O → Na2CO3·10H2O recrystallisation

The salt of a strong base and a weak acid, so its solution is basic (not a "basic salt" in the hydroxide-carrying sense of section 6). Uses: glass, soap and paper industries; making borax; a household cleaning agent; removing the permanent hardness of water. 2024-A Q21 asks for Y when the Solvay product X is heated: X = NaHCO3, Y = Na2CO3.

⑤ Water of crystallisation — and plaster of Paris

Definition

Water of crystallisation is the fixed number of water molecules present in one formula unit of a salt: CuSO4·5H2O (blue vitriol, blue — white when heated, blue again with water), FeSO4·7H2O (green vitriol), Na2CO3·10H2O (washing soda), CaSO4·2H2O (gypsum). A hydrated salt is not "wet" — the crystals are dry to the touch.

CaSO4·2H2O → CaSO4·½H2O + 1½H2O gypsum heated at 373 K gives plaster of Paris, calcium sulphate hemihydrate — "half a molecule of water", i.e. two formula units share one H2O
CaSO4·½H2O + 1½H2O → CaSO4·2H2O POP + water sets to a hard solid mass of gypsum

Uses of POP: supporting fractured bones, toys, decorative materials, smoothing surfaces. Sample Q19 chains the whole story: H2SO4 + Ca(OH)2 gives calcium sulphate (A), which crystallises as gypsum (B), which at 100 °C gives plaster of Paris (C). 2018-S Q28 just wants gypsum's formula — CaSO4·2H2O, against the ·3H2O and anhydrous distractors.

Salt-tree traps

  • Gypsum ·2, POP ·½, blue vitriol ·5, green vitriol ·7, washing soda ·10, baking soda none. Allen tests the number, not the name.
  • 373 K, not 373 °C. Heat gypsum harder and it loses all its water (dead-burnt plaster, which does not set) beyond NCERT.
  • Bleaching powder comes from dry slaked lime Ca(OH)2, not from lime water and not from quicklime.
  • Chlorine is at the anode. Positive electrode, negative ion. Hydrogen — not sodium — at the cathode.
  • Solvay makes the hydrogencarbonate first; the carbonate needs a heating step; the decahydrate needs recrystallisation. A chain item can stop at any of the three.

9Five worked questions — at TALLENTEX pitch

Each one chains two of the machines above, the way the real items do. Try it under 90 seconds, then open the working — the key is revealed only when you do.

1Recognise from the colour, then count · ~60 seconds

A white crystalline solid P is heated strongly in a dry test tube. It crackles, brown fumes appear, and a yellow residue is left. If 4 mol of P decompose completely, the total number of moles of gas evolved is

15
28
310
420
Working

Step 1 — name it. White solid, brown fumes, yellow residue: the section-3 table has exactly one row with that triple — lead nitrate, PbO, NO2.

Step 2 — the balanced equation, gas coefficients only. 2Pb(NO3)2 → 2PbO + 4NO2 + O2: 2 mol of P give 4 + 1 = 5 mol of gas. So 4 mol of P give 10 mol.

Elimination. 5 is the gas from the equation's 2 mol, never scaled to the 4 mol asked. 8 counts the brown gas and forgets the oxygen — but a nitrate cannot balance without O2. 20 takes "5 mol of gas" as being per mole of P. The chain is name → equation → scale; the marks are lost at the third step.
2Multi-statement · four labels, four checks · ~90 seconds

Consider the statements:

  1. Na2SO4(aq) + BaCl2(aq) → BaSO4(s) + 2NaCl(aq) is both a double displacement and a precipitation reaction.
  2. CaO + H2O → Ca(OH)2 is a combination reaction and is endothermic.
  3. The electrolysis of acidified water is a decomposition reaction in which the gas collected at the cathode has twice the volume of the gas at the anode.
  4. In CuO + H2 → Cu + H2O, copper oxide is the reducing agent.

The correct statements are

1I and II only
2I and III only
3I, III and IV only
4II and IV only
Working

I — true. Two compounds swap partners (double displacement) and one product is insoluble (precipitation); both labels hold at once — the 2018-S Q25 rule.

II — false. Quicklime + water is a combination, but it is exothermic: NCERT's beaker gets hot. One wrong clause kills the statement.

III — true. Electrolytic decomposition; H2 at the cathode, O2 at the anode, 2 : 1 by volume.

IV — false. CuO gives its oxygen and is reduced — that makes it the oxidising agent. H2 is the reducing agent.

Elimination. Settle II first — "combination" is right, so a hurried reader ticks it; the word endothermic is the planted error, and it removes (1) and (4) together. Then IV's agent-naming trap separates (2) from (3). Three of the four options contain I, so I only removes (4): spend your seconds on II and IV, which decide the rest.
3Identify three liquids, then predict a test · ~90 seconds

Three colourless liquids P, Q and R are tested. P turns turmeric paper reddish brown. Q turns methyl orange red. R changes neither. Phenolphthalein stays colourless in Q and in R. A few drops of Q are then poured on a pinch of baking soda and the gas produced is bubbled through lime water. Which statement is correct?

1P is lime water, Q is sugar solution, R is vinegar; the gas turns lime water milky
2P is vinegar, Q is lime water, R is sugar solution; the gas is hydrogen
3P is sugar solution, Q is vinegar, R is lime water; the gas is carbon dioxide
4P is lime water, Q is vinegar, R is sugar solution; the gas turns lime water milky
Working

Step 1 — read the indicators. Turmeric goes reddish brown only in a base → P is the base (lime water). Methyl orange red → Q is an acid (vinegar). No change with either → R is neutral (sugar solution). Phenolphthalein colourless in Q and R is consistent (it is colourless in acid and in neutral) — it is there to tempt you into reading "colourless" as "neutral" and doubting Q.

Step 2 — the test. Acid + hydrogencarbonate → salt + H2O + CO2, which turns lime water milky. So: P lime water, Q vinegar, R sugar solution, gas CO2 → milky.

Elimination. (1) makes Q the sugar solution — but sugar solution does not fizz with baking soda, and it would not turn methyl orange red. (2) swaps P and Q and calls the gas hydrogen — hydrogen comes from acid + metal, not acid + carbonate. (3) has the gas right and the liquids wrong: sugar solution leaves turmeric yellow. Two of the four options are wrong on the first clause and one on the last — read every clause of every option.
4pH arithmetic, then colours · ~75 seconds · beyond NCERT

Solution X has a hydrogen-ion concentration 100 times that of solution Y. The pH of Y is 5. The pH of X, the colour of phenolphthalein in X, and the colour of methyl orange in X are, respectively,

13 · colourless · red
27 · colourless · orange
33 · pink · red
42.5 · colourless · red
Working

Step 1 — the number. pH 5 means [H+] = 10−5. A hundred times more is 10−3 → pH 3. More H+ means a lower pH; each factor of 10 is one unit.

Step 2 — the colours. pH 3 is acidic: phenolphthalein colourless, methyl orange red.

Elimination. (2) moved the pH the wrong way (5 + 2) and then chose neutral colours to match — internally consistent, which is what makes it dangerous. (3) has the number and gets phenolphthalein backwards (pink is base). (4) halves the pH because "100 = 10²" felt like "divide by 2"; pH is a logarithm, so 100× is subtract 2. Notice the colour clauses alone eliminate (2) and (3); the arithmetic is only needed to separate (1) from (4).
5The salt tree, four steps deep · ~90 seconds

A gas G produced at the anode in the chlor-alkali process is passed over dry slaked lime to give X. Separately, carbon dioxide is passed through brine saturated with ammonia to give a sparingly soluble salt Z; heating Z gives Y; recrystallising Y from water gives W. The number of water molecules per formula unit of W, and the formula of X, are

12 and CaOCl2
210 and CaCl2
310 and CaOCl2
40 and CaOCl2
Working

Branch 1. Anode gas of the chlor-alkali process = Cl2; chlorine + dry slaked lime = bleaching powder, CaOCl2 → X.

Branch 2. CO2 into ammoniacal brine = the Solvay step → Z = NaHCO3 (baking soda, the sparingly soluble one). Heat → Y = Na2CO3. Recrystallise → W = Na2CO3·10H2O, washing soda: 10 water molecules.

Elimination. (1) is gypsum's 2 — the wrong branch of the tree. (2) has the right number and the wrong product for chlorine + Ca(OH)2 (CaCl2 is what HCl would give). (4) stopped at baking soda, which has no water of crystallisation — the tree has three steps on that branch and the question walked all three. Only (2) is wrong on X; the water count decides between (1), (3) and (4), so the tree walk is the real work.
