Trigonometry

Official topic: Introduction to Trigonometry (Class 10). Most of this chapter is new to you — your IGCSE course teaches sin, cos, tan and the exact values, but it has no sec, cosec or cot and no identities at all. That missing half is precisely what TALLENTEX asks. Sections 1–4 follow the NCERT chapter; sections 5 and 6 are the two machines the real papers add; section 7 is a shortcut the chapter used to have. About a 40-minute read — once now and once the night before.

1The six ratios — three you know, three you don't

NCERT names the sides of a right-angled triangle relative to the angle you are looking at: the side opposite the angle, the side adjacent to it, and the hypotenuse (always the side facing the right angle). The usual Indian classroom shorthand, which this page follows, is P perpendicular (= opposite), B base (= adjacent), H hypotenuse. NCERT's own text never writes P, B, H, so expect to meet both. Get this labelling right and the rest is bookkeeping.

The three you already have — sine, cosine, tangent

sin A = P/H  ·  cos A = B/H  ·  tan A = P/B = sin A / cos A

Identical to the SOH-CAH-TOA you use in IGCSE — only the letters differ. In NCERT's words: sine of ∠A = side opposite to angle A ÷ hypotenuse, and so on. In its Exercise 8.1 Q1 the right angle is at B, AB = 24 and BC = 7, so AC = 25: sin A = 7/25 and cos A = 24/25, but at the other acute angle sin C = 24/25 and cos C = 7/25 — the swap in the picture above.

The three that are new to you — cosecant, secant, cotangent

Each is simply one of the first three turned upside down. There is nothing else to them.

cosec A = H/P = 1/sin A
Pairs with sin. Note the spelling: cosec, and it is the reciprocal of sin — not of cos, despite the "co".
sec A = H/B = 1/cos A
Pairs with cos. sec goes with cos — the one without the "co" in its name.
cot A = B/P = 1/tan A = cos A / sin A
Pairs with tan. Also equal to cos/sin, which is the form that turns up most often inside an expression.

The pairing trap — worth ten seconds of memorising

The obvious guess is that sec pairs with sin and cosec with cos, because the names look like they match. It is the other way round. The reliable rule:

Exactly one of the two names in a reciprocal pair carries "co". sin ↔ cosec. cos ↔ sec. tan ↔ cot. If you ever write a pair with two "co"s or none, it is wrong.

Four things NCERT says about a ratio

  • It is one symbol. “sin A” is short for “the sine of the angle A”. It is not sin × A, and “sin” on its own means nothing. (And cos A is the cosine — the cosecant is cosec A.)
  • It belongs to the angle, not to the triangle. Two right triangles with the same acute angle are similar, so their sides are in proportion and every ratio comes out the same — the picture below. So “sin A = 1/3” tells you only that opposite : hypotenuse = 1 : 3. The sides are k and 3k for some positive k, and the k always cancels.
  • Equal ratio, equal angle. For acute angles, if sin B = sin Q then ∠B = ∠Q (Example 2 — the two right triangles turn out to have all three pairs of sides in one ratio, so they are similar). Exercise 8.1 Q6 asks the same for cos, and the argument works for tan too: one value of a ratio fixes the shape of the triangle. This is what lets you write “sin R = 1/2, so R = 30°”.
  • Its size is limited — for sin, cos, sec and cosec only. The hypotenuse is the longest side, so sin A and cos A never exceed 1, and their reciprocals cosec A and sec A are never less than 1. tan A and cot A have no such limit: tan 60° = √3 is already more than 1. So “sec A = 12/5 for some angle A” is true, while “sin θ = 4/3 for some angle θ” and “tan A is always less than 1” are both false — three of the five true-or-false statements of Exercise 8.1 Q11; the other two are in the first bullet.

Where the words come from

Trigonometry is Greek: tri (three) + gon (sides) + metron (measure) — the study of the relationships between the sides and angles of a triangle. The earliest known work on it was recorded in Egypt and Babylon, and early astronomers used it to find the distances of the stars and planets. NCERT opens with three places a right triangle hides: a student looking up at the top of the Qutub Minar, a girl on a balcony looking down across a river, and a hot-air balloon watched from the ground — heights and distances found without measuring them. In this chapter the angle is acute, plus the two end values 0° and 90°; the ratios are extended to other angles in later classes.

Sine is Indian. Aryabhata's Aryabhatiyam (A.D. 500) used ardha-jya, “half-chord”, shortened to jya or jiva. The Arabic translation kept jiva; the Latin one turned it into sinus (“curve”), which became sine. The English astronomer Edmund Gunter (1581–1626) first wrote it as sin.

Cosine came from needing the sine of the complementary angle. Aryabhata called it kotijya; the name cosinus is also Gunter's, and Sir Jonas Moore first wrote cos in 1674.

How to rebuild the table in fifteen seconds

Write 0   1   2   3   4. Take the square root of each and halve it — that is the whole sin row:

√0/2 = 0  ·  √1/2 = 1/2  ·  √2/2 = 1/√2  ·  √3/2  ·  √4/2 = 1

The cos row is the same five numbers read backwards. The tan row is sin ÷ cos. The bottom three rows are the top three flipped — with one exception: cot 90° is 0 (it is cos 90° ÷ sin 90°), although tan 90° is not defined. You never memorise thirty numbers — you memorise five and a direction.

Where "not defined" bites

tan 90° and sec 90° are not defined because cos 90° = 0 and you cannot divide by zero. Likewise cot 0° and cosec 0°, because sin 0° = 0. Write "not defined" — never ∞, and never 0. An option offering "0" for tan 90° is there to catch exactly that.

Why 0° and 90° have values at all — and which way each ratio moves

No triangle has an angle of 0° or 90° at A, so NCERT defines these values by watching what the triangle does as it closes up (the picture below):

  • As A shrinks towards 0°, the opposite side shrinks to nothing and the adjacent side becomes almost the whole hypotenuse — so sin 0° = 0 and cos 0° = 1.
  • As A opens towards 90°, it is the adjacent side that vanishes — so sin 90° = 1 and cos 90° = 0.
  • The other four ratios at 0° and 90° then follow by dividing, and a division by 0 is “not defined”.

The same picture gives the direction of travel. As A goes from 0° to 90°, sin A rises from 0 to 1 and cos A falls from 1 to 0; tan A rises from 0 without limit. sin and cos are equal only at 45°. So “the value of sin θ increases as θ increases” is true, “the value of cos θ increases as θ increases” is false, and “sin θ = cos θ for all values of θ” is false. With “sin(A + B) = sin A + sin B” (false, below) and “cot A is not defined for A = 0°” (true), those are the five statements of Exercise 8.2 Q4.

sin is not a multiplier — you cannot split it, or pull a number out of it

sin(A + B) is not sin A + sin B. Test it: A = 60°, B = 30° gives sin 90° = 1 on the left and √3/2 + 1/2 ≈ 1.37 on the right. In the same way sin 2A is not 2 sin A: sin 60° = √3/2 but 2 sin 30° = 1. (Of 0°, 30°, 45° and 60°, the two agree only at A = 0°.)

NCERT's Exercise 8.2 Q2 turns this into three look-alike fractions, each to be worked out from the table. Two of them then have to be recognised as a ratio of a different angle:

2 tan 30° / (1 + tan²30°) = (2/√3) ÷ (4/3) = √3/2 = sin 60°

2 tan 30° / (1 − tan²30°) = (2/√3) ÷ (2/3) = √3 = tan 60°

The third, (1 − tan²45°) / (1 + tan²45°), is the same routine with tan 45° = 1: 0 ÷ 2 = 0, a plain number. One sign in the denominator separates the first two — read it before you pick an option.

Evaluating an expression that leaves a surd underneath

Substitute from the table, simplify, then clear the surd from the denominator — that is the form in which NCERT prints its answers to Exercise 8.2. Its Q1(iii):

cos 45° / (sec 30° + cosec 30°) = (1/√2) ÷ (2/√3 + 2) = √3 / [2√2 (√3 + 1)]

= √3 (√3 − 1) / (2√2 × 2) = (3 − √3)/(4√2) = (3√2 − √6)/8

Two steps clear the denominator: multiply top and bottom by the conjugate (√3 − 1), which turns the bracket into 3 − 1 = 2, and then by √2. If your answer is not among the options, it is usually the same number with the denominator not yet cleared.

3The three identities — this is the new half of the chapter

Your IGCSE syllabus contains no trigonometric identities at all. These three lines are the single most valuable thing on this page: seven of the eight real questions in the table at the top of the page are solved with them (the eighth, 2018 Q56, needs only the exact values).

All three are Pythagoras in disguise. Start with p² + b² = h² and divide the whole line by the square of each of the three sides in turn:

Divide p² + b² = h² by…You getWhich readsTrue for
h²(p/h)² + (b/h)² = 1sin²A + cos²A = 10° ≤ A ≤ 90°
b²(p/b)² + 1 = (h/b)²1 + tan²A = sec²A0° ≤ A < 90°
p²1 + (b/p)² = (h/p)²1 + cot²A = cosec²A0° < A ≤ 90°

So there is really only one identity, met three times. If you ever blank, write Pythagoras down and divide.

An identity is an equation that is true for every value of the angle, not just for some. The last column is NCERT's small print: the second line has no meaning at 90°, where tan and sec are not defined, and the third has none at 0°, where cot and cosec are not.

The rearranged forms — memorise these, not the originals

The paper almost never hands you an identity the right way up. It hands you a fragment and expects you to recognise it. These are the forms that actually appear:

sec²A − tan²A = 1
So sec²A − 1 = tan²A and tan²A + 1 = sec²A. Sample Q52 is nothing but spotting sec²A − 1 and writing tan²A.
cosec²A − cot²A = 1
So cosec²A − 1 = cot²A. The same move, one row down.
sin²A = 1 − cos²A
And cos²A = 1 − sin²A. This is how you turn a mixed equation into one containing a single ratio — see section 5.

The conjugate trick — the highest-value line on this page beyond NCERT

Because sec²A − tan²A = 1 is a difference of two squares, it factorises:

(sec A + tan A)(sec A − tan A) = 1

Which means sec A + tan A and sec A − tan A are reciprocals of each other. Hand one to a student and they have the other for free. The same holds one row down:

(cosec A + cot A)(cosec A − cot A) = 1

Whenever a question gives you sec θ + tan θ = k, immediately write sec θ − tan θ = 1/k. Adding the two lines gives you 2 sec θ; subtracting them gives you 2 tan θ. Two lines of work and the question is finished — worked example 3 does exactly this.

Notation: sin²A means (sin A)², not sin(A²)

sin²A = sin A × sin A. It is written with the square on the "sin" purely to avoid a forest of brackets. sin(A²) is a completely different and entirely useless object — it will never appear. Similarly sin⁻¹A is not 1/sin A — that is cosec A. It means "the angle whose sine is A", so sin⁻¹(1/2) = 30°. (NCERT says only that sin⁻¹ has “a different meaning”; you know which from IGCSE.)

Given the sum of a reciprocal pair beyond NCERT

Wherever both are defined, sin θ × cosec θ = 1, cos θ × sec θ = 1 and tan θ × cot θ = 1. So when a sum or a difference of a pair is squared, the middle term is just a number — +2 for a sum, −2 for a difference:

(sin θ + cosec θ)² = sin²θ + cosec²θ + 2  →  if sin θ + cosec θ = 5/2, then sin²θ + cosec²θ = 25/4 − 2 = 17/4

The expansion itself is NCERT's — Exercise 8.3 Q4(viii) squares sin A + cosec A. What goes beyond the chapter is being handed the value of the sum. The same line works for cos with sec and for tan with cot.

The MCQ shortcut — test one angle beyond NCERT

An identity is true for every angle, so when an MCQ asks what an expression “equals”, put in one easy angle and see which option agrees. Take (1 + tan θ + sec θ)(1 + cot θ − cosec θ) at θ = 45°:

(1 + 1 + √2)(1 + 1 − √2) = (2 + √2)(2 − √2) = 4 − 2 = 2

Three cautions. Pick an angle at which every ratio in the expression is defined and no denominator becomes 0. If two options give the same number at your angle, try a second one — 30° or 60° — before you choose. And it works only for an identity: if the stem gives an equation the angle has to satisfy, the angle is no longer yours to choose.

4Technique 1 — one ratio given, all six wanted

The commonest opening move in the whole chapter. You are told one ratio and asked for an expression built from several. Do not reach for an identity. Draw the triangle.

Suppose you are told sec θ = 13/12 — the given of NCERT's Exercise 8.1 Q5 — and asked for tan θ + cot θ.

From the triangle: tan θ = 5/12 and cot θ = 12/5, so

tan θ + cot θ = 5/12 + 12/5 = 25/60 + 144/60 = 169/60

NCERT's Example 1 (tan A = 4/3) and its Exercise 8.1 Q4 (15 cot A = 8) run exactly the same way.

Learn five Pythagorean triples by sight

Examiners reuse them endlessly because they keep the arithmetic calculator-free — NCERT's own examples and exercises in this chapter use all five:

3, 4, 5  ·  5, 12, 13  ·  8, 15, 17  ·  7, 24, 25  ·  20, 21, 29

and their multiples (6-8-10, 9-12-15, 10-24-26 …); beyond NCERT's five, 9-40-41 and 12-35-37 are two more worth knowing. If a question hands you 5/13 or 8/17, the missing side is already known to you — that is a free five seconds.

The identity route, for when the triangle is awkward

The same answer falls out algebraically, and it is worth seeing because the paper sometimes wants the general form rather than a number:

tan θ + cot θ = sin θ/cos θ + cos θ/sin θ = (sin²θ + cos²θ)/(sin θ cos θ) = 1/(sin θ cos θ)

Check it with the triangle's sides: sin θ cos θ = (P × B)/H² = 60/169, so 1/(sin θ cos θ) = 169/60 ✓ — the same number by a different road. Two routes agreeing is the cheapest error-check you own; use it whenever you have thirty spare seconds.

When the third side is not a whole number

Most ratios do not come from a triple, and nothing changes — the third side is simply left as a surd. With sin A = 3/4: opposite 3, hypotenuse 4, so the adjacent side is √(16 − 9) = √7, and cos A = √7/4, tan A = 3/√7 (Exercise 8.1 Q3). NCERT's text does the same with sin A = 1/3 before its first example. Its Example 4 is the other common surd: tan A = 1 makes the two legs equal, k and k, so the hypotenuse is k√2 and sin A = cos A = 1/√2 — which is why 2 sin A cos A = 1.

One side, and the sum or difference of the other two — NCERT Example 5, Exercise 8.1 Q10

Pythagoras is a difference of two squares: H² − P² = B², so (H − P)(H + P) = B². Given B and one of the brackets, the other bracket is one division away.

  • Example 5. One side is 7 cm, and hypotenuse − other side = 1 cm. Then hypotenuse + other side = 49 ÷ 1 = 49, so the two are 25 and 24. The angle opposite the 7 has sine 7/25 and cosine 24/25.
  • Exercise 8.1 Q10. One side is 5 cm, and hypotenuse + other side = 25 cm. Then hypotenuse − other side = 25 ÷ 25 = 1, so they are 13 and 12.

The same factorisation finds a missing side quickly — with hypotenuse 29 and one side 21 the other is √(8 × 50) = √400 = 20 (Example 3) — and it finishes a difference of squared ratios: cos²θ − sin²θ = (21² − 20²)/29² = 41/841.

The two acute angles of one triangle — the ratio you were given may not be needed

In a right-angled triangle the two acute angles A and C use the same three sides with “opposite” and “adjacent” swapped, so sin C = cos A and cos C = sin A. Exercise 8.1 Q9 gives tan A = 1/√3 and asks for sin A cos C + cos A sin C. Swap the C-ratios and it is sin²A + cos²A = 1; likewise cos A cos C − sin A sin C = cos A sin A − sin A cos A = 0. Neither answer depends on the tan A you were handed. Exercise 8.1 Q2 is the same idea: in its figure (right angle at Q, PQ = 12 cm, PR = 13 cm) tan P and cot R are the same two sides, QR over PQ, so tan P − cot R = 0.

5Technique 2 — a mixed equation becomes a quadratic

When the given equation contains both sin and cos, and one of them is squared, you can always force it into a quadratic in a single ratio. This is the engine behind 2024 code A, Q56. The chapter supplies the identity but sets no question of this kind. beyond NCERT

Worked: if 2 sin²θ = 3 cos θ and θ is acute, find cos θ.

  1. Pick the survivor. The unsquared ratio is cos, so aim for an equation in cos alone.
  2. Replace the squared one using sin²θ = 1 − cos²θ:
    2(1 − cos²θ) = 3 cos θ
  3. Tidy into standard form. Write c for cos θ:
    2 − 2c² = 3c  →  2c² + 3c − 2 = 0
  4. Factorise: (2c − 1)(c + 2) = 0  →  c = 1/2 or c = −2
  5. Throw out the impossible root. A cosine can never be less than −1, so c = −2 is rejected. cos θ = 1/2, i.e. θ = 60°.

Step 5 is not optional, and it is where the marks go

A quadratic hands you two roots and the paper will always list the rejected one as an option. sin θ and cos θ live between −1 and 1; for an acute angle they live between 0 and 1. Anything outside that is not an answer, it is a trap. Check the range before you look at the options, not after — once you have seen your number printed as option (4) it is very hard to talk yourself out of it.

(tan and cot have no such limit — they run over every real number. sec and cosec are the opposite way round: they can never lie strictly between −1 and 1.)

Same machine, uglier numbers

2024 code A, Q56 gives you 8 tan θ = 3 cos θ. Write tan as sin/cos, multiply up, replace cos²θ by 1 − sin²θ, and you have a quadratic in sin θ — then all six ratios follow from the triangle, exactly as in section 4. But read its demand to the end: it does not want E, it wants E written as (a + b√c)/d and then the value of a + b + c + d. Working E out and bubbling it scores −1, because E itself is not one of the options. You will meet that question in a mock; the method above is all you need to beat it.

6Technique 3 — the expression is the identity

The hardest-looking trigonometry in these papers is also the most mechanical once you see the shape. You are given a monstrous expression and asked what it equals. The move is always the same: factor out, then use sin² + cos² = 1 to collapse a bracket to 1. beyond NCERT

Worked — the official sample paper, Q58. If Tn = sinⁿθ + cosⁿθ, find (T₃ − T₅) / T₁.

Write the numerator out and group the sines with the sines:

T₃ − T₅ = (sin³θ + cos³θ) − (sin⁵θ + cos⁵θ) = sin³θ(1 − sin²θ) + cos³θ(1 − cos²θ)

Now each bracket collapses — 1 − sin²θ = cos²θ and 1 − cos²θ = sin²θ:

= sin³θ·cos²θ + cos³θ·sin²θ = sin²θ cos²θ (sin θ + cos θ) = sin²θ cos²θ · T₁

So (T₃ − T₅)/T₁ = sin²θ cos²θ. The question asks which of the listed expressions equals it — and (T₅ − T₇)/T₃ collapses to exactly the same thing by exactly the same two lines. Official key: option (1).

The pattern to carry into the exam

Any time you see a difference of two powers of sin and cos, the lower power factors out and what is left is 1 − (something)², which is the other ratio squared. You are never really doing trigonometry — you are doing algebra with sin²θ + cos²θ = 1 as the only fact.

2024 code U, Q56 is the same idea run backwards: a given cubic in sin θ is rearranged into a cubic in cos²θ, and the expression you are asked for turns out to be that very cubic. You will meet it in a mock.

7Complementary angles — a free shortcut

Two acute angles of a right triangle add to 90°. Stand at one, then the other, and P and B swap — which is the picture at the very top of this page. Every complementary rule is just that swap written down.

cut from NCERT This was a section of the chapter until NCERT removed it in its 2022 rationalisation, so today's book does not teach it. It stays here because it is one line away from the definitions and because NCERT's own history box still says the cosine began as the sine of the complementary angle. None of the eight real questions at the top needed it — treat it as a shortcut, not a priority.

sin(90° − A) = cos A
and cos(90° − A) = sin A
tan(90° − A) = cot A
and cot(90° − A) = tan A
sec(90° − A) = cosec A
and cosec(90° − A) = sec A

The rule in words: the angle turns into its complement and the name swaps with its "co" partner. So sin 52° = cos 38°, because 52 + 38 = 90.

What this is for

It turns a fraction that looks impossible without a calculator into the number 1:

sin 25° / cos 65° = sin 25° / sin 25° = 1

Whenever you see two angles in one expression that add to 90, this is why they are there. Scan for that sum first — it is usually the entire question.

Three more shapes the same swap takes

  • As an equation. If sin x = cos y — or tan x = cot y, or sec x = cosec y — and both angles are acute, then x + y = 90°. So sin 3A = cos(A − 26°) means 3A + A − 26° = 90°, and A = 29°.
  • As a product. tan A × tan(90° − A) = tan A × cot A = 1. So tan 15° × tan 75° = 1, and a chain such as tan 5° · tan 25° · tan 45° · tan 65° · tan 85° pairs off (5 with 85, 25 with 65) and leaves tan 45° = 1.
  • As a sum of squares. sin²A + sin²(90° − A) = sin²A + cos²A = 1. So sin²25° + sin²65° = 1, although neither term can be worked out by hand.

Three errors this chapter punishes hardest

  • Answering the wrong ratio. You correctly find θ = 30° and then read off sin instead of cot. Underline what is being asked before you start, and check it again before you bubble — this is the single most expensive slip in the section.
  • (sin A + cos A)² is not sin²A + cos²A. It is sin²A + cos²A + 2 sin A cos A = 1 + 2 sin A cos A. The cross term is deliberately left out of one option in nearly every paper that asks this.
  • Flipping a reciprocal one time too many or too few. If you want cot and you have tan = 3/4, cot is 4/3 — not 3/4, and not −3/4. Say the word "flip" out loud as you do it.

Working

40 cot A = 9 → cot A = 9/40. cot is B/P, so B = 9, P = 40, and H² = 9² + 40² = 1681, so H = 41 — the 9-40-41 triple.

Only the sine is needed: sin A = P/H = 40/41.

(1 + 40/41) / (1 − 40/41) = (81/41) / (1/41) = 81 — the 41s cancel, which is why the answer is a clean number and why you should never convert to decimals.

Where each wrong option comes from. (2) 25/16 — reading cot A = 9/40 as tan A = 9/40. That swaps P and B, giving sin A = 9/41, and (50/41) / (32/41) = 25/16. (1) 1/81 — the right sine, but the final fraction written upside down. (3) 9 — this is sec A + tan A = 41/9 + 40/9. Elimination: for an acute angle 1 + sin A is more than 1 and 1 − sin A is less than 1, so the fraction must be bigger than 1 — (1) dies before you compute a single thing. And cot A = 9/40 is far below 1, so A is a large angle with sin A close to 1: the denominator is tiny and the answer is large.

3The conjugate trick · ~80 seconds

Working

Because sec²θ − tan²θ = 1, the conjugate is the reciprocal: sec θ − tan θ = 3/4.

Add the two equations: 2 sec θ = 4/3 + 3/4 = 25/12 → sec θ = 25/24 → cos θ = 24/25.

Subtract them: 2 tan θ = 4/3 − 3/4 = 7/12 → tan θ = 7/24.

sin θ = tan θ × cos θ = (7/24)(24/25) = 7/25. Check: sec θ + tan θ = 25/24 + 7/24 = 32/24 = 4/3 ✓

(Faster still: sec θ = 25/24 means H = 25, B = 24, so P = 7 — the 7-24-25 triangle — and sin θ = 7/25 straight off.)

Where each wrong option comes from. Every wrong option is a real number you produce on the way: (4) 25/24 is sec θ, (2) 7/24 is tan θ, (3) 24/25 is cos θ. Not one of them is filler. That is the designed trap — the working is easy and the only difficulty is remembering which of four numbers on your page the question actually wanted. Underline the word "sin" in the stem before you start. Elimination: a sine can never exceed 1, so (4) 25/24 is impossible however naturally the working produces it. And sec θ + tan θ = 4/3 is only a little more than 1, so θ is a small angle: its sine is small and its cosine close to 1, which removes (3). Between the two that are left, sin θ = tan θ × cos θ is always the smaller for an acute angle — 7/25, not 7/24.

Working

Section 5's machine again, but the constants are different and there is a stray − 1 on the right, so nothing can be copied across — you have to build the quadratic yourself.

The unsquared ratio is sin, so aim for sin. Replace cos²θ = 1 − sin²θ, and write s for sin θ:

5(1 − s²) = 7s − 1 → 5 − 5s² = 7s − 1 → 5s² + 7s − 6 = 0

Factorise: (5s − 3)(s + 2) = 0, so s = 3/5 or s = −2. Reject −2: a sine can never be below −1. Hence sin θ = 3/5.

P = 3, H = 5, so B = 4 — the 3-4-5 triangle — and cot θ = B/P = 4/3.

Where each wrong option comes from. All three belong to a student who did the hard part perfectly and then read the wrong ratio off the triangle: (1) 3/4 is tan θ — the reciprocal flipped the wrong way; (2) 4/5 is cos θ; (4) 5/3 is cosec θ. The algebra is not what this question tests. Finding sin θ is the first half of the work, not the end of it — and in a paper marked +4/−1, stopping early costs you 5 marks against the student who did not. Elimination: sin θ = 3/5 puts θ below 45°, where tan θ < 1 and so cot θ > 1. That strikes (1) and (2) on sight, leaving a two-way choice worth +1.5 marks on average even if the last step deserts you.
