Gravitation, Work & Energy

Official topics: Gravitation and Work, Power and Energy (both Class 9) — two topics, one card. Your IGCSE course has weight and g, free fall, density, pressure, work, kinetic and potential energy, power and efficiency (0625 sections 1.2–1.8). What it does not have — and Allen asks — is the universal law of gravitation and how g changes with height, depth and latitude; buoyancy, Archimedes' principle and relative density; and the NCERT framing of energy items (kinetic energy at the top of a throw, mechanical energy on a frictionless track, work by a variable force as an area). A 30-minute read; the paper is the point.

1The universal law of gravitation

The one thing in this unit your IGCSE course never states. Learn the sentence, the formula, and the three scaling moves — every real gravitation item is one of them.

NCERT statement

Every object in the universe attracts every other object with a force which is proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. The force is along the line joining the centres of the two objects.

F = G m₁m₂ / d²

G is the universal gravitational constant, 6.673 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻² (NCERT uses 6.7 × 10⁻¹¹ in its worked examples). Its unit comes from G = F d² / (m₁m₂). "Universal" means the same G everywhere — between two marbles, between you and the Earth, between the Earth and the Sun. The medium between the bodies makes no difference: a sheet of glass or a vacuum in the gap changes nothing.

Why it matters (NCERT's list — Allen asks it as a statement item)

  • the force that binds us to the Earth;
  • the motion of the Moon around the Earth;
  • the motion of the planets around the Sun;
  • the tides due to the Moon and the Sun.

Two 1 kg masses 1 m apart attract with only 6.7 × 10⁻¹¹ N — which is why you never feel the pull of the person next to you. Replace one mass by the Earth (6 × 10²⁴ kg) at its radius (6.4 × 10⁶ m) and the force on 1 kg becomes 9.8 N. Same law, one enormous mass.

The four-statement item (2024-U Q7, 2018-S Q11)

Allen lists true statements and asks for the false one, or true properties and asks what the force does not depend on. The true list: always attractive, never repulsive · acts along the line joining the centres · obeys the inverse-square law · depends on the product of the masses (never the sum, never the difference) · independent of the medium · the two forces form an action–reaction pair. In 2024-U Q7 three options were true and "does not obey the inverse-square law" was the odd one out; in 2018-S Q11 "sum of masses" was the one thing on the list the force ignores.

2g — where it comes from, and how it changes

Put the Earth into the law and the mass of the falling body cancels. That one line explains why everything falls together, and why g changes with where you stand.

Acceleration due to gravity

For a body of mass m at the Earth's surface, F = G M m / R² and F = m g, so g = G M / R² — the m has gone. With NCERT's numbers, g = 6.7 × 10⁻¹¹ × 6 × 10²⁴ / (6.4 × 10⁶)² = 9.8 m/s². Allen's items use g = 10 m/s² unless the stem says 9.8.

Free fall is motion under gravity alone. All bodies, whatever their mass, fall with the same g (Sample Q11: the 20 kg iron sphere and the 12 kg aluminium sphere share the same acceleration and the same velocity at every height — but not the same kinetic energy or momentum). Air resistance is what makes a feather lag a coin; in a vacuum they land together.

Where g is bigger and smaller

  • Height: smaller. g at height h is G M / (R + h)². For a mountain the change is tiny (Everest is 0.14% of R); for a satellite it is not.
  • Depth: smaller, falling to zero at the centre — where you are pulled equally in every direction. (Allen's module: gd = g (1 − d/R); beyond NCERT, but the direction "decreases" is what 2024-U Q11 tested.)
  • Latitude: the Earth is not a perfect sphere — its radius increases from the poles to the equator — so g is greater at the poles than at the equator (NCERT's sentence). A spring balance reads a little more at the pole; a beam balance reads the same everywhere.
  • Another planet: g′ / g = (M′/M) × (R/R′)². Moon: M = 7.36 × 10²² kg, R = 1.74 × 10⁶ m → gmoon ≈ 1.63 m/s², one-sixth of Earth's. A planet with half the Earth's mass and half its radius has g′ = ½ × 4 = 2g.

Free-fall arithmetic — the equations of motion with a = g

t = √(2h/g)
Fall time from rest. t ∝ √h — from 2h takes √2 as long (2018-S Q2, key 1 : √2, not 1 : 2).
v = √(2gh)
Speed after falling h, any mass. Thrown up at u: rises h = u²/2g, and comes back at u.
up: a = −g
Take up as +. At the top v = 0 (not a = 0). Time up = u/g; total flight 2u/g on level ground.
on planet P: g′ = g · (M′/M)(R/R′)²
Then re-run t, v, h with g′. The subject paper's Q9 is exactly this chain.

Orbit items belong here too

The Moon stays in orbit because gravity supplies the centripetal force — always toward the Earth, always at right angles to the velocity, which is along the tangent (2024-A Q7). Switch gravity off and the Moon carries on in a straight line along the tangent at the same speed (2017-E Q11) — not "towards the Earth", not "stationary", not "the same orbit". Because F ⟂ v, gravity does no work on an orbiting body, which is why its speed in a circular orbit never changes.

3Mass and weight

NCERT definitions

  • Mass is the measure of a body's inertia. It is the same everywhere — on the Earth, on the Moon, in a spacecraft. Scalar, kilogram, measured on a beam balance.
  • Weight is the force with which the Earth attracts the body: W = m g. It acts vertically downward, is measured in newtons on a spring balance, and changes with g.
  • Weight on the Moon is about one-sixth of the weight on Earth, because g there is one-sixth. The mass is unchanged.

A 60 kg student weighs 600 N on Earth (g = 10) and 100 N on the Moon; she is 60 kg in both places. Sample Q13 runs the machine backwards: a rock weighing 200.6 N on the Moon has mass 200.6 / 1.7 = 118 kg — and its mass on Earth is the same 118 kg. The distractors are 200.6/9.8 ≈ 20 (used Earth's g), 200 (the weight number read as a mass) and 200.6 × 10/1.7 = 1180 (the weight converted to Earth with g = 10 instead of finding the mass).

Words to read twice

"Weight in kg" on a shop scale is mass. "Weightless" in orbit means the body and its support fall together — gravity is very much still acting. "The same weight on the Moon" is false; "the same mass" is true. A statement that the mass of a body on the Moon is one-sixth is a planted error.

4Thrust, pressure, buoyancy — the half Allen has not asked yet

These sit in the Gravitation chapter in NCERT and on the official topic list, and the hub's Physics subject paper asks them, but none of the five cached papers ever did. Learn them in one pass; do not over-invest.

Thrust and pressure

  • Thrust is the force acting on an object perpendicular to its surface. Unit: newton.
  • Pressure is thrust per unit area: P = F / A. SI unit pascal, 1 Pa = 1 N/m². The same thrust on a smaller area gives a larger pressure — a sharp knife, a nail's point, a camel's wide feet in reverse.
  • Pressure in fluids (liquids and gases): a fluid exerts pressure on the base and walls of its container, and pressure applied to a confined fluid is transmitted undiminished in all directions. At depth h in a liquid of density ρ the extra pressure is h ρ g (from your IGCSE; NCERT Class 9 states the idea, not the formula): 10 m of water adds 10 × 1000 × 10 = 10⁵ Pa, about one atmosphere.

Buoyancy and Archimedes' principle

  • Buoyancy is the upward force a fluid exerts on a body placed in it — the upthrust (buoyant force). It is why a bucket feels lighter until it clears the water and why a cork pushed under bobs back up.
  • Archimedes' principle: when a body is immersed fully or partially in a fluid, it experiences an upward force equal to the weight of the fluid displaced by it: upthrust = Vdisplaced × ρfluid × g.
  • Float or sink is decided by density: denser than the liquid → sinks; less dense → floats with part of it above the surface; equal → hangs anywhere in the liquid.
  • Density = mass / volume (kg/m³; water 1000 kg/m³ = 1 g/cm³). Relative density = density of the substance / density of water — a pure ratio, no unit. Iron RD 7.8; ice 0.92; petrol about 0.7. Allen and the hub's subject paper use it; keep it whether or not your NCERT print carries the section.

5Work — a force and a displacement, with a sign

NCERT's definition is narrow on purpose, and the marks are in the sign and the zero cases.

NCERT definition

Work done on an object = force × displacement of the object in the direction of the force: W = F s. Two conditions must both hold — a force must act, and the object must be displaced. If either is missing the work is zero, however tired you are. Unit: joule, 1 J = 1 N m — the work done when a force of 1 N moves an object through 1 m along the force. Scalar.

  • Positive work: force along the displacement (2024-U Q15: Minakshee pushes the block from A to B along the table; her push and the motion are the same way — positive, and nothing in the figure says otherwise).
  • Negative work: force opposite to the displacement — friction on a sliding block; gravity on a ball moving up; the retarding force of a brake. W = −F s.
  • Zero work: displacement zero (pushing a wall, holding a bag still) — or force at right angles to the displacement (the standard examples, not NCERT's own): the load on a porter's head as he walks along a level platform; gravity on a satellite; the centripetal force in any circular motion.

Work by a force that changes — the area rule (2017-E Q10)

When F varies with position, work = area under the F–x graph, counting area below the axis as negative — exactly as area under F–t is change of momentum. 2017-E Q10 (a triangle then three rectangles) gave +30 − 20 + 20 − 10 = +20 J, so the kinetic energy rose from 25 J to 45 J. Worked example 4 below runs the same machine. Beyond NCERT's text; Allen has asked it.

6Energy — the conservation ladder

Kinetic and potential energy, and the one rule that answers four of the twenty real items.

NCERT definitions

  • Energy is the capacity to do work; same unit, the joule. 1 kJ = 1000 J.
  • Kinetic energy — the energy possessed by an object due to its motion: KE = ½ m v². NCERT derives it from the work done to bring a body from rest to v (F s = m a s = ½ m v² using v² = 2as). Never negative; doubles v → four times the KE.
  • Potential energy — the energy possessed by an object because of its position or configuration. Gravitational PE at height h: PE = m g h, the work done to lift it. It depends on the reference level you choose, and on the height only, not the path taken to get there.
  • Law of conservation of energy: energy can only be converted from one form to another; it can neither be created nor destroyed. The total energy before and after a transformation remains the same.

The four real energy items, one machine

  • KE at the highest point of a vertical throw is zero — the body is momentarily at rest (2024-U Q4; the 15 kg and 40 m/s are decoration). Only for a vertical throw: a projectile keeps its horizontal speed at the top — but projectiles are not on the 2027 list.
  • PE now = mghstart − KE now when released from rest (2024-A Q4).
  • Total mechanical energy is constant along a frictionless track, whatever its loops and curves (2024-A Q15) — it does not "decrease going down and increase going up"; that describes PE alone.
  • Name the energies (Sample Q9): four columns — at A (on the tee), from A to B (in flight), from B to C (coming down and rolling), at D (stopped in the cup). The rule Allen's key applies: at rest → PE only; moving → KE and PE. Two options had KE + PE in both middle columns; what separated them was the first cell — a ball at rest on the tee has no KE, so "only KE at A" is the planted error. The other two options put "only PE" or "only KE" on the moving intervals.

When friction is present — the work–energy account (2018-S Q10)

With air resistance or friction, mechanical energy is not conserved; the missing part is the work done by the resisting force, which is negative. Bookkeeping: KE gained = work by gravity + work by friction. 2018-S Q10: dropped from H it should arrive at √(2gH); it arrives at 1.2√(gH), so KE = ½m(1.44 gH) = 0.72 mgH against the mgH gravity supplied. Work by air friction = 0.72 mgH − mgH = −0.28 mgH. The planted options: +0.28 (sign dropped), ±0.14 (half the true loss). In Allen's language this is the work–energy theorem: net work on a body = its change in kinetic energy. NCERT uses the idea to derive ½mv² but never names it.

Bulb: electrical → light + heat. Dynamo: mechanical → electrical. Plant: light → chemical. Muscle: chemical → mechanical + heat.

7Power — the rate, and the ratio items

NCERT definition

Power is the rate of doing work or of transfer of energy: P = W / t. Unit watt, 1 W = 1 J/s; 1 kW = 1000 W. Average power = total energy consumed ÷ total time. Two agents doing the same work in different times have different powers — the faster one is more powerful.

Commercial unit: the kilowatt-hour — the energy used by 1 kW running for 1 hour: 1 kWh = 1000 W × 3600 s = 3.6 × 10⁶ J, the "unit" on an electricity bill. In the NCERT text; no cached paper has asked it — one line is enough.

The machines Allen actually uses

  • Climbing: P = m g h / t. A 50 kg student up 3 m of stairs in 5 s: 50 × 10 × 3 / 5 = 300 W. Efficiency of the body is not asked — the useful work is the lift.
  • Ratio of two climbers: same stairs, so P₁/P₂ = (W₁/W₂) × (t₂/t₁) — weights the right way up, times upside down. 2018-S Q15 ≡ Sample Q15: weights 5 : 3, times 11 : 9 → (5/3) × (9/11) = 15/11. The planted 11/15 is the ratio inverted; 11/9 and 9/11 forget the weights.
  • A flow of water (dam, pump, waterfall): P = (mass per second) × g × h, then multiply by the efficiency for useful output, or divide by it for the input drawn. 2017-E Q15: 2000 kg/s, height difference 550 − 50 = 500 m: 2000 × 10 × 500 = 10⁷ W = 10 MW; × 0.8 = 8 MW. Using 550 m gives 8.8 MW, which is not an option — the sign to recheck the height; forgetting the efficiency gives 10 MW, which is.
  • Efficiency = useful output / input (× 100 for a percentage) — never above 100%. A 40% efficient motor drawing 500 W delivers 200 W of lifting; to get 200 W useful from it you must supply 200/0.4 = 500 W. Decide which way the question runs before you touch the numbers.
  • P = F v for a force moving something at steady speed v (beyond NCERT; Allen's module) — a car's engine power against a constant resisting force.

1Sample Q14's machine, carried one step on · ~90 seconds

Two solid lead spheres of radii r and 2r are placed touching each other, and the gravitational force between them is F. Two more lead spheres are now made with each radius doubled (2r and 4r) and placed touching. The force between the new pair is

18F
216F
332F
464F
Working

Step 1 — masses. Same material, so m ∝ r³. Doubling a radius multiplies its mass by 2³ = 8. Both spheres are doubled, so the product m₁m₂ becomes 8 × 8 = 64 times bigger.

Step 2 — distance. Touching spheres have their centres r + 2r = 3r apart; the new pair 2r + 4r = 6r — the distance has doubled, so d² is 4 times bigger.

Step 3 — F′ = 64F / 4 = 16F. Option (2).

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) 8F — only one sphere's mass scaled (× 8) and the distance ignored. (3) 32F — masses right (× 64) but the doubled distance divided in once, not squared. (4) 64F — the distance forgotten altogether. Elimination. The distance grows, so the answer must be less than the mass factor alone — (4) is out at once; anyone who remembers "distance squared" is choosing between (2) and (1), and (1) needs a mass to have stayed the same, which the stem rules out.

Step 1 — g on the planet. g′ = g × (M′/M) × (R/R′)² = 10 × 2 × (½)² = 10 × 2/4 = 5 m/s². Twice the mass pulls twice as hard; twice the radius weakens it four times.

3The five-statement judgement · ~2 minutes

Consider the following statements about gravitation.

  1. The weight of a body at the centre of the Earth is zero.
  2. The acceleration due to gravity is greater at the equator than at the poles.
  3. A body of mass 6 kg on the Earth has a mass of 1 kg on the Moon.
  4. A spring balance reads slightly more for the same body at the pole than at the equator.
  5. Placing a sheet of glass between two bodies does not change the gravitational force between them.

Which of the statements are correct?

1I, IV and V only
2I, II and V only
3II, III and IV only
4I, III, IV and V only
Working

I — true. At the centre the pull from every direction cancels: g = 0, so W = mg = 0 (mass is still there).

II — false. The Earth bulges at the equator, so R is larger there and g is smaller; NCERT: g is greater at the poles.

III — false. Mass never changes with place; it is the weight that falls to one-sixth. 6 kg stays 6 kg.

IV — true. A spring balance measures weight = mg, and g is larger at the pole (statement II corrected). A beam balance would read the same at both.

V — true. Gravitation is independent of the medium between the bodies.

True: I, IV, V — option (1).

Where each wrong option comes from. (2) — II believed (the common "spinning Earth" muddle applied the wrong way). (3) — II and III both believed and V rejected (a "shielding" idea); I doubted ("there is still mass, so there must be weight"). (4) — everything right except III ("one-sixth" attached to mass). Elimination. III is the fastest kill on the page — any option containing III goes, which removes (3) and (4) in ten seconds; then II decides between (1) and (2).
42017-E Q10's machine — area under F–x · ~2 minutes

A particle moving along the x-axis is acted on by a force F that varies with position as shown. At x = 0 the particle has kinetic energy 20 J. Its kinetic energy at x = 12 m is

132 J
252 J
368 J
484 J
Working

Step 1 — work = signed area under the graph. 0 to 4 m: a triangle, ½ × 4 × 8 = +16 J. 4 to 8 m: a rectangle, 4 × 8 = +32 J. 8 to 12 m: a rectangle below the axis, 4 × (−4) = −16 J.

Step 2 — net work. 16 + 32 − 16 = +32 J.

Step 3 — work–energy. KE = 20 + 32 = 52 J. Option (2).

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) 32 J — the net work reported as the kinetic energy; the 20 J it started with forgotten. (3) 68 J — the triangle's ½ dropped (32 instead of 16). (4) 84 J — the region below the axis added instead of subtracted. Elimination. The last 4 m is a retarding force, so the answer must be less than 20 + 16 + 32 = 68 — (3) and (4) go. Between (1) and (2) there is no shortcut — add the areas; (1) is what you get if you forget to add the 20 J.
5Power with an efficiency, run backwards · ~90 seconds

A lift in a Gurugram office tower, of total mass 600 kg with its passengers, rises 30 m at a steady speed in 40 s. The motor is 75% efficient. Taking g = 10 m/s², the electrical power the motor draws is

10.45 kW
23.375 kW
34.5 kW
46 kW
Working

Step 1 — useful work. Steady speed, so the motor's work is the gain in PE: mgh = 600 × 10 × 30 = 180 000 J.

Step 2 — useful power. 180 000 / 40 = 4500 W = 4.5 kW.

Step 3 — which way the efficiency runs. 4.5 kW is the output; the input is larger: 4.5 / 0.75 = 6 kW. Option (4).

Where each wrong option comes from. (3) 4.5 kW — the efficiency ignored. (2) 3.375 kW — the efficiency applied the wrong way (× 0.75): the motor would be drawing less than it delivers. (1) 0.45 kW — mass × speed, the g forgotten: 600 × 30/40. Elimination. Input must exceed useful output, so the answer is the only option above 4.5 kW — you can pick (4) the moment you have 4.5 without ever dividing.
