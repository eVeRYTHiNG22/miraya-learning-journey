Gravitation, Work & Energy

Official topics: Gravitation and Work, Power and Energy (both Class 9) — two topics, one card. Your IGCSE course has weight and g, free fall, density, pressure, work, kinetic and potential energy, power and efficiency (0625 sections 1.2–1.8). What it does not have — and Allen asks — is the universal law of gravitation and how g changes with depth and latitude (your course says only that it falls with distance from a planet); buoyancy, Archimedes' principle and relative density; and the NCERT framing of energy items (kinetic energy at the top of a throw, mechanical energy on a frictionless track, work by a variable force as an area). A 30-minute read; the paper is the point.

1The universal law of gravitation

The one thing in this unit your IGCSE course never states. Learn the sentence, the formula, and the three scaling moves — every real gravitation item is one of them.

NCERT statement

Every object in the universe attracts every other object with a force which is proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. The force is along the line joining the centres of the two objects.

F = G m₁m₂ / d²

G is the universal gravitational constant, 6.673 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻² (NCERT uses 6.7 × 10⁻¹¹ in its worked examples). Its unit comes from G = F d² / (m₁m₂). "Universal" means the same G everywhere — between two marbles, between you and the Earth, between the Earth and the Sun; NCERT's words are that the law applies to all bodies, big or small, celestial or terrestrial. The medium between the bodies makes no difference beyond NCERT: a sheet of glass or a vacuum in the gap changes nothing.

The value of G was found by Henry Cavendish (1731–1810), using a sensitive balance. And NCERT's own illustration of "inverse square": make d 6 times bigger and F becomes 36 times smaller.

How NCERT gets to the law — the apple and the Moon

  • The stone on a thread (Activity 9.1). Whirled round at a steady speed, the stone changes direction at every point — so its velocity changes and it is accelerating. The force causing that points to the centre: the centripetal force. Let go, and the stone flies off along the tangent.
  • The story. An apple fell on Newton as he sat under a tree, and he asked: if the Earth can attract an apple, can it not attract the Moon? He concluded that the same force is at work in both.
  • The Moon is falling too. At every point of its orbit the Moon falls towards the Earth instead of going off in a straight line — that is what keeps it in orbit. The force that keeps any body on a circle acts towards the centre: the centripetal ("centre-seeking") force. For the Moon, the Earth's attraction supplies it. Without it the Moon would move off along the tangent — a straight line that meets the circle at one point only.
  • Does the apple attract the Earth? Yes — by the third law, with an equal force. But by the second law the acceleration a force produces is inversely proportional to the mass, and the Earth's mass is enormous, so its acceleration towards the apple is far too small to see. The same argument explains why the Earth does not move towards the Moon.
  • The same force keeps all the planets going round the Sun — so Newton concluded that all objects in the universe attract each other.
  • NCERT's worked number (Example 9.1). Earth 6 × 10²⁴ kg, Moon 7.4 × 10²² kg, 3.84 × 10⁵ km apart, G = 6.7 × 10⁻¹¹: F = 6.7×10⁻¹¹ × 6×10²⁴ × 7.4×10²² ÷ (3.84×10⁸)² = 2.02 × 10²⁰ N. The one step that goes wrong: kilometres must become metres before squaring.

Why it matters (NCERT's list — Allen asks it as a statement item)

  • the force that binds us to the Earth;
  • the motion of the Moon around the Earth;
  • the motion of the planets around the Sun;
  • the tides due to the Moon and the Sun.

Two 1 kg masses 1 m apart attract with only 6.7 × 10⁻¹¹ N — which is why you never feel the pull of the person next to you. Replace one mass by the Earth (6 × 10²⁴ kg) at its radius (6.4 × 10⁶ m) and the force on 1 kg becomes 9.8 N. Same law, one enormous mass. NCERT's summary puts it in one line: gravitation is a weak force unless large masses are involved.

The four-statement item (2024-U Q7, 2018-S Q11)

Allen lists true statements and asks for the false one, or true properties and asks what the force does not depend on. The true list: always attractive, never repulsive · acts along the line joining the centres · obeys the inverse-square law · depends on the product of the masses (never the sum, never the difference) · independent of the medium · the two forces form an action–reaction pair. In 2024-U Q7 three options were true and "does not obey the inverse-square law" was the odd one out; in 2018-S Q11 "sum of masses" was the one thing on the list the force ignores.

The null point between two masses beyond NCERT

Between two masses a distance d apart there is one point on the line joining them where their pulls cancel. Take 9M and M, with x measured from the heavier one; the mass of the body placed there cancels, leaving G·9M/x² = G·M/(d − x)². Take square roots instead of expanding: 3/x = 1/(d − x), so x = 3d/4 — three-quarters of the way from the heavier mass. The point always lies nearer the lighter mass, and a body placed there feels no net gravitational pull.

2g — where it comes from, and how it changes

Put the Earth into the law and the mass of the falling body cancels. That one line explains why everything falls together, and why g changes with where you stand.

Acceleration due to gravity

For a body of mass m at the Earth's surface, F = G M m / R² and F = m g, so g = G M / R² — the m has gone. With NCERT's numbers, g = 6.7 × 10⁻¹¹ × 6 × 10²⁴ / (6.4 × 10⁶)² = 9.8 m/s². Allen's items use g = 10 m/s² unless the stem says 9.8.

Free fall, in NCERT's words, is falling towards the Earth under the gravitational force alone; a body thrown upwards is treated the same way in problems, moving under gravity alone. All bodies, whatever their mass, fall with the same g (Sample Q11: the 20 kg iron sphere and the 12 kg aluminium sphere share the same acceleration and the same velocity at every height — but not the same kinetic energy or momentum). Air resistance is what makes a feather lag a coin; in a vacuum they land together.

NCERT opens the section with Activity 9.2: throw a stone up; it rises, stops and falls back — the earth's attraction at work.

NCERT's version of the feather-and-coin experiment (Activity 9.3): drop a sheet of paper and a stone together from the first floor. The paper lands a little later — because of air resistance, which is greater on the paper. Do it in a glass jar from which the air has been sucked out and the two fall at the same rate. The story NCERT attaches: Galileo is said to have dropped different objects from the top of the Leaning Tower of Pisa to show exactly this.

While a body is falling freely, the direction of its motion does not change; only the magnitude of its velocity does (NCERT). A body thrown upwards slows to a stop and comes back down — with the same acceleration g all the way. Near the Earth g can be taken as constant; for an object far from the Earth use g = GM/d² with d measured from the Earth's centre.

Where g is bigger and smaller

  • Height: smaller. g at height h is G M / (R + h)². For a mountain the change is tiny (Everest is 0.14% of R); for a satellite it is not.
  • Depth: smaller, falling to zero at the centre (for a uniform Earth) — where you are pulled equally in every direction. (Allen's module: gd = g (1 − d/R); beyond NCERT, but the direction "decreases" is what 2024-U Q11 tested.)
  • Latitude: the Earth is not a perfect sphere — its radius increases from the poles to the equator — so g is greater at the poles than at the equator (NCERT's sentence). A spring balance reads a little more at the pole; a beam balance reads the same everywhere.
  • Another planet: g′ / g = (M′/M) × (R/R′)². Moon: M = 7.36 × 10²² kg, R = 1.74 × 10⁶ m → gmoon ≈ 1.63 m/s², one-sixth of Earth's. A planet with half the Earth's mass and half its radius has g′ = ½ × 4 = 2g.

NCERT's two free-fall examples. Example 9.2: a car falls off a ledge and reaches the ground in 0.5 s (g = 10). Speed on striking the ground v = gt = 5 m/s; average speed (0 + 5)/2 = 2.5 m/s; height of the ledge ½gt² = 1.25 m. Example 9.3: an object thrown straight up rises 10 m (g = 9.8). u = √(2 × 9.8 × 10) = 14 m/s, and the time to the top is u/g = 1.43 s. NCERT's sign rule for both: take a as positive when it is in the direction of motion and negative when it opposes it — so a = +g coming down, −g going up.

Free-fall arithmetic — the equations of motion with a = g

NCERT prints the three equations of motion with a (its equations 9.10–9.12) and says they hold "with acceleration a replaced by g". Written out: v = u + gt, s = ut + ½gt², v² = u² + 2gs — g taken positive along the motion and negative against it.

Orbit items belong here too

The Moon stays in orbit because gravity supplies the centripetal force — always toward the Earth, always at right angles to the velocity, which is along the tangent (2024-A Q7). Switch gravity off and the Moon carries on in a straight line along the tangent at the same speed (2017-E Q11) — not "towards the Earth", not "stationary", not "the same orbit". Because F ⟂ v, gravity does no work on an orbiting body, which is why its speed in a circular orbit never changes.

3Mass and weight

What each word means

  • Mass is the measure of a body's inertia. It is the same everywhere — on the Earth, on the Moon, in a spacecraft. Scalar, kilogram, measured on a beam balance.
  • Weight is the force with which the Earth attracts the body: W = m g. It acts vertically downward, is measured in newtons on a spring balance, and changes with g.
  • Weight on the Moon is about one-sixth of the weight on Earth, because g there is one-sixth. The mass is unchanged.
  • At any one place g is fixed, so weight is directly proportional to mass there — which is why a weight reading can be used as a measure of mass. Weight is a force: it has magnitude and a direction (vertically downwards).

Where the one-sixth comes from (NCERT's Table 9.1). Earth: mass 5.98 × 10²⁴ kg, radius 6.37 × 10⁶ m. Moon: mass 7.36 × 10²² kg, radius 1.74 × 10⁶ m. Weight is G × (mass of the body it stands on) × m ÷ (its radius)², so Wmoon = 2.431 × 10¹⁰ × G m and Wearth = 1.474 × 10¹¹ × G m; the ratio is 0.165 ≈ 1/6. NCERT's two examples: a 10 kg object weighs 10 × 9.8 = 98 N on Earth (Example 9.4); an object weighing 10 N on Earth weighs 10/6 = 1.67 N on the Moon (Example 9.5).

A 60 kg student weighs 600 N on Earth (g = 10) and 100 N on the Moon; she is 60 kg in both places. Sample Q13 runs the machine backwards: a rock weighing 200.6 N on the Moon has mass 200.6 / 1.7 = 118 kg — and its mass on Earth is the same 118 kg. The distractors are 200.6/9.8 ≈ 20 (used Earth's g), 200 (the weight number read as a mass) and 200.6 × 10/1.7 = 1180 (the weight converted to Earth with g = 10 instead of finding the mass).

Words to read twice

"Weight in kg" on a shop scale is mass. "Weightless" in orbit beyond NCERT means the body and its support fall together — gravity is very much still acting. "The same weight on the Moon" is false; "the same mass" is true. A statement that the mass of a body on the Moon is one-sixth is a planted error.

4Thrust, pressure, buoyancy — the half Allen has not asked yet

These sit in the Gravitation chapter in NCERT and on the official topic list, and the hub's Physics subject paper asks them, but none of the five real papers ever did. Learn them in one pass; do not over-invest.

Thrust and pressure

  • Thrust is the force acting on an object perpendicular to its surface. Unit: newton.
  • Pressure is thrust per unit area: P = F / A. SI unit pascal, 1 Pa = 1 N/m². The same thrust on a smaller area gives a larger pressure — a sharp knife, a nail's point, a camel's wide feet in reverse.
  • Pressure in fluids (liquids and gases): a fluid exerts pressure on the base and walls of its container, and pressure applied to a confined fluid is transmitted undiminished in all directions. At depth h in a liquid of density ρ the extra pressure is h ρ g (from your IGCSE beyond NCERT; NCERT Class 9 never says that pressure grows with depth): 10 m of water adds 10 × 1000 × 10 = 10⁵ Pa, about one atmosphere.

The questions NCERT opens with — each is "same force, different area". Why can a camel run easily in the desert? Why does an army tank weighing more than a thousand tonnes rest on a continuous chain? Why do trucks and buses have much wider tyres? (A large area keeps the pressure low.) Why do cutting tools have sharp edges? (A tiny area makes the pressure high — the same reason a drawing pin, NCERT's first example, has a point.) Stand on loose sand and your feet sink in; lie down and you hardly sink — the thrust, your weight, is the same, but the area is larger. Buildings have wide foundations for the same reason.

Example 9.6. A 5 kg wooden block, 40 cm × 20 cm × 10 cm, on a table. Thrust = 5 × 9.8 = 49 N whichever face it rests on. On the 20 cm × 10 cm face (0.02 m²): 49/0.02 = 2450 N/m². On the 40 cm × 20 cm face (0.08 m²): 49/0.08 = 612.5 N/m². The unit is named after Blaise Pascal.

Buoyancy and Archimedes' principle

  • Buoyancy is the upward force a fluid exerts on a body placed in it — the upthrust (buoyant force). It is why a bucket feels lighter until it clears the water and why a cork pushed under bobs back up.
  • Archimedes' principle: when a body is immersed fully or partially in a fluid, it experiences an upward force equal to the weight of the fluid displaced by it: upthrust = Vdisplaced × ρfluid × g.
  • Float or sink is decided by density: denser than the liquid → sinks; less dense → floats with part of it above the surface; equal → hangs anywhere in the liquid beyond NCERT.
  • Density = mass / volume (kg/m³; water 1000 kg/m³ = 1 g/cm³). Relative density = density of the substance / density of water — a pure ratio, no unit. Iron RD 7.8; ice 0.92; petrol about 0.7. beyond NCERT The print no longer has a relative-density section; Allen and the hub's subject paper use it all the same.

How NCERT builds buoyancy — four activities and a story

  • You already know it: you feel lighter in a swimming pool; a bucket of water feels heavier once it is lifted clear of the well; a ship of iron and steel floats while the same iron as a flat sheet sinks.
  • The empty bottle (Activity 9.4). A stoppered empty plastic bottle floats. Push it under and you feel an upward push that grows as more of the bottle goes under, until it is completely immersed; let go and it bounces back up. That upward push is the upthrust, or buoyant force. Its size depends on the density of the fluid. To keep the bottle under, you must push down with at least the difference, upthrust − weight.
  • The nail and the cork (Activities 9.5, 9.6). An iron nail and a piece of cork of equal mass are placed on water: the nail sinks, the cork floats. It is density (mass per unit volume) that decides, not mass: a cork pushed fully under feels an upthrust greater than its weight, so it rises and floats; the nail feels less than its weight even when fully under, so it sinks.
  • The stone on a spring balance or a rubber string (Activity 9.7). Lower the stone into water and the reading falls steadily as more of it goes under — then stops changing once it is fully immersed, because from then on it displaces no more water.
  • Archimedes was a Greek scientist. He found the principle on noticing the water overflow as he stepped into a bath, and ran through the streets shouting "Eureka!" — "I have got it". He used it to test the purity of the gold in the king's crown. His work in geometry and mechanics — levers, pulleys, the wheel-and-axle — made him famous and helped the Greek army against the Romans.
  • Where the principle is used: designing ships and submarines; lactometers, which test the purity of milk; hydrometers, which measure the density of liquids.
P = F/A · 1 Pa = 1 N/m²

Relative density from a spring balance beyond NCERT

Hang the body from a spring balance in air, then fully under water. The loss of weight is the upthrust — the weight of an equal volume of water. So RD of the body = weight in air ÷ loss of weight in water. Dip it in another liquid too, and RD of the liquid = loss of weight in the liquid ÷ loss of weight in water. Example: 60 N in air, 45 N in water, 48 N in a liquid → RD of the body = 60 ÷ 15 = 4; RD of the liquid = 12 ÷ 15 = 0.8.

5Work — a force and a displacement, with a sign

NCERT's definition is narrow on purpose, and the marks are in the sign and the zero cases.

NCERT definition

Work done on an object = force × displacement of the object in the direction of the force: W = F s. Two conditions must both hold — a force must act, and the object must be displaced. If either is missing the work is zero, however tired you are. Unit: joule, 1 J = 1 N m — the work done when a force of 1 N moves an object through 1 m along the force. Scalar.

"Work" in science is not "working hard". NCERT's cases: Kamali studies for hours — a great deal of effort, very little work. You push a huge rock that does not move, or stand still with a heavy load on your head: you tire, but the work done on the rock or the load is zero, because nothing is displaced. Push a pebble, pull a trolley, lift a book, climb a staircase: a force acts and the object moves, so work is done. Its two worked numbers: 5 N through 2 m is 10 J (Example 10.1); a porter lifting 15 kg of luggage 1.5 m on to his head does mg × s = 15 × 10 × 1.5 = 225 J (Example 10.2). And Activity 10.4: when you lift an object, your force does positive work and gravity does negative work on it.

NCERT's other examples of work done: a bullock pulling a cart, and a baby pulling a toy car along the ground — positive work, since the force is along the motion. Its Activities 10.1–10.3 ask you to list everyday "work" and, for each, what the force acts on, what happens to it and what does the work — and to think of a force with no displacement, and a displacement with no force.

  • Positive work: force along the displacement (2024-U Q15: Minakshee pushes the block from A to B along the table; her push and the motion are the same way — positive, and nothing in the figure says otherwise).
  • Negative work: force opposite to the displacement — friction on a sliding block; gravity on a ball moving up; the retarding force of a brake. W = −F s.
  • Zero work: displacement zero (pushing a wall, holding a bag still) — or force at right angles to the displacement — NCERT's own cases are gravity on an orbiting satellite and gravity on a mass moved along a level table (its Exercises 11 and 5); others are the load on a porter's head as he walks along a level platform, and the centripetal force in any circular motion.
  • beyond NCERT A pull at an angle θ to the motion: only its component along the motion, F cos θ, does work, so W = F s cos θ. If the body moves at constant velocity along a level floor, friction balances that component, friction = F cos θ, so the pull does work +F s cos θ and friction does −F s cos θ.

Work by a force that changes — the area rule (2017-E Q10)

When F varies with position, work = area under the F–x graph, counting area below the axis as negative — exactly as area under F–t is change of momentum. 2017-E Q10 (a triangle then three rectangles) gave +30 − 20 + 20 − 10 = +20 J, so the kinetic energy rose from 25 J to 45 J. Worked example 4 below runs the same machine. Beyond NCERT's text; Allen has asked it.

6Energy — the conservation ladder

Kinetic and potential energy, and the one rule that answers four of the twenty real items.

NCERT definitions

  • Energy is the capacity to do work; same unit, the joule. 1 kJ = 1000 J.
  • Kinetic energy — the energy possessed by an object due to its motion: KE = ½ m v². NCERT derives it from the work done to bring a body from rest to v (F s = m a s = ½ m v² using v² = 2as). Never negative; doubles v → four times the KE.
  • Potential energy — the energy possessed by an object because of its position or configuration. Gravitational PE at height h: PE = m g h, the work done to lift it. It depends on the reference level you choose, and on the height only, not the path taken to get there.
  • Law of conservation of energy: energy can only be converted from one form to another; it can neither be created nor destroyed. The total energy before and after a transformation remains the same. NCERT adds that the law is valid in all situations and for all kinds of transformations.

What else NCERT says about energy

  • Sources. The Sun is the biggest natural source of energy, and many other sources derive from it. Energy also comes from the nuclei of atoms, the interior of the Earth and the tides — three that do not come from sunlight.
  • Who gains and who loses. An object that can do work possesses energy. The object that does the work loses energy; the object on which the work is done gains it.
  • Capacity to do work — NCERT's examples. A fast cricket ball knocks the wicket over; a raised hammer drives a nail in; a wound toy car runs; a balloon pressed hard bursts with a bang. Its "Think it over" asks how you would know that something is a form of energy — because it can do work. A second one asks what would have happened if nature had not allowed energy to change form: NCERT reports the view that life could not have been possible, and asks whether you agree.
  • Forms of energy. Mechanical energy (kinetic + potential), heat, chemical, electrical and light energy.
  • Kinetic energy. NCERT's examples: a moving bullet, the blowing wind, a rotating wheel, a speeding stone, a falling coconut, flowing water, a running athlete — and an object moving faster can do more work than an identical one moving slowly. By definition, the kinetic energy of a body moving with a certain velocity equals the work done on it to make it acquire that velocity. In general the work done equals the change in kinetic energy: W = ½m(v² − u²). NCERT's examples: 15 kg at 4 m/s has 120 J (Example 10.3); taking a 1500 kg car from 30 km/h to 60 km/h needs ½ × 1500 × [(50/3)² − (25/3)²] = 156 250 J (Example 10.4). Its demonstrations: a ball dropped on wet sand from 25 cm, 50 cm, 1 m and 1.5 m makes a deeper dent each time (Activity 10.6); a trolley pulled by a heavier load hits a block harder and shifts it further (Activity 10.7).
  • Potential energy is not only height. A stretched rubber band, a stretched or compressed slinky, the wound spring of a toy car (more windings, more energy stored) and a bent bow all store energy because of their changed shape — their configuration (Activities 10.8–10.10 and 10.12; Activity 10.11, an object lifted through a height, is energy of position). NCERT's rule: energy transferred to an object is stored as potential energy if it is not used to change the object's speed. The bow's stored energy becomes the arrow's kinetic energy.
  • Gravitational potential energy: the work done in raising the object from the ground to that point against gravity; the least force needed is its weight, mg, so Ep = mgh. 10 kg at 6 m: 10 × 9.8 × 6 = 588 J (Example 10.5). 12 kg with 480 J (g = 10): h = 480 ÷ 120 = 4 m (Example 10.6).
  • Conversions in nature — NCERT's Activity 10.13 asks about these; the answers: green plants turn the Sun's light into the chemical energy of food; the Sun's heating makes the air move; coal and petroleum are stored energy from plants and animals of long ago; the water cycle is driven by the Sun.
  • NCERT's Activity 10.15 leaves this table for you to fill; filled in: 20 kg dropped from 4 m, g = 10. At 4, 3, 2, 1 m and just above the ground the potential energy is 800, 600, 400, 200, 0 J and the kinetic energy 0, 200, 400, 600, 800 J — the sum is 800 J in every row. That sum is the total mechanical energy.

James Prescott Joule (1818–1889), British physicist, known for his work on electricity and thermodynamics: he gave the law for the heating effect of an electric current, verified the law of conservation of energy by experiment and found the mechanical equivalent of heat. The unit of work and energy is named after him.

The four real energy items, one machine

  • KE at the highest point of a vertical throw is zero — the body is momentarily at rest (2024-U Q4; the 15 kg and 40 m/s are decoration). Only for a vertical throw: a projectile keeps its horizontal speed at the top — but projectiles are not on the 2027 list.
  • PE now = mghstart − KE now when released from rest (2024-A Q4).
  • Total mechanical energy is constant along a frictionless track, whatever its loops and curves (2024-A Q15) — it does not "decrease going down and increase going up"; that describes PE alone.
  • Name the energies (Sample Q9): four columns — at A (on the tee), from A to B (in flight), from B to C (coming down and rolling), at D (stopped in the cup). The rule Allen's key applied: at rest → PE only; moving → KE and PE (the tee is above the zero level). Two options had KE + PE in both middle columns; what separated them was the first cell — a ball at rest on the tee has no KE, so "only KE at A" is the planted error. The other two options put "only PE" or "only KE" on the moving intervals.

When friction is present — the work–energy account (2018-S Q10)

With air resistance or friction, mechanical energy is not conserved; the missing part is the work done by the resisting force, which is negative. Bookkeeping: KE gained = work by gravity + work by friction. 2018-S Q10: dropped from H it should arrive at √(2gH); it arrives at 1.2√(gH), so KE = ½m(1.44 gH) = 0.72 mgH against the mgH gravity supplied. Work by air friction = 0.72 mgH − mgH = −0.28 mgH. The planted options: +0.28 (sign dropped), ±0.14 (half the true loss). In Allen's language this is the work–energy theorem: net work on a body = its change in kinetic energy. NCERT uses the idea to derive ½mv² but never names it.

7Power — the rate, and the ratio items

NCERT definition

Power is the rate of doing work or of transfer of energy: P = W / t. Unit watt, 1 W = 1 J/s; 1 kW = 1000 W. Average power = total energy consumed ÷ total time. Two agents doing the same work in different times have different powers — the faster one is more powerful.

The watt is named after James Watt (1736–1819). NCERT's two examples: two girls, each weighing 400 N, climb 8 m of rope, A in 20 s and B in 50 s — P = mgh/t = 400 × 8 ÷ 20 = 160 W for A and 400 × 8 ÷ 50 = 64 W for B (Example 10.7); a 50 kg boy runs up 45 steps of 15 cm in 9 s (g = 10) — height 6.75 m, P = 500 × 6.75 ÷ 9 = 375 W (Example 10.8).

Activity 10.16 is the idea in words: two children of equal weight climb the same 8 m rope, A in 15 s and B in 20 s. They do the same work, but A does it faster, so A's power is greater.

Commercial unit: the kilowatt-hour — the energy used by 1 kW running for 1 hour: 1 kWh = 1000 W × 3600 s = 3.6 × 10⁶ J, the "unit" on an electricity bill. beyond NCERT The print no longer has this section — but its Activity 10.17 has you read the "units" on your electricity meter, and Exercise 9 of the same chapter, Work and Energy ("250 units of energy"), cannot be done without it. Learn the one line.

The machines Allen actually uses

  • Climbing: P = m g h / t. A 50 kg student up 3 m of stairs in 5 s: 50 × 10 × 3 / 5 = 300 W. Efficiency of the body is not asked — the useful work is the lift.
  • Ratio of two climbers: same stairs, so P₁/P₂ = (W₁/W₂) × (t₂/t₁) — weights the right way up, times upside down. 2018-S Q15 ≡ Sample Q15: weights 5 : 3, times 11 : 9 → (5/3) × (9/11) = 15/11. The planted 11/15 is the ratio inverted; 11/9 and 9/11 forget the weights.
  • A flow of water (dam, pump, waterfall): P = (mass per second) × g × h, then multiply by the efficiency for useful output, or divide by it for the input drawn. 2017-E Q15: 2000 kg/s, height difference 550 − 50 = 500 m: 2000 × 10 × 500 = 10⁷ W = 10 MW; × 0.8 = 8 MW. Using 550 m gives 8.8 MW, which is not an option — the sign to recheck the height; forgetting the efficiency gives 10 MW, which is.
  • beyond NCERT Efficiency = useful output / input (× 100 for a percentage) — never above 100%. A 40% efficient motor drawing 500 W delivers 200 W of lifting; to get 200 W useful from it you must supply 200/0.4 = 500 W. Decide which way the question runs before you touch the numbers.
  • P = F v for a force moving something at steady speed v (beyond NCERT; Allen's module) — a car's engine power against a constant resisting force.

9Five worked questions — at TALLENTEX pitch

Try each before opening the working and give yourself 90 seconds — the real paper's average. The elimination reasoning is written out because on the day, ruling two options out is worth almost as much as solving.

1Sample Q14's machine, carried one step on · ~90 seconds

Two solid lead spheres of radii r and 2r are placed touching each other, and the gravitational force between them is F. Two more lead spheres are now made with each radius doubled (2r and 4r) and placed touching. The force between the new pair is

18F
216F
332F
464F
Working

Step 1 — masses. Same material, so m ∝ r³. Doubling a radius multiplies its mass by 2³ = 8. Both spheres are doubled, so the product m₁m₂ becomes 8 × 8 = 64 times bigger.

Step 2 — distance. Touching spheres have their centres r + 2r = 3r apart; the new pair 2r + 4r = 6r — the distance has doubled, so d² is 4 times bigger.

Step 3 — F′ = 64F / 4 = 16F. Option (2).

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) 8F — only one sphere's mass scaled (× 8) and the distance ignored. (3) 32F — masses right (× 64) but the doubled distance divided in once, not squared. (4) 64F — the distance forgotten altogether. Elimination. Each mass grows 8 times, so the masses alone would make the force 64F, and moving the centres further apart can only reduce it — (4) is out at once. The distance between the centres doubles, so divide by 2² = 4: 16F. Neither 8F nor 32F is 64F ÷ 4.
2g on another world, then free fall · ~90 seconds

A planet has twice the mass of the Earth and twice its radius. A stone is dropped from rest from a height of 40 m above the planet's surface. Taking Earth's g = 10 m/s² and no atmosphere, the stone reaches the surface after

12 s
22√2 s
34 s
44√2 s
Working

Step 1 — g on the planet. g′ = g × (M′/M) × (R/R′)² = 10 × 2 × (½)² = 10 × 2/4 = 5 m/s². Twice the mass pulls twice as hard; twice the radius weakens it four times.

Step 2 — fall time. h = ½ g′ t² → t = √(2h/g′) = √(80/5) = √16 = 4 s. Option (3).

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) 2 s — g′ taken as 2g = 20 (the radius forgotten): √(80/20) = 2. (2) 2√2 s — Earth's g used unchanged: √(80/10) = √8. 3The five-statement judgement · ~2 minutes

Consider the following statements about gravitation.

  1. The weight of a body at the centre of the Earth is zero.
  2. The acceleration due to gravity is greater at the equator than at the poles.
  3. A body of mass 6 kg on the Earth has a mass of 1 kg on the Moon.
  4. A spring balance reads slightly more for the same body at the pole than at the equator.
  5. Placing a sheet of glass between two bodies does not change the gravitational force between them.

Which of the statements are correct?

1I, IV and V only
2I, II and V only
3II, III and IV only
4I, III, IV and V only
Working

I — true. At the centre the pull from every direction cancels: g = 0, so W = mg = 0 (mass is still there).

II — false. The Earth bulges at the equator, so R is larger there and g is smaller; NCERT: g is greater at the poles.

III — false. Mass never changes with place; it is the weight that falls to one-sixth. 6 kg stays 6 kg.

IV — true. A spring balance measures weight = mg, and g is larger at the pole (statement II corrected). A beam balance would read the same at both.

V — true. Gravitation is independent of the medium between the bodies.

True: I, IV, V — option (1).

Where each wrong option comes from. (2) — II believed (the common "spinning Earth" muddle applied the wrong way). (3) — II and III both believed and V rejected (a "shielding" idea); I doubted ("there is still mass, so there must be weight"). (4) — everything right except III ("one-sixth" attached to mass). Elimination. III is the fastest kill on the page — any option containing III goes, which removes (3) and (4) in ten seconds; then II decides between (1) and (2).
42017-E Q10's machine — area under F–x · ~2 minutes

A particle moving along the x-axis is acted on by a force F that varies with position as shown. At x = 0 the particle has kinetic energy 20 J. Its kinetic energy at x = 12 m is

132 J
252 J
368 J
484 J
Working

Step 1 — work = signed area under the graph. 0 to 4 m: a triangle, ½ × 4 × 8 = +16 J. 4 to 8 m: a rectangle, 4 × 8 = +32 J. 8 to 12 m: a rectangle below the axis, 4 × (−4) = −16 J.

Step 2 — net work. 16 + 32 − 16 = +32 J.

Step 3 — work–energy. KE = 20 + 32 = 52 J. Option (2).

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) 32 J — the net work reported as the kinetic energy; the 20 J it started with forgotten. (3) 68 J — the triangle's ½ dropped (32 instead of 16). (4) 84 J — the region below the axis added instead of subtracted. Elimination. The last 4 m is a retarding force, so the answer must be less than 20 + 16 + 32 = 68 — (3) and (4) go. Between (1) and (2) there is no shortcut — add the areas; (1) is what you get if you forget to add the 20 J.
5Power with an efficiency, run backwards · ~90 seconds

A lift in a Gurugram office tower, of total mass 600 kg with its passengers, rises 30 m at a steady speed in 40 s. The motor is 75% efficient. Taking g = 10 m/s², the electrical power the motor draws is

10.45 kW
23.375 kW
34.5 kW
46 kW
Working

Step 1 — useful work. Steady speed, so the motor's work is the gain in PE: mgh = 600 × 10 × 30 = 180 000 J.

Step 2 — useful power. 180 000 / 40 = 4500 W = 4.5 kW.

Step 3 — which way the efficiency runs. 4.5 kW is the output; the input is larger: 4.5 / 0.75 = 6 kW. Option (4).

Where each wrong option comes from. (3) 4.5 kW — the efficiency ignored. (2) 3.375 kW — the efficiency applied the wrong way (× 0.75): the motor would be drawing less than it delivers. (1) 0.45 kW — mass × speed, the g forgotten: 600 × 30/40. Elimination. Input must exceed useful output, so the answer is the only option above 4.5 kW — you can pick (4) the moment you have 4.5 without ever dividing.

10A 40 kg object is raised 5 m above the ground. What is its potential energy? If it falls, what is its kinetic energy half-way down?2000 J; 1000 J (with g = 10)Half the height gone, half the PE converted.
11What is the work done by gravity on a satellite moving round the Earth?Zero — in a circular orbit the force is always at right angles to the displacement.Force ⟂ velocity.
12Can there be displacement of an object in the absence of any force acting on it?Yes — an object already moving keeps moving at uniform velocity with no net force on it.First law.
13A person holds a bundle of hay over his head for 30 minutes and gets tired. Has he done work?No — the bundle is not displaced.Tired, but s = 0.
14An electric heater is rated 1500 W. How much energy does it use in 10 hours?5.4 × 10⁷ J (15 kWh)1500 W × 36 000 s
15Describe the energy changes when a pendulum bob is pulled aside and released. Why does it eventually stop? Is the law of conservation violated?At each end all potential, at the lowest point all kinetic, and a mixture in between. Air resistance and friction at the support turn the energy little by little into heat, so it stops. No violation — the energy has gone to the surroundings.PE ⇄ KE, with a slow leak to heat.
16An object of mass m moves with constant velocity v. How much work must be done on it to bring it to rest?½mv², done against its motion (the stopping force does −½mv² of work on it).Work = change in KE.
17Calculate the work needed to stop a 1500 kg car moving at 60 km/h.208 333 J (about 2.08 × 10⁵ J), done against the motion½ × 1500 × (50/3)²
18Three diagrams, each with the displacement from west to east. The force acts (i) vertically upward, (ii) towards the east, (iii) towards the west. Is the work negative, positive or zero?(i) zero; (ii) positive; (iii) negativeAt right angles; along; against.
19Soni says the acceleration of an object could be zero even when several forces act on it. Do you agree?Yes — if the forces balance, the net force is zero and so is the acceleration.Balanced forces.
20Find the energy in joules consumed in 10 hours by four devices of power 500 W each.7.2 × 10⁷ J (20 kWh)4 × 500 W × 36 000 s
21A freely falling object eventually stops on reaching the ground. What happens to its kinetic energy?It is converted into heat and sound, and into the work of deforming the ground and the object.Nothing is destroyed.
