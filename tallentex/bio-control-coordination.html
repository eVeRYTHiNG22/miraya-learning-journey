Control & Coordination

Official topic: Control and Coordination — NCERT Class 10 Science, Chapter 6. You have never been taught biology, so this page assumes nothing: it starts from why a body needs a control system at all, draws each part before naming it, and walks every process as a numbered sequence. It is one chapter with three halves — nerves (fast, electrical), hormones (slow, chemical) and how plants manage with hormones alone. Budget 35–40 minutes for the read, then the worked questions and the ten-question check.

Every Biology question (Q31–45) in all five cached papers — four real sittings and Allen's official sample — was read page by page (the 2017 and 2018 booklets are image scans, so they were rendered and read as pictures) and every item from this chapter was matched to Allen's own key sheet. What that tells you:

  2. Every item so far is single-fact recall, and most are vocabulary tests: the name of the gap-crossing chemical, the name of the fear hormone, which brain part does which job.   3. Allen reaches past the NCERT chapter. Attempt order on the day: Biology is the section you bank first (strategy rule 2). Every question from this chapter should take under 45 seconds — know it or eliminate to two and move on.

1Why a body needs a control system — and the two it uses

Before any names: what problem is this chapter solving?

You touch a hot pan. Your hand is off it before you have decided anything. You see a stray dog running at you and your heart is pounding before you have taken a step. A plant on a windowsill slowly leans towards the glass. In each case, one part of a living thing has detected a change (heat, a threat, light) and a different part has responded (a muscle, the heart, a growing stem). Something had to carry the message from the detector to the responder, and something had to decide how big the response should be. That carrying-and-deciding is control and coordination.

Three words you will use on every page

Stimulus — any change in the surroundings (or inside the body) that an organism can detect: heat, light, touch, a chemical, gravity, a drop in blood sugar.
Receptor — the part that detects the stimulus. In animals these are cells in the sense organs: gustatory receptors in the tongue detect taste; olfactory receptors in the nose detect smell; the skin has receptors for heat, pressure and pain; the eye for light.
Effector — the part that responds. In animals, a muscle (it contracts) or a gland (it secretes).

Animals have two systems doing this job, and the whole chapter is really a comparison of them:

Nervous systemHormonal (endocrine) system
Message isan electrical impulse along a nerve cell, with a chemical hand-over at each junctiona chemical (a hormone) poured into the blood
Travels bya fixed wire — the nerve fibre — to one exact placethe bloodstream, so it reaches everywhere; only the target organs respond

Effect lastsbriefly; stops when the impulse stopsa long time — growth, puberty, blood-sugar control
Good forquick, precise actions: pulling a hand back, blinking, walkingslow, whole-body changes: growth, metabolism, the fear response, sugar balance

Plants have no nerves at all. They coordinate entirely with chemicals (plant hormones) and with growth — which is why a plant can bend towards light but cannot pull a leaf away from a flame. That is the third part of the chapter (sections 7–8).

2The neuron — the wire, drawn first

The nervous system is built from one kind of cell. The sample paper's Q45 asked only its name; the real papers ask what its parts do.

A neuron (nerve cell) is the structural and functional unit of the nervous system — the phrase Allen's sample used. It is the longest cell in the body: a motor neuron running from your spinal cord to your toe is about a metre long. It has three parts you must be able to name on a labelled figure:

  • Dendrites — short, branched fibres that receive the message. (Greek dendron = tree.)
  • Cell body (cyton) — holds the nucleus and most of the cytoplasm.
  • Axon — one long fibre that carries the message away from the cell body to the nerve endings (axon terminals). Many axons are wrapped in a fatty myelin sheath that insulates them and speeds the impulse; the gaps in the sheath are nodes of Ranvier. ◆ beyond NCERT

How a message travels along one neuron — the sequence

  1. The stimulus is picked up at the tip of a dendrite (in a receptor, or from the previous neuron).
  2. It sets off a chemical reaction there that creates an electrical impulse.
  3. The impulse travels dendrite → cell body → along the axon to its end.
  4. At the nerve ending, the electrical impulse triggers the release of chemicals — and that is where the next section starts.

Three kinds of neuron — all three are labelled on NCERT’s reflex-arc figure

Sensory neuron — carries the impulse from a receptor (skin, eye, tongue) to the brain or spinal cord.
Relay neuron (interneuron) — lies inside the brain or spinal cord and connects a sensory neuron to a motor neuron.
Motor neuron — carries the impulse from the brain or spinal cord to an effector (a muscle or gland).
A nerve is a bundle of many axons wrapped together, like a cable of wires. ◆ Only the word interneuron and the definition of a nerve are extras — NCERT’s Figure 6.2 prints “Sensory neuron”, “Relay neuron” and “Motor neuron” on the diagram itself, so you can be asked them.

3The synapse — where two neurons meet without touching

2017-E Q31 drew two neurons end to end and asked why the impulse crosses. This is the single most-asked idea in the chapter.

Two neurons do not touch. Between the nerve ending of one and the dendrite of the next there is a tiny gap called a synapse. An electrical impulse cannot jump a gap — so the body converts it:

  1. The impulse arrives at the nerve ending of neuron A.
  2. It makes the ending release chemicals — called neurotransmitters ◆ the name Allen used; NCERT just says “chemicals” — into the gap.
  3. The chemicals diffuse across the gap and land on the dendrite of neuron B.
  4. There they start a fresh electrical impulse, which now travels along neuron B.

The same kind of junction delivers the message from the last neuron to the effector — a muscle cell or a gland cell — which is how “decide to move” becomes “muscle contracts”.

The two things the distractors say

“The dendrites are connected” / “Neuron A hits neuron B” — no: there is a gap and no contact; that is the whole point of the word synapse. “Carbohydrates are released” — no: the chemicals are neurotransmitters, not food. And one more: the impulse crosses a synapse in one direction only (nerve ending → dendrite), because only the nerve-ending side can release the chemicals. ◆ beyond NCERT

4Reflex action — the shortcut that skips the brain

Why is your hand off the hot pan before you have thought about it? Because the response did not wait for the thinking part of the brain — the message does reach it, at the same time — but you become aware of the burn only after the hand has moved.

Definitions

A reflex action is a sudden, involuntary (not under your will), rapid response to a stimulus, without the thinking part of the brain taking part.
A reflex arc is the path the impulse takes in a reflex: receptor → sensory neuron → spinal cord → motor neuron → effector.

  1. Stimulus and receptor. Heat from the flame is detected by receptors in the skin of the finger.
  2. Sensory neuron. Carries the impulse from the receptor into the spinal cord.
  3. Spinal cord. Inside its grey matter a relay neuron passes the impulse straight across to a motor neuron — one synapse, no thinking.
  4. Motor neuron. Carries the impulse out to the muscles of the arm.
  5. Effector. The muscle contracts and the hand is pulled away. Meanwhile a copy of the message has travelled up the spinal cord to the brain — which is why you feel the pain and say “ouch” after the hand has already moved.

Why bother with a shortcut? Thinking is a complex job done by the fore-brain, and it takes time. For a hot pan, time is damage. So the nerves that carry input from all over the body meet in a bundle in the spinal cord on their way to the brain, and reflex arcs are formed in the spinal cord — the response is sent back out before the information has even reached the brain. NCERT's phrase: reflex arcs “have evolved in animals because the thinking process of the brain is not fast enough”.

◆ Conditioned reflexes — a coaching favourite

Your mouth watering at the sight of a golgappa stall is a reflex you learned — a conditioned reflex. The Russian scientist Pavlov rang a bell whenever he fed his dogs; soon the bell alone made them salivate. Pulling your hand from a flame is an unconditioned (inborn) reflex. Both are involuntary; only one needed practice.

5The brain — what each part does (the part Allen actually tests)

2018-S asked two brain questions in one paper: a mismatched pair, and what the thalamus does. Learn the table below like a phone number.

The brain and the spinal cord together are the central nervous system (CNS). The nerves that run out of them to the rest of the body — cranial nerves from the brain and spinal nerves from the spinal cord — are the peripheral nervous system (PNS). ◆ 12 pairs of cranial nerves, 31 pairs of spinal nerves.

The brain has three regions. NCERT names the fore-, mid- and hind-brain and gives jobs to the cerebrum, cerebellum and medulla; the coaching modules (and 2018-S) add the thalamus, and the jobs of the hypothalamus and the pons — both of which NCERT labels on its Figure 6.3 without explaining. All in one table:

The swap the paper plays

Cerebrum ↔ cerebellum ↔ medulla are the three that get swapped. Fix them with what they sound like: cerebrum is the big thinking one; cerebellum is the small one at the back that keeps you balanced (“bell” — it hangs behind); medulla sits on the spinal cord and does the maintenance jobs you never think about. And hypothalamus ≠ voluntary movements — voluntary is always cerebrum.

How the CNS is protected

  • The brain sits inside a bony box, the skull (cranium); the spinal cord runs inside the vertebral column (backbone).
  • Inside the skull the brain floats in fluid — the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) — held in what NCERT calls a fluid-filled balloon, which absorbs shocks. ◆ The “balloon” is a sac of three membranes called the meninges; the CSF is the fluid inside it.

◆ Grey matter and white matter — the “butterfly” in 2018-S Q31

Grey matter is where the cell bodies are; white matter is the myelin-covered axons. In the brain the grey matter is on the outside (the folded cortex). In the spinal cord it is inside, and in cross-section it is shaped like a butterfly (or the letter H) with the white matter around it — exactly as Figure 3 draws it. The 2018 paper listed “spinal cord → butterfly-shaped grey matter” as a true pair.

How does a nerve impulse become a movement?

The last neuron ends on a muscle cell. Muscle cells contain special proteins that change both their shape and their arrangement when the electrical impulse arrives — the cell becomes shorter, and a muscle made of many such cells contracts. That is the entire mechanism of “nervous tissue causes action”, and it is one line in NCERT that is easy to set as a “which statement is correct” item.

6Nerves are half the story — where hormones come in

A neuron can only deliver a message to cells it is wired to. Two limits follow, and hormones exist to get round them.

  1. Reach. A nerve cannot reach every cell of the body, and it cannot keep firing forever. Growth, puberty, keeping blood sugar steady — these need every relevant cell told, for months.
  2. Plants. A plant has no nervous tissue at all, yet it bends towards light and its roots grow downward. Something chemical is doing the coordinating.

Definition

A hormone is a chemical messenger made in one part of an organism, carried (in animals, by the blood) to another part, where it produces a specific effect. Animal hormones are made by endocrine glands — glands with no duct, which pour their secretion directly into the blood. (Ducted glands like the salivary glands and sweat glands are exocrine; the pancreas is both.)

Hormones are slow and long-lasting; nerves are fast and brief. The body uses both: when you are frightened, nerves make you jump now, and the hormone adrenaline keeps your heart racing for the next ten minutes. Sections 7–8 take the plant side first (it is simpler), section 9 the animal glands.

7Coordination in plants — two kinds of movement

Plants move. Slowly, and never from place to place, but they move — and in two completely different ways that a question will ask you to tell apart.

(a) Immediate response — no growth involved

Touch the leaves of the touch-me-not (Mimosa pudica, chhui-mui) and they fold up and droop within seconds. The plant has no nerves, so how?

  1. The touch is detected at the leaf; the plant uses electrical–chemical means to pass the information from cell to cell (not a nervous system — there is no specialised tissue for it).
  2. Cells at the base of the leaflets and leaf-stalk change shape by changing the amount of water in them — water moves out, the cells go limp, the leaf folds. ◆ The swollen base of the leaf-stalk that does this is called the pulvinus.
  3. No growth has happened; when the cells refill, the leaf opens again.

Such movements are not in a particular direction — the leaf folds the same way whichever side is touched — and NCERT calls them the plant's immediate response to stimulus. ◆ Coaching name: nastic movement, and this one is thigmonasty (thigmo = touch); flowers opening by day and closing at night are photonasty.

(b) Movement due to growth — tropisms

A pea tendril meets a stick and slowly coils round it; a seedling on a windowsill leans towards the glass; roots go down and shoots go up however you plant the seed. These are slow, take hours or days, and are directional: towards or away from the stimulus. They happen because one side of the organ grows faster than the other — in the tendril, the cells on the side away from the point of contact grow faster, so it bends around the support.

Definition

A tropism (tropic movement) is a growth movement of a plant part in a direction determined by the stimulus. Positive = towards the stimulus; negative = away from it.

Nastic vs tropic — the one distinction this section is for

Ask two questions of any plant movement: Is it growth? and Does its direction depend on where the stimulus came from? Touch-me-not: no and no — not a tropism, however much the word “touch” tempts you. Tendril: yes and yes — a tropism (thigmotropism). A question that lists “folding of Mimosa leaves” among tropisms is testing exactly this.

8Plant hormones — how a shoot knows which way the light is

A tropism is a growth difference between two sides of a stem. Something must tell one side to grow faster. That something is a hormone called auxin, and the experiment below is the one that gets drawn in questions.

  1. Auxin is synthesised at the tip of the shoot.
  2. When light falls on one side, the auxin diffuses towards the shady side of the shoot.
  3. Auxin makes cells grow longer — so the shaded side elongates more than the lit side.
  4. A stem longer on one side bends towards the other: the shoot bends towards the light.

Which part of the shoot actually sees the light?

The tip — not the part that bends. That is worth saying on its own, because it is the whole content of the classic experiment (and of worked question 4). Cover the tip with a little opaque cap and the shoot grows straight up even in one-sided light: auxin is still made, but nothing tells it which side is dark. Leave the tip bare and wrap an opaque sleeve round the stem lower down, and the shoot still bends — the bending region never needed to see anything. Cut the tip off altogether and there is no auxin at all, so again no bend. ◆ The experiment is Darwin’s; NCERT gives the auxin mechanism but not the cap-and-sleeve test.

So a tropism is not the plant “sensing and deciding”; it is a hormone piling up on one side and growth following it. ◆ In roots the same auxin inhibits elongation, which is why a root bends the other way (away from light, towards gravity).

The “which one inhibits” question

Auxin, gibberellin and cytokinin all promote something (elongation, stem growth, cell division). Abscisic acid is the odd one out — it inhibits growth and causes wilting. If a question says “growth inhibitor” or “wilting of leaves”, the answer is abscisic acid, and if it says “cell division” it is cytokinin, not auxin.

Story 1 — adrenaline: the fear response as a sequence (2017-E Q35)

A scared person on the seventh floor feels the tremors and runs down the stairs. Which hormone started it? Adrenaline, from the adrenal glands, secreted directly into the blood and carried to every part of the body. What it does, in order:

  1. The heart beats faster → more oxygen reaches the muscles.
  2. Small arteries to the digestive system and skin contract → blood is diverted away from them and towards the skeletal muscles. (This is the “which does NOT happen” trap: blood to the gut goes down, not up.)
  3. The breathing rate rises — the diaphragm and rib muscles contract faster.
  4. Together the body is ready to run or fight; nerves made you jump in the first instant, adrenaline keeps you going for minutes.

The distractors in 2017 were insulin, glucagon and gastrin — a sugar hormone, its opposite, and a ◆ digestive hormone from the stomach. None has anything to do with fear.

Story 2 — growth hormone

The pituitary, a pea-sized gland hanging below the hypothalamus, secretes growth hormone, which regulates growth and development. A child who makes too little of it grows up very short — dwarfism — with a body that is small but in proportion.

Story 3 — thyroxine and why salt has iodine in it

The thyroid, in the front of the neck, makes thyroxine, which sets the rate at which the body uses carbohydrates, proteins and fats (its metabolism). To make thyroxine the thyroid needs iodine. If the diet has too little iodine, the gland cannot keep up, enlarges trying, and the neck swells: goitre. That is why iodised salt is compulsory in India — about 150 micrograms a day is all an adult needs.

Story 4 — insulin, and how a hormone knows when to stop (feedback)

Sugar arrives in the blood after a meal. Insulin from the pancreas makes the body's cells take glucose in (and the liver store it), so the level falls back. If the pancreas makes too little insulin, sugar stays high in the blood — diabetes — and the patient injects insulin instead. NCERT's point is the feedback: a hormone must be released in exactly the right amount at exactly the right time, and the way the body manages that is to let the effect of the hormone regulate its own release.

◆ The two “shared with Excretion” items — ADH and renin (2017-E Q38, 2018-S Q43)

ADH (anti-diuretic hormone; “diuretic” = something that makes you pass urine, so anti- = the opposite) is made in the hypothalamus and released by the pituitary when the body is short of water. It makes the kidney's collecting tubules reabsorb more water back into the blood, so less urine is made. More ADH → less urine. Alcohol and cold weather suppress it — which is why both send you to the toilet.
Renin is an enzyme made in the kidney that helps raise blood pressure; rennin (two n's) is a stomach enzyme that curdles milk in infants; aldosterone is an adrenal hormone; ADH is hypothalamus/pituitary. So “produced in the kidneys” = renin, and nothing else on the list.

Pulvinus ◆ — the swollen leaf-stalk base that folds the touch-me-not.
Cretinism ◆ — thyroxine deficiency from infancy: stunted growth.
Gigantism ◆ — too much growth hormone in childhood.
Goitre — swollen thyroid from iodine deficiency.
Dwarfism — growth-hormone deficiency in childhood.
Diabetes — insulin deficiency; blood sugar stays high.
Feedback — the hormone's effect controls its own release.
ADH ◆ — anti-diuretic hormone; more ADH → less urine.

11Eight worked questions — at TALLENTEX pitch

Try each before opening the working; give yourself 60 seconds — Biology should run faster than the 90-second paper average. The elimination is written out because in this chapter the wrong options are always the neighbouring word, and ruling two out is worth the guess.

1Diagram · reflex arc · ~60 s

The figure shows the path of a nerve impulse when a boy's finger touches a hot iron.

Which statement about the labelled parts is correct?

1Q is a motor neuron and S is a sensory neuron.
2The impulse crosses from Q to S in the brain, after the brain has processed the information.
3R is a relay neuron in the grey matter of the spinal cord, and the impulse crosses two synapses between Q and S.
4T is a receptor and P is an effector.
Working

Read the arrows before the letters: they run P → Q → R → S → T. So P (at the fingertip, where the stimulus is) is the receptor, Q carries the impulse towards the cord — a sensory neuron; R sits inside the butterfly-shaped grey matter — the relay neuron; S carries it away to the muscle — a motor neuron; T is the muscle, the effector. Q → R is one synapse and R → S is a second, both in the spinal cord. Option (3).

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) — the names swapped: sensory goes in, motor goes out; the arrows settle it. (2) — the whole point of a reflex arc is that the crossing happens in the spinal cord, before the brain has processed anything. (4) — P and T swapped; the stimulus is at P, so P detects and T responds. Elimination. (2) contradicts the definition of a reflex and goes first. (1) and (4) are each a straight swap of a pair — check one arrow and both fall.
2Match the following · brain · ~75 s

Match Column I with Column II and choose the correct option.

Column IColumn II
ACerebellum(i)Blood pressure, salivation, vomiting
BMedulla(ii)Hunger, thirst, body temperature
CHypothalamus(iii)Thinking, memory, voluntary actions
DCerebrum(iv)Posture, balance, precision of movements
1A-(iii), B-(i), C-(ii), D-(iv)
2A-(iv), B-(i), C-(ii), D-(iii)
3A-(iv), B-(ii), C-(i), D-(iii)
4A-(i), B-(iv), C-(ii), D-(iii)
Working

Cerebellum = balance and precision (iv); medulla = the involuntary maintenance jobs (i); hypothalamus = hunger, thirst, temperature (ii); cerebrum = thinking (iii). Option (2).

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) — cerebrum and cerebellum swapped (the classic). (3) — medulla and hypothalamus swapped: both are “involuntary”, but hunger and temperature are the hypothalamus, vomiting and blood pressure the medulla. (4) — cerebellum and medulla swapped. Elimination. Fix the one pair you are surest of — cerebrum = thinking (iii) — and (1) is gone. Then medulla = (i) removes (3) and (4) together. You never needed the hypothalamus row.
3Three-student statement · synapse · ~90 s

Three students described what happens when a nerve impulse reaches the end of a neuron.

  • Aarav: The electrical impulse jumps across the synapse directly, because the nerve ending of one neuron touches the dendrite of the next.
  • Bhavna: The electrical impulse causes chemicals to be released at the nerve ending; these diffuse across the gap and start a new electrical impulse in the dendrite of the next neuron.
  • Chirag: The same kind of junction is used to deliver the impulse from a neuron to a muscle cell, and the message can pass across a synapse in either direction.

Which of the students is/are correct?

1Only Aarav
2Only Bhavna
3Bhavna and Chirag
4All three
Working

Bhavna has NCERT word for word. Aarav has two errors in one sentence — the neurons do not touch, and an electrical impulse cannot cross a gap. Chirag's first half is true (neuron → muscle uses a similar synapse) but his second half is false: the chemicals are made only at the nerve-ending side, so the crossing is one-way. A statement with one false clause is a false statement. Option (2).

The trap is Chirag. A two-clause statement whose first clause is true lures you into (3). Read every clause; in a statement question the paper is asking 4–6 small questions for one 4-mark answer, and “either direction” is the one that costs you. (1) and (4) die with Aarav's “touches”.
4Diagram · phototropism experiment · ~90 s

Four identical seedlings are lit from the right for two days.

Which of the seedlings will bend towards the light?

1I only
2I and III
3I and IV
4I, II and IV
Working

Bending needs two things from section 8: auxin must be made (at the tip) and the tip must see which side the light is on. I has both — it bends. II has no tip, so no auxin — no bending. III has a tip but it is in the dark under the cap, so auxin is made but not pushed to one side — it grows straight. IV has its tip exposed; the sleeve covers only the region that bends, but the bending region does not need to see the light — the tip does. It bends. I and IV: option (3).

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) — assuming any covering stops the response; the sleeve in IV is below the sensing region. (2) — the mirror-image mistake: putting the light-detector in the wrong place, so that the part which bends is the part that must see the light. Then IV’s sleeve blocks it and III’s cap does not matter, because auxin is still made under the cap — which gives I and III. (4) — including II: without a tip there is no auxin at all, and a stem with no auxin does not bend. Elimination. II can never bend (no tip → no auxin), which kills (4) at once. Then the whole question is III versus IV — “where is the light detected?” — and the answer is the tip.
5Assertion–Reason · plant movement · ~60 s

Assertion (A): The folding of the leaves of the touch-me-not plant when touched is not a tropic movement.
Reason (R): The folding is caused by cells changing the amount of water in them, involves no growth, and happens the same way whichever side of the leaf is touched.

1Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
2Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A.
3A is true but R is false.
4A is false but R is true.
Working

A tropism is a growth movement in a direction set by the stimulus. The touch-me-not's response is neither growth nor directional, so A is true. R states exactly those two facts plus the mechanism (water leaving cells), all correct — and those facts are precisely why it is not a tropism. So R explains A. Option (1).

How to handle A–R in 60 s. First decide A and R separately (both true here — (3) and (4) go). Then ask “if I were explaining A to a friend, would I say R?” — yes, that is the whole argument. (2) is the trap for a student who knows both facts but has not connected them: the definition of a tropism is the link.
6“Which does NOT happen” · adrenaline · ~60 s

While walking home, Riya sees a snake on the path and freezes, her heart thumping. Which of the following is not true of the hormone responsible for her response?

1Her heart beats faster, so more oxygen reaches her muscles.
2Her breathing rate increases because the diaphragm and rib muscles contract faster.
3The small arteries to her digestive system and skin widen, so more blood flows to them.
4The hormone reaches her heart through the blood, having been secreted directly into it by the adrenal glands.
Working

The hormone is adrenaline. Walk the sequence in section 9: heart faster (1 ✓), breathing faster (2 ✓), secreted straight into the blood by the adrenals (4 ✓) — and the arteries to the gut and skin contract, so blood is diverted away from them to the skeletal muscles. Option (3) says the opposite. Option (3).

The trap. Every option is written in the confident tone of a true fact, and (3) even names the right organs. The single word that flips it is “widen”. In a NOT question, read each option looking for the one verb that is reversed — that is where the paper hides it.
7Three patients · hormone deficiencies · ~90 s

A doctor sees three patients on the same morning.

  • P — a 14-year-old boy far shorter than his classmates, though his body is in proportion.
  • Q — a woman from a hill village with a large, painless swelling at the front of her neck.
  • R — a man whose blood sugar stays high after meals and who has been prescribed daily injections.

The glands whose hormones are deficient in P, Q and R respectively are

1thyroid, pituitary, pancreas
2pituitary, thyroid, adrenal
3adrenal, thyroid, pancreas
4pituitary, thyroid, pancreas
Working

P: short but in proportion = too little growth hormone in childhood → pituitary. Q: swelling in the front of the neck from a hill region (where soil and food are low in iodine) = goitre → thyroid. R: high blood sugar treated with injections = diabetes, too little insulin → pancreas. Option (4).

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) — P and Q's glands swapped. (2) — R given to the adrenal gland (adrenaline is the fear hormone, not the sugar one). (3) — P given to the adrenal gland. Elimination. R is the surest: injections + sugar = insulin = pancreas, which removes (2). Then Q's neck swelling = thyroid removes (1). Only (3) and (4) remain and they differ only on P — and nothing about height has anything to do with the adrenals.
8Correctly paired · tropisms and hormones · ~90 s

Consider the following pairs.

  1. A pollen tube growing towards the ovule — chemotropism
  2. The root of a seedling growing downward — negative geotropism
  3. A plant hormone that promotes cell division in fruits and seeds — cytokinin
  4. The plant hormone responsible for the wilting of leaves — gibberellin
  5. A tendril coiling around a support — cells on the side away from the support grow faster

Which of the pairs are correctly matched?

1(a), (b) and (c)
2(a), (c) and (e)
3(b), (d) and (e)
4(a), (c), (d) and (e)
Working

(a) ✓ — NCERT's chemotropism example. (b) ✗ — a root growing towards gravity is positive geotropism; the shoot is the negative one. (c) ✓ — cytokinins, greatest where cells divide fastest. (d) ✗ — wilting is abscisic acid; gibberellins promote stem growth. (e) ✓ — NCERT's own explanation of the tendril. So (a), (c), (e): option (2).

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) — accepting (b): the sign of a tropism is the most-flipped word in the chapter — say it as “towards = positive” every time. (3) — accepting both (b) and (d). (4) — accepting (d), i.e. attaching wilting to the wrong hormone. Elimination. (d) is the quickest kill — the one inhibitor in the chapter is abscisic acid — which removes (3) and (4). Between (1) and (2) the only difference is (b) vs (e); “root down = positive” settles it.
