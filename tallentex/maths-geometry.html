Lines, Angles, Quadrilaterals & Triangles

Official topics: Lines and Angles (Class 9), Quadrilaterals (Class 9) and Triangles (Class 10) — three topics, one card. Your IGCSE course already has the angle rules, the special quadrilaterals, similarity and Pythagoras, so this page is about NCERT's names for them and the few machines Allen builds out of them. New to you: the basic proportionality theorem by name, the mid-point theorem, the altitude-on-the-hypotenuse results, the angle-bisector shortcuts, and the area-ratio tricks Allen asks in four of the five papers. A 30-minute read, then the paper.

1Lines and angles — five facts, three shortcuts

You know every rule here from IGCSE; what changes is the names — NCERT's words appear in Allen's statement items — and the shortcuts that turn a three-line chase into one line.

NCERT's vocabulary

  • Linear pair — two adjacent angles whose outer arms form a line; they add to 180° (NCERT's linear pair axiom). Vertically opposite angles are equal.
  • Complementary — add to 90°. Supplementary — add to 180°. Reflex — between 180° and 360°.
  • For two lines cut by a transversal: corresponding angles (same position at each crossing), alternate interior angles (between the lines, opposite sides of the transversal — your "Z"), and interior angles on the same side of the transversal (your "co-interior", "C" or "U").
  • If the lines are parallel: corresponding angles are equal, alternate interior angles are equal, and interior angles on the same side add to 180°. Each of the three converses is true too — any one of those facts proves the lines parallel.
  • Lines parallel to the same line are parallel to each other.

2018-S Q50 in one line. In its figure, x + 50° sits below ℓ₁ to the left of the transversal and y − 20° above ℓ₂ to the right — alternate interior angles, so x + 50 = y − 20, i.e. y = x + 70. Complementary means x + y = 90: x + x + 70 = 90 → x = 10°, y = 80°. The trap is to call them co-interior and add them to 180°; read which side of each line the arc is on before choosing the rule.

Sample Q49 in one line each. The line D–G–C–B cuts the parallels GE and AF. ∠DGE = 95° and ∠GCF are corresponding, so ∠GCF = 95°; x = ∠ACB is vertically opposite ∠GCF, so x = 95°; y = ∠FCB makes a linear pair with ∠GCF, so y = 85°; and in △ABC, z = 180° − 35° − 95° = 50°. Three unknowns, three different facts — the figure is worth copying onto your rough work.

The hub's Maths subject paper uses the first one. It is ∠A that gets halved, although A is the one vertex whose angle is not bisected — so find ∠A first if the question gives B and C. The third row iterates: if the interior bisector of B and the exterior bisector of C meet at T (∠BTC = ½A), do the same in △TBC — its exterior angle at C lies on the same extended line — and the new meeting point T₁ has ∠BT₁C = ½ × ½A = ¼A. With A = 80°: 40°, then 20°.

Triangle angles — the exterior angle and the isosceles double answer

Exterior angle = sum of the two interior opposite angles (NCERT's name). It is the fastest route whenever an extended side appears in a figure.

When an isosceles triangle has two answers

"An isosceles triangle has an angle of k°" gives two triangles whenever k < 90 and k ≠ 60: k is the apex (base angles (180 − k)/2 each) or k is a base angle (apex 180 − 2k). If k ≥ 90 it can only be the apex — two base angles of 90° or more would already reach 180°. Before choosing a single number, ask whether the stem said which angle. If an option says "either" or "both", that is usually why it is there.

2Triangles — similarity, the BPT, and the right-angle family

NCERT's Class 10 "Triangles" is about similar triangles: the same shape, not necessarily the same size. Congruence is the Class 9 chapter; it is not on the 2027 list by name, but Allen's statement items use its rules as tools, so they are here too.

The criteria, by NCERT's names

  • Similar (△ABC ~ △DEF): corresponding angles equal and corresponding sides in the same ratio. For triangles, either one forces the other.
  • AAA (NCERT's name; since the angle sum is fixed, two equal angles are enough: AA). SSS: all three pairs of sides in the same ratio. SAS: one equal angle between two pairs of proportional sides.
  • Congruent — the same shape and size. Criteria: SAS, ASA, AAS, SSS, RHS. There is no SSA and no AAA congruence: AAA only gives similarity. Equal areas never prove congruence.
  • Write the letters in matching order. △ABC ~ △DEF means A↔D, B↔E, C↔F, so AB/DE = BC/EF = CA/FD. Most wrong ratios come from a correspondence written in the wrong order.

The basic proportionality theorem (BPT) — and the ratio that is not the similarity ratio

Two ratios, two jobs

  • AD : DB (part : part) is the BPT ratio — it transfers to AE : EC. AD : AB (part : whole) is the similarity ratio — it gives DE : BC. Using 3 : 4 for DE : BC is the commonest wrong option.
  • Areas of similar triangles go as the square of the ratio of sides: ar(ADE) : ar(ABC) = 9 : 49. (The rationalised Class 10 chapter dropped this result; Allen and the hub's subject paper still use it.) The trapezium DBCE is the remaining 40 parts.

Pythagoras, its converse, and the triples that save time

In a right triangle, hypotenuse² = sum of the squares of the other two sides. Converse: if a² + b² = c² for the three sides, the angle opposite c is 90° — the test for "is this triangle right-angled?". Know these by sight, with their multiples: 3-4-5 · 5-12-13 · 8-15-17 · 7-24-25 · 9-40-41 · 20-21-29. For an equilateral triangle of side a: altitude (√3/2)a, area (√3/4)a².

The right-angle family — the altitude to the hypotenuse

AD² = BD · DC
The altitude is the geometric mean of the two pieces. This is 2024-A Q60's key.
AB² = BD · BC
A leg² = (its own piece) × (the whole hypotenuse). The piece next to that leg.
AC² = CD · CB
The same rule for the other leg — with DC, not BD. Swapping them is the planted error.
AB · AC = BC · AD
Twice the area, computed two ways (leg × leg, or base × altitude).

Four more similarity tools

Altitudes go as the sides
In similar triangles, corresponding altitudes, medians and angle bisectors are all in the ratio of the sides (areas: its square).
Square on the base: x = bh/(b + h)
A square standing on a triangle's base b (height h) cuts off a similar triangle of base x and height h − x: x/b = (h − x)/h. With b = 12, h = 6: x = 72/18 = 4.
Lamp and shadow: tip at dH/(H − h)
Lamp height H, child of height h standing d from its foot. The shadow tip s from the foot: h/H = (s − d)/s. H = 6, h = 1.5, d = 3 → s = 18/4.5 = 4, shadow 1.
BPT twice: AD² = AF · AB
DE ∥ BC (D on AB, E on AC) and FE ∥ DC (F on AD): AF/AD = AE/AC = AD/AB. AF = 9, AB = 16 → AD = 12.

3Area ratios — no area is ever measured

Four of the eleven real items are area-ratio items (the fourth, 2024-A Q52, is the similar-triangle one worked in section 2). Four rules settle them, and a ratio never needs a height to be found.

Same height → areas ∝ bases
Two triangles with their bases on one line and the same apex: ar ratio = base ratio.
A median halves the area
ar(ABD) = ar(ADC) when D is the midpoint of BC. A diagonal halves a parallelogram.
Same base, same parallels
Triangles on one base between the same parallels have equal area; a triangle and a parallelogram so placed: the triangle is half.
Similar → ratio of sides, squared
Only for similar figures. Do not square a same-height ratio.

2018-S Q51 (median AD, AP : PD = 1 : 2): ar(ABD) = ½ ar(ABC), and △ABP takes the AP share of it, ⅓. So ar(ABP) = ⅓ × ½ = ⅙. The option ⅓ is the answer with the median step forgotten.

2017-E Q51 (C on BD with BC : CD = 2 : 3; E on AC with AE : EC = 3 : 4): ar(ABC) = ⅖ of the whole and ar(ACD) = ⅗. Then ar(AEB) = 3⁄7 × ⅖ = 6⁄35 and ar(ECD) = 4⁄7 × ⅗ = 12⁄35, so ar(ECD) : ar(AEB) = 12 : 6 = 2 : 1. Its third given, BC : AE = 2 : 3, compares a length on one line with a length on another and is never used: a given you cannot use is allowed — do not bend the working to fit it in.

Sample Q50 (parallelogram ABCD, E the midpoint of AD): the diagonal BD gives ar(ABD) = ½ ar(ABCD), and BE is a median of △ABD, so ar(BED) = ¼ ar(ABCD).

4Quadrilaterals — the parallelogram theorems and the mid-point theorem

Angle sum 360°, always. Everything else is about the parallelogram family, which NCERT builds as a chain of theorems each with a converse. Allen's statement items test exactly which ones are true.

A quadrilateral is a parallelogram if (any one of these)

  • both pairs of opposite sides are parallel (the definition);
  • both pairs of opposite sides are equal;
  • both pairs of opposite angles are equal;
  • its diagonals bisect each other;
  • one pair of opposite sides is equal and parallel.

And in any parallelogram: a diagonal splits it into two congruent triangles, and adjacent angles add to 180° (interior angles on the same side), so the bisectors of two adjacent angles meet at 90°.

Read the table both ways: a parallelogram with equal diagonals is a rectangle; one with perpendicular diagonals is a rhombus; both, a square. A rectangle's diagonals are not perpendicular unless it is a square — the classic false statement. Rhombus area = ½ × d₁ × d₂ (it is two triangles on one diagonal), and like any parallelogram its height = area ÷ side.

Bisector + parallel side → isosceles
In parallelogram ABCD, the bisector of ∠A meets DC at E. ∠DEA = ∠EAB (alternate) = ∠DAE, so DE = DA. Two such bisectors give two isosceles triangles.
Trapezium midline = ½(a + b)
Joins the midpoints of the legs, parallel to the parallel sides a > b. The diagonals cut it into b/2 · ½(a − b) · b/2. With a = 10, b = 4: 7 = 2 + 3 + 2.

The mid-point theorem — and what the midpoints of any quadrilateral make

Mid-point theorem: the segment joining the midpoints of two sides of a triangle is parallel to the third side and half of it. Converse: a line through the midpoint of one side, parallel to a second side, bisects the third. (It is the BPT with ratio 1 : 1.)

Which shape do the midpoints make?

It depends only on the diagonals of the outer shape, never on its sides. Rectangle (equal diagonals) → rhombus. Rhombus (perpendicular diagonals) → rectangle. Square → square. Kite (perpendicular diagonals) → rectangle. The answer "the same shape again" is the trap.

5Fact bank — everything above on one screen

∥ lines: corr = alt, co-int = 180°
Two angle sizes at every crossing, and they add to 180°. The converses prove lines parallel.
Bend: ∠APC = ∠BAP + ∠DCP
Draw a parallel through the bend. Many bends: one side's bends add to the other side's.
Parallel arms → equal or supplementary
Equal if both arms point the same way (2024-U Q60); 180° if one arm is reversed.
Exterior ∠ = sum of interior opposites
Isosceles with an angle k < 90°: two triangles — k at the apex or at the base.
∠BOC = 90° + ½A

6Five worked questions — at TALLENTEX pitch

Try each before opening the working and give yourself 90 seconds — the real paper's average. The elimination reasoning is written out because on the day, ruling two options out is worth almost as much as solving.

1The bend, then the bisectors · ~75 seconds

In the figure, AB ∥ CD and ∠APC = 110°. The bisectors of ∠BAP and ∠DCP meet at Q. What is ∠AQC?

155°
2110°
3125°
4145°
Working

Step 1 — the bend at P. ∠APC = ∠BAP + ∠DCP, so ∠BAP + ∠DCP = 110°. You are never told the two angles separately, and you do not need them.

Step 2 — Q is another bend. Q also lies between the parallels, joined to A and C, so the same rule gives ∠AQC = ∠BAQ + ∠DCQ = ½∠BAP + ½∠DCP = ½ × 110° = 55°. Option (1).

Where each wrong option comes from. (2) 110° — the bend rule applied to Q with the halving forgotten. (3) 125° — treating A, Q, C as a triangle whose angles at A and C are the two halves: 180° − 55°. Those halves are measured from AB and CD, not from AC, so they are not the triangle's angles. (4) 145° — the "90° + ½A" bisector result, which belongs to a triangle's bisectors, not to a bend. Elimination. Each of the two angles that make up ∠AQC is half of one that makes up ∠APC, so ∠AQC must be smaller than 110°: every option ≥ 110° is out, leaving only (1).
2BPT, then a same-height ratio · ~90 seconds

In △ABC, D lies on AB and E on AC with DE ∥ BC, and AD : DB = 3 : 2. If ar(△ADE) = 18 cm², then ar(△DEC) is

18 cm²
212 cm²
327 cm²
432 cm²
Working

Step 1 — BPT moves the ratio across. DE ∥ BC, so AE : EC = AD : DB = 3 : 2.

Step 2 — same apex, bases on one line. △ADE and △DEC share the apex D, and their bases AE and EC lie along AC, so they have the same height from D. Their areas go as the bases: ar(DEC) = 18 × 2/3 = 12 cm². Option (2).

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) 8 — squaring the ratio, 18 × (2/3)²: the square law is for similar triangles, and △ADE and △DEC are not similar. (3) 27 — the ratio turned upside down, 18 × 3/2. (4) 32 — the trapezium DBCE: ar(ABC) = 18 × 25/9 = 50, minus 18. A true number, but the wrong region. Elimination. △DEC is only part of the trapezium, so it is less than 32; its base EC is shorter than AE on the same height, so it is less than 18. Only 8 and 12 survive, and the square law does not apply.
32024-A Q60 machine, as a statement item · ~90 seconds

In △ABC, ∠BAC = 90° and AD ⊥ BC, with D on BC. Consider:
I. AD² = BD × DC    II. AB² = BD × BC    III. AC² = BD × BC    IV. AB × AC = AD × BC
Which statements are correct?

1I and II only
2I, II and III only
3I, II and IV only
4I, II, III and IV
Working

Step 1 — test with numbers, not proofs. Take the figure in section 2: BD = 4, DC = 9, BC = 13, AD = 6, AB² = 52, AC² = 117.

Step 2 — check each. I: 36 = 4 × 9 ✓. II: 52 = 4 × 13 ✓. III: 117 ≠ 4 × 13 = 52 ✗ — the leg AC pairs with its own piece DC, and 9 × 13 = 117. IV: AB × AC = √52 × √117 = √6084 = 78 and AD × BC = 6 × 13 = 78 ✓ (both are twice the area). So I, II and IV — option (3).

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) — IV not recognised as "twice the area, two ways". (2) — both mistakes at once: III accepted and IV missed. (4) — III accepted because it looks symmetric with II. Elimination. I and II are in every option, so spend no time on them: only III and IV decide it. One numerical example settles both in 30 seconds.
4Rhombus, diagonals, then the mid-point theorem · ~2 minutes

ABCD is a rhombus of perimeter 40 cm with ∠ABC = 120°. P, Q, R, S are the midpoints of AB, BC, CD, DA. The area of PQRS is

125 cm²
225√3 cm²
350√3 cm²
4100√3 cm²
Working

Step 1 — the diagonals. Side = 40/4 = 10. Adjacent angles add to 180°, so ∠BAD = 60°, which makes △ABD equilateral: BD = 10. Half of AC is the altitude of that equilateral triangle, 5√3, so AC = 10√3.

Step 2 — the midpoint figure. PQ = ½AC = 5√3 and QR = ½BD = 5, and since the rhombus's diagonals are perpendicular, PQRS is a rectangle: 5√3 × 5 = 25√3 cm². Option (2). (Check: half of ar(ABCD) = ½ × ½ × 10 × 10√3 = 25√3 ✓.)

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) 25 — assuming the midpoints make a square of side 5 ("half the side"). (3) 50√3 — the rhombus itself. (4) 100√3 — d₁ × d₂ with the ½ dropped. Elimination. The midpoint figure is always half the outer area — so find ar(ABCD) any way you can and halve it. Two of the options are the rhombus and twice the rhombus.
5An area chain with a given you do not need · ~75 seconds

In △ABC, D is a point on BC with BD : DC = 3 : 2, and E is a point on AD with AE : ED = 1 : 3. If ar(△ABC) = 80 cm², then ar(△BEC) is

120 cm²
236 cm²
348 cm²
460 cm²
Working

Step 1 — split along AD. ar(ABD) = ⅗ × 80 = 48 and ar(ACD) = 32.

Step 2 — same apex on each side. △BED is the ED share of △BAD: ¾ × 48 = 36. △CED is the ED share of △CAD: ¾ × 32 = 24. So ar(BEC) = 36 + 24 = 60 cm² — which is simply ¾ × 80. Option (4). The 3 : 2 cancelled out: the figure in section 3 shows why.

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) 20 — the AE share, ¼ × 80: that is ar(ABE) + ar(ACE), the region outside △BEC. (2) 36 — only △BED, with △CED forgotten. (3) 48 — ar(ABD), the first step mistaken for the answer. Elimination. E is near A (only a quarter of the way down AD), so △BEC is most of the triangle — more than half of 80. Only 48 and 60 survive, and 48 does not use E at all.
