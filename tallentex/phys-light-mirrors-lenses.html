Light: Mirrors & Lenses

Official topics: Reflection of Light and Refraction of Light (Class 10). This is the biggest single block of physics on the whole 2027 list — and almost all of the machinery is new to you. Your IGCSE course teaches plane mirrors, refraction and lens ray diagrams, but it has no spherical mirrors, no sign convention, no mirror or lens formula and no lens power at all. That missing machinery is exactly what Allen asks. Read it once now — about 50 minutes — and once the night before.

  1. Four of the fifteen Physics questions, every time. Each of the three recent papers — both 2024 sittings and the sample — carries exactly four. That is 16 marks a paper — among the largest shares of any unit in Physics.
  2. The 2017 and 2018 papers contain not one light question between them. 1The words Allen points at — pole, focus, centre of curvature

A spherical mirror is a slice cut from a silvered sphere. Everything in this chapter is measured from one point on it, so learn these names before anything else — Allen has pointed at them on a drawing and asked what they are called (2024-A Q2).

Where NCERT starts — straight lines and two laws

  • How we see. An object reflects the light that falls on it, and that light entering our eyes lets us see it — which is why nothing is visible in a dark room. A transparent medium lets light through; a highly polished surface, such as a mirror, reflects most of it.
  • Light seems to travel in straight lines. NCERT's evidence: a small source casts a sharp shadow of an opaque object. That straight path is what a ray of light is.
  • The two laws of reflection — (i) the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection; (ii) the incident ray, the normal at the point of incidence and the reflected ray all lie in the same plane. They hold for every reflecting surface, curved ones included — which is why the ray rules of section 3 work.
  • The image in a plane mirror, NCERT's list: always virtual and erect; the same size as the object; as far behind the mirror as the object is in front; and laterally inverted (left and right swapped).

The chapter's first "More to Know" box adds one caution: when an obstacle is very small, light bends round it instead of going straight — diffraction — and rays stop working. Light is then treated as a wave, and the modern quantum theory treats it as neither simply a wave nor simply a particle. NCERT only mentions this; nothing else in the chapter uses it.

The five names, in NCERT's words

  • Pole (P) — the centre of the mirror's reflecting surface. Every mirror distance is measured from here (for a lens, from the optical centre O — section 2).
  • Centre of curvature (C) — the centre of the sphere the mirror was cut from. Not a point on the mirror.
  • Radius of curvature (R) — the distance PC.
  • Principal focus (F) — where rays parallel to the axis actually meet (concave), or appear to come from (convex). It sits exactly halfway between C and P.
  • Focal length (f) — the distance PF. NCERT's relation is R = 2f, so f = R/2 — true for mirrors whose aperture is small compared with R, which is every mirror in this chapter.

NCERT defines two more words in the same paragraphs. The principal axis is the straight line through the pole and the centre of curvature; it is the normal to the mirror at its pole. The aperture is the diameter of the mirror's circular reflecting surface — the distance across its rim.

The trap Allen actually plants here

In 2024-A Q2 the wrong options did not offer silly words — they offered "Y → focal length, Z → radius of curvature" and "X → optical centre". That is, they swapped a point for a distance, and a mirror's word for a lens's word. F and C are places; f and R are lengths; the optical centre belongs to a lens, never to a mirror. Read the option to the end before you tick it.

Two things NCERT does with a curved mirror before any formula

  • The spoon (Activity 9.1). The inside of a shiny spoon is a concave mirror: held close it shows your face enlarged and erect; move it away and the image turns upside down. The back of the spoon is a convex mirror: your face is erect and smaller at every distance.
  • Burning paper (Activity 9.2). Point a concave mirror at the Sun and move a sheet of paper until the bright spot on it is sharpest. The spot is a real image of the Sun, formed at the principal focus, and it is hot enough to char the paper. So the distance from mirror to paper is the approximate focal length — NCERT's quick way to measure f, and it works with any distant object, not only the Sun. (Never look at the Sun, or at its reflection, to do it.)

Before the curved ones: the plane-mirror rules

Two of the twelve real questions — Sample Q6 and Q7, eight marks — are about an ordinary flat mirror. Your IGCSE course has the law of reflection and plane-mirror calculations, which settle Q7 once you convert one angle; Q6 needs a rule it never states. Rule 1 below is that rule, and Rule 2 is the angle conversion. beyond NCERT NCERT itself stops at the list of image properties above. The double-speed rule is Allen's (Sample Q6); the two-mirror rule and the half-height mirror in Rule 3 are standard competitive-paper results, which this unit's Paper 1 uses.

Rule 1 — a mirror image closes on you at double speed

A plane mirror puts the image as far behind the mirror as the object is in front, same size, erect, virtual, left–right reversed. So if you walk towards it at v, your image walks towards it at v too — from the other side. You and it therefore approach each other at 2v.

Sample Q6: you run at a mirror at 15 m/s, so the image's speed relative to you is 2 × 15 = 30 m/s. The trap option is 15 — the image's speed relative to the mirror, which is not what was asked.

Rule 2 — angles are measured from the normal, so subtract from 90°

The laws of reflection (i = r, measured from the normal) are the easy half. The marks are lost converting: the angle a ray makes with the mirror is 90° minus the angle it makes with the normal.

Sample Q7: a mirror is tilted 30° to the horizontal and a vertical ray hits it. The normal is perpendicular to the mirror, so it sits 30° from the vertical — which makes i = 30°, and so r = 30°. The question asks for the angle between the mirror and the reflected ray: 90° − 30° = 60°. Answering 30° — the angle from the normal — is the whole trap, and 30° is one of the four options.

Rule 3 — three more plane-mirror facts worth having

  • Two mirrors at an angle θ: draw the triangle made by the corner and the two points where the ray strikes. Its angles are θ at the corner and (90° − i) at each mirror, and they add to 180°, so i₁ + i₂ = θ. That holds in the usual set-up — the ray reflected off the first mirror travels towards the corner, with i₁ < θ. Otherwise one of those angles is (90° + i) and the result changes, so draw the triangle rather than trust the formula. Check it on the easy case — two mirrors at 90°, hit the first at 30°, and you meet the second at 60°.
  • The shortest mirror you can see your whole self in is half your height — and that answer does not depend on how far away you stand, which is the part that gets asked. Light from your head reaches your eye off a point halfway between them, so the mirror only has to span head-to-eye and eye-to-toe at half scale.
  • A plane mirror is just a spherical mirror with R = ∞, so f = ∞ and 1/f = 0. Feed that into the mirror formula: 1/v + 1/u = 0 ⇒ v = −u — the image is exactly as far behind as the object is in front — and m = −v/u = +1, erect and same size. Rule 1 was not a separate fact after all; it falls straight out of section 4.

2The sign convention — this is the chapter

Almost every mark lost in this topic is a sign, not a formula. NCERT uses the New Cartesian Sign Convention, and it is really just one idea: put the pole (or the optical centre) at the origin, make the light travel left to right, and then read every distance off an ordinary x-axis. Right is positive. Left is negative. Up is positive. Down is negative.

The whole convention

  • The object is always placed on the left, so light travels left → right.
  • All distances are measured from the pole P (mirror) or the optical centre O (lens) — never from C, never from F.
  • Distances measured along the incoming light (to the right) are +; those measured against it (to the left) are −.
  • Heights above the principal axis are +; heights below it are −.

Two consequences do all the work: u is always negative (the object is always on the left), and an inverted image has a negative height, so a negative magnification means inverted.

The one that catches everybody

A concave mirror has a negative f, and a convex mirror has a positive f — but for lenses it is the other way round: a convex (converging) lens has a positive f and a concave (diverging) lens a negative one. The words "concave" and "convex" do not carry a sign with them. What carries the sign is which side F is on: a concave mirror focuses light back in front of itself (left, negative); a convex lens focuses it out behind (right, positive).

3Where the image goes — the two rays and the six cases

To locate an image you only ever need two rays from the top of the object, and NCERT gives you four to choose from. Two of them are enough, and these two are the easiest to draw.

The four ray rules (concave and convex mirrors alike)

  1. A ray parallel to the principal axis reflects through F (concave) or appears to come from F (convex).
  2. A ray through F (or heading towards F) reflects parallel to the axis. Rule 1 run backwards.
  3. A ray through C — or, for a convex mirror, heading towards C — hits the mirror head-on, along a radius and so along the normal, and retraces its own path.
  4. A ray striking the pole reflects at the same angle on the other side of the axis.

NCERT's Activity 9.3 is this figure done on a table: chalk lines at P, F and C, a candle, and a paper screen moved until the image is sharp. A sharp image appears on the screen for every position beyond F. With the object at F the reflected light leaves parallel, so no screen at any distance catches it; and with the object between P and F there is nothing to catch at all — the image is virtual, and you see it by looking into the mirror. That last one is the case NCERT's activity asks you to find.

You do not have to redraw this in the exam. Learn the table — it is 2024-A Q9, which handed out a concave mirror of focal length 25 cm and four object distances (15, 25, 50 and 75 cm) and asked you to match each to its image.

Object at F — NCERT and Allen's key word it differently

With the object exactly at F the reflected rays leave parallel to each other, so they never meet at any finite distance — panel (e) above. NCERT (Table 9.1) writes this row as: position of the image "at infinity", size and nature "Image would not be formed". Allen's 2024-A Q9 calls the very same case "a highly magnified real image", and that was the key. Both are describing the same parallel rays, so in an MCQ take whichever of the two the options offer — and expect only one of them to be there.

NCERT's own words for the size column are highly diminished, point-sized · diminished · same size · enlarged. "Enlarged" and "magnified" mean the same thing.

How to use that table in 40 seconds

Do not memorise six rows. Memorise the one row that breaks the pattern — the last one — and two facts: a concave mirror gives a real, inverted image for every object beyond F, and the only virtual image it can give is when the object is closer than F (that is your shaving mirror, and a dentist's). Then reconstruct the rest from the seesaw.

But read a match-the-following item before you start matching. In 2024-A Q9, f = 25, so 15 cm is inside F — the virtual, magnified row — and it is tempting to start there. It would have wasted your time: all four options already agreed that (a) is the virtual, magnified one. The rows that actually decided it were the two special positions: (b) 25 cm is exactly F, so the image is at infinity — "highly magnified real" — which killed two options, and then (c) 50 cm is exactly 2f, giving the same-size real image, which killed a third. On a matching question, find the row the options disagree about and test only that. Checking a row they all share tells you nothing.

Where concave mirrors are used — NCERT's list

  • Torches, search-lights and vehicle headlights — to get a powerful parallel beam. The bulb sits at the focus: row "at F", with the light going the other way.
  • Shaving mirrors and dentists' mirrors — the face or the tooth is closer than F, so the image is erect and enlarged.
  • Solar furnaces — a large concave mirror concentrates sunlight at its focus to produce heat.

The convex mirror only ever does one thing

Whatever you put in front of a convex mirror, wherever you put it, the image is virtual, erect, diminished and squeezed into the space between P and F, behind the mirror. That is why every vehicle in India carries one: a huge field of view, at the price of everything looking small and far away.

That is the whole of NCERT's Table 9.2 — two rows (its Fig. 9.8 draws the same two cases). Activity 9.5 has you slide a pencil away from a convex mirror: the image stays erect, gets smaller, and creeps towards F without ever reaching it.

Why the rear-view mirror is convex — and the mirror at Agra Fort

NCERT gives two reasons a convex mirror is used as the rear-view (wing) mirror of a vehicle: it always gives an erect image, though a diminished one, and it has a wider field of view because it curves outwards — the driver sees a much larger area than a plane mirror would show.

Activity 9.6 asks which mirror shows the full-length image of a tall building or tree in a small space. NCERT's answer is a small convex mirror, and its example is the one fitted in a wall of the Agra Fort facing the Taj Mahal, in which the whole Taj can be seen.

4The mirror formula and magnification

Two lines of algebra that your IGCSE course never gives you, and that TALLENTEX expects you to apply in under ninety seconds.

1/v + 1/u = 1/f
The mirror formula. Note the plus. u object distance, v image distance, f focal length — all measured from the pole, all carrying signs.
m = h′/h = −v/u
The magnification of a mirror. Note the minus. m > 0 ⇒ erect and virtual; m < 0 ⇒ inverted and real.
f = R/2
Mirrors only. f and R always share a sign, because C and F are always on the same side.

The four-step drill — do it the same way every time

  1. Write the signs down first, before any arithmetic. u = −(the number). f = −(the number) for concave, +(the number) for convex. If you were given R, halve it now.
  2. Put them in the formula and solve for the one thing missing.
  3. Read the sign of the answer. For a mirror, a negative v means the image is real and in front; a positive v means virtual and behind.
  4. Only then use m = −v/u, and check the sign of m agrees with step 3. If it does not, you have a sign error — go back, do not "adjust".

2024-U Q9 is this drill run backwards, and it is worth walking through because it is the exact shape Allen likes. A convex mirror has radius of curvature 20 cm; the image is half the size of the object; find the object distance. R = 20 for a convex mirror, so f = +10. A convex mirror only makes erect virtual images, so m = +½ — that sign is not given to you, you have to know it. Then m = −v/u gives v = −u/2, and with u = −d that is v = +d/2. Substituting: 2/d − 1/d = 1/10, so d = 10 cm. The whole question turns on knowing that m is +½ and not −½.

Three sign traps, in the order they bite

  • Being handed R and using it as f. Any distance that scales with f comes out doubled — 2024-U Q9 would give 20 cm instead of 10 — and a doubled value is an easy option for a setter to plant.
  • Dropping the minus in m = −v/u. You get the right size and the wrong nature, and "real and erect" is a combination that cannot exist for a single mirror.
  • Using the lens formula on a mirror. 1/v − 1/u instead of 1/v + 1/u. The two formulas differ by exactly one sign.

NCERT's two mirror examples, done with the four-step drill

Example 9.1. A convex rear-view mirror has a radius of curvature of 3.00 m, and a bus is 5.00 m from it. Find the position, nature and size of the image.
R = +3.00 m, so f = +1.50 m; u = −5.00 m. 1/v = 1/f − 1/u = 1/1.50 + 1/5.00 = 6.50/7.50, so v = +1.15 m — 1.15 m behind the mirror. m = −v/u = −(1.15)/(−5.00) = +0.23: virtual, erect, and 0.23 times the size of the bus.

Example 9.2. An object 4.0 cm tall stands 25.0 cm in front of a concave mirror of focal length 15.0 cm. Where must a screen go for a sharp image, and what is the image like?
u = −25.0 cm, f = −15.0 cm. 1/v = 1/f − 1/u = −1/15.0 + 1/25.0 = −2.0/75.0, so v = −37.5 cm — the screen goes 37.5 cm in front of the mirror. h′ = −(v/u) × h = −(1.5) × 4.0 = −6.0 cm: real, inverted and enlarged.

NCERT's own sentence about the formula is worth keeping: it is valid for all spherical mirrors and for all positions of the object — provided every number goes in with its sign.

5Refraction — what changes and what never does

Light bends when it enters a new medium because it changes speed there. Everything else in this half of the chapter follows from that one sentence.

What NCERT notices first — things you have already seen

  • The bottom of a tank or pond looks raised; so do letters under a thick glass slab.
  • A pencil standing partly in water looks displaced at the surface, as if broken there: the light from the part under water reaches your eye from a different direction from the light above it.
  • A lemon in a glass of water looks bigger than it is when seen from the side.
  • A coin in a bucket of water is not where it seems: try to pick it up in one go and you miss (Activity 9.7). A coin in an empty bowl, just hidden by the rim, comes back into view when water is poured in, because it now appears slightly raised (Activity 9.8).
  • A straight ink line under a glass slab looks bent at the slab's edges when the slab lies at an angle across it, does not look bent when the slab is square to the line, and looks raised when you look down from above (Activity 9.9).

Swap the water for kerosene or turpentine, or the glass for clear plastic, and the size of each effect changes. NCERT's conclusion: light changes direction when it passes obliquely from one transparent medium into another, by a different amount for each pair of media. That change of direction is refraction.

The two laws of refraction, as NCERT states them

  1. The incident ray, the refracted ray and the normal to the interface at the point of incidence all lie in the same plane.
  2. The ratio sin i / sin r is a constant — for light of a given colour and for a given pair of media. This is Snell's law, and the constant is the refractive index of the second medium with respect to the first. (NCERT adds that it holds for 0° < i < 90°.)

Two details that get turned into statement questions: the constant depends on the colour of the light as well as on the two media; and a ray along the normal (i = 0) is not bent at all.

Notation. NCERT writes the refractive index as n. Allen's papers, and our Paper 1, often write μ (mu). It is the same quantity.

n = c / v
Absolute refractive index. c = 3×10⁸ m/s in vacuum; v is the speed in the medium. n is always > 1: light is fastest in vacuum, so no material is faster. Between two materials it can speed up — from glass into water, it does.
n₂₁ = n₂/n₁ = v₁/v₂
Relative refractive index of medium 2 with respect to 1. The speeds flip over — note the subscripts. Swap the order and you get the reciprocal: n₁₂ = v₂/v₁ = 1/n₂₁.
sin i / sin r = n₂₁
Snell's law (the second law of refraction). i and r are measured from the normal.
λmedium = λair / n
Wavelength shrinks by the same factor the speed does, because frequency is fixed. beyond NCERT

What changes when light enters glass — and what does not

  • Speed: changes. It drops by a factor of n. This is the cause of everything else.
  • Wavelength: changes. It shrinks by the same factor n.
  • Frequency: NEVER changes. The source sets it; the medium cannot. Colour, which is what your eye reads, therefore does not change either.
  • Direction: changes — unless the ray arrives along the normal.

NCERT's chapter speaks only of speed and direction. Wavelength and frequency are beyond NCERT — but Allen asked exactly this in 2024.

This is 2024-U Q12, and it is a gift

You were shown a ray bending as it crossed from X into Y and asked what happened. The four options paired speed, frequency and wavelength in different combinations, and every option containing the word "frequency" was wrong on sight — because frequency never changes. One remembered sentence halves the paper in front of you — but it does not finish the question, and this is where people stop too early. The two survivors were "both the speed and wavelength decrease" and "the speed decreases and wavelength increases". To pick between them you need the second fact: the figure shows the ray bending towards the normal, so Y is denser, so the speed falls — and wavelength always falls with speed, because frequency is pinned. Answer: both decrease.

Sample Q8 has a shortcut too, but a different one — its four options are all speeds and the word "frequency" never appears. There, the lever is that water (n = 4/3) is rarer than glass (n = 3/2), so light must travel faster in water than the 2.00×10⁸ m/s you were given. Both options below that value die on sight, leaving two.

Sample Q8 shows how the speed relation is asked: given nglass = 3/2, nwater = 4/3 and a speed in glass of 2.00 × 10⁸ m/s, find the speed in water. You are not given c — you recover it: c = ng vg = 1.5 × 2×10⁸ = 3×10⁸, then vw = c / nw = 3×10⁸ ÷ (4/3) = 2.25×10⁸ m/s. Two steps, both one line. And Sample Q10 asks nothing but the ordering: where the ray bends towards the normal, it entered a denser medium — read the bends off the figure and the inequality chain writes itself. And where a ray crosses a boundary at a slant without bending at all, the two media have the same refractive index — that is where an "=" in the chain comes from.

Optically denser does not mean heavier

"Denser" and "rarer" in this chapter always mean optically denser and rarer — a comparison of refractive indices, not of mass. NCERT's example: kerosene (n = 1.44) is optically denser than water (n = 1.33), although its mass density is less. So light travels slower in kerosene than in water, and a ray passing from water into kerosene bends towards the normal. A statement such as "the medium with the greater density always has the greater refractive index" is false.

6The glass slab — Allen's favourite object

A rectangular slab refracts light twice, at the top face and the bottom face, and the two bends are equal and opposite. So the ray comes out parallel to how it went in — just shifted sideways. Three of the twelve real questions live on this one shape.

How NCERT traces the ray — Activity 9.10, with four pins

Draw the outline ABCD of a glass slab on paper. Fix two pins E and F so that the line through them meets the face AB at an angle. Look through the opposite face and fix two more pins G and H in line with the images of E and F. Take the slab away: EF meets AB at O, HG meets CD at O′, and OO′ is the path the light took inside the glass.

  • At O (air → glass, rarer → denser) the ray bends towards the normal.
  • At O′ (glass → air, denser → rarer) it bends away from the normal.
  • The two bends are equal and opposite because the faces are parallel, so the emergent ray O′H is parallel to the incident ray EO — only shifted sideways.
  • A ray that meets the slab along the normal goes straight through: no bending, no shift.

Lateral shift

d = t · sin(i − r) / cos r
Lateral shift. t = thickness of the slab, i and r the angles at the first face. Zero when i = 0, and it grows with both t and i.
L = t / cos r
beyond NCERT The distance the ray actually travels inside the slab — a different question from the shift, and asked as one. The slab is t thick straight down, but the ray crosses it at r to the normal, so its path is longer. At r = 0 it is just t.

NCERT does not give you this formula. Allen asks for it anyway.

NCERT shows the parallel-shifted ray (its Fig. 9.10) and says only that the ray is "shifted sideward slightly" — it does not name the shift and it never writes a formula. Allen calls it the lateral shift, and the formula above is beyond NCERT. 2024-U Q5 asked for a number. (i = 60°, t = 2√3 m, n = √3: Snell gives sin r = sin 60° / √3 = ½, so r = 30°, and d = 2√3 · sin 30° / cos 30° = 2√3 · (½)/(√3/2) = 2 m.) Learn the formula — it is the clearest example in this chapter of Allen asking past the textbook, and you have now seen it done.

Apparent depth

Look straight down into water and the bottom seems closer than it is, because the rays leaving the water bend away from the normal and your brain traces them back in straight lines. This is 2024-A Q12 — which did not even ask for a number, only for the two labels. NCERT describes the effect — a coin, a tank bottom and printed letters all "appear raised" — but gives no formula and never uses the words real depth or apparent depth. Those two names, and the formula below, are beyond NCERT.

n = real depth / apparent depth
So apparent depth = real depth ÷ n, and the object always appears raised. For layers stacked on top of each other, shrink each layer by its own n and add.

2024-A Q5, the same idea run backwards

An air bubble inside a slab of n = 1.6 appears 4 cm deep from one face and 5 cm deep from the other. How thick is the slab? Each apparent depth is a real depth divided by 1.6, so multiply back: the bubble is really 4 × 1.6 = 6.4 cm below one face and 5 × 1.6 = 8.0 cm below the other. The slab is 6.4 + 8.0 = 14.4 cm thick. The trap is dividing instead of multiplying — which gives 5.6 cm — thinner than 4 + 5 = 9 cm, the least the slab could possibly be, and therefore impossible. Sanity always beats arithmetic: apparent depth is smaller than real depth, so going from apparent back to real you must multiply.

7Lenses — everything mirrors do, with two signs changed

A lens refracts light through itself instead of bouncing it back, so real images now form on the far side. You already draw lens ray diagrams in IGCSE. What is new is the formula, the magnification and the power.

The lens words, in NCERT's words

  • A lens is a transparent material bounded by two surfaces, of which one or both are spherical — so a lens may have one flat face. A watchmaker's magnifying glass and the glasses in spectacles are lenses.
  • A convex lens (double convex) bulges outwards — thicker in the middle than at the edges. It converges light: a converging lens.
  • A concave lens (double concave) curves inwards — thicker at the edges than in the middle. It diverges light: a diverging lens.
  • Each surface is part of a sphere, and the centres of those spheres are the lens's centres of curvature, C₁ and C₂. The straight line through them is the principal axis.
  • The optical centre O is the central point of the lens. A ray through O passes without any deviation.
  • A lens has two principal foci, F₁ and F₂, one on each side, because light can reach it from either side. The focal length f is the distance from O to a principal focus.
  • The aperture is the effective diameter of the lens's circular outline. The chapter deals only with thin lenses of small aperture, whose two centres of curvature are equally far from O.

Finding f in a minute (Activity 9.11). Hold a convex lens towards the Sun and move a sheet of paper until the bright spot is sharpest. The spot is a real image of the Sun at the focus — it will burn the paper — so the lens-to-paper distance is the approximate focal length. (Never look at the Sun through a lens.) The points marked 2F₁ and 2F₂ in the diagrams are simply twice that distance from O.

The ray rules for a thin lens

  1. A ray parallel to the axis leaves through the focus — F₂ on the far side for a convex lens; for a concave lens it emerges spreading, and only appears to come from F₁ on the near side. (For the convex lens that focus is real — the light really passes through it; for the concave lens it is virtual — the light only seems to come from it.)
  2. A ray through the optical centre O goes straight on, undeviated. This is the easiest ray in the whole chapter — always draw it first.
  3. A ray through the near focus F₁ of a convex lens emerges parallel to the axis. For a concave lens it is the ray heading towards the far focus F₂ that emerges parallel (NCERT Fig. 9.14(b)) — a ray through its near focus only spreads out further.

And the concave (diverging) lens, like the convex mirror, has only one answer: virtual, erect, diminished, always between F₁ and O, wherever you put the object. NCERT's Table 9.5 has just two rows: object at infinity → image at F₁, point-sized; object anywhere nearer → image between F₁ and O, diminished. Its Activity 9.13 is the check: look at a candle through a concave lens and no screen will ever catch the image — you see it only by looking through the lens, and it shrinks as the candle is moved away.

1/v − 1/u = 1/f
The lens formula. Minus — the mirror's is plus. Same u, v, f conventions, measured from the optical centre O.
m = h′/h = v/u
The lens magnification. No minus — the mirror's has one. m negative still means inverted and real.
P = 1/f (in metres)
Power. SI unit: the dioptre (D). Convex lens ⇒ +; concave lens ⇒ −. A lens of f = 25 cm has P = 1/0.25 = +4 D.
P = P₁ + P₂ + …
Thin lenses in contact: the net power is the algebraic sum, signs included — +1.5 D with −4 D makes −2.5 D, a diverging pair: the stronger lens decides the sign. Focal lengths do not add, which is why opticians work in powers.

The crossed signs — write this on your hand

FormulaMagnification
Mirror1/v + 1/u = 1/fm = −v/u
Lens1/v − 1/u = 1/fm = +v/u

They are crossed: the one with the plus in the formula has the minus in the magnification. Getting this pair the wrong way round is an easy way to lose the mark — and an easy option for a setter to plant.

Two more power traps

  • f must be in metres. Feeding 25 instead of 0.25 gives 0.04 D instead of 4 D — a slip a paper can easily build an option on.
  • The sign of P is the sign of f — and you cannot guess it. Both 2024 papers put a power question at Q14 and they pull in opposite directions. 2024-A corrects a short-sighted eye (far point 4.03 m) with a concave lens: key −0.25 D. 2024-U corrects a near point at 75 cm with a convex lens: key +8/3 D. So never learn "spectacle lens ⇒ minus". Work out which way the lens has to bend the light, get the sign of f from that, and let it carry through to P. (Those two are eye questions and belong to the next unit — but the formula they run on is this one.)

NCERT's two lens examples, and what it says about power

Example 9.3. A concave lens of focal length 15 cm forms an image 10 cm from the lens. How far from the lens is the object, and what is the magnification?
A concave lens always forms a virtual image on the object's side, so v = −10 cm and f = −15 cm. 1/u = 1/v − 1/f = −1/10 + 1/15 = −1/30, so u = −30 cm — the object is 30 cm from the lens. m = v/u = (−10)/(−30) = +1/3 ≈ +0.33: erect, virtual, one-third the size.

Example 9.4. A 2.0 cm tall object stands 15 cm from a convex lens of focal length 10 cm. Find the nature, position and size of the image.
u = −15 cm, f = +10 cm. 1/v = 1/f + 1/u = 1/10 − 1/15 = 1/30, so v = +30 cm — real, on the far side. m = v/u = 30/(−15) = −2, so h′ = −4.0 cm: inverted, and twice the size.

  • 1 dioptre is the power of a lens whose focal length is 1 metre: 1 D = 1 m⁻¹.
  • NCERT's two prescriptions: +2.0 D is a convex lens of f = +0.50 m; −2.5 D is a concave lens of f = −0.40 m.
  • A lens of short focal length bends light through larger angles — more power. That is all "power" means.
  • Lenses in contact: NCERT's example is +2.0 D and +0.25 D acting together as one +2.25 D lens — how an optician builds up a prescription in the trial frame. The lenses of cameras, microscopes and telescopes are combinations in contact too — combined, in NCERT's words, "to increase the magnification and sharpness of the image", and chosen so that one lens reduces the image defects of another.

8Five worked questions — at TALLENTEX pitch

One per machine, in the order you met them. Try each before opening the working and give yourself 90 seconds — the real paper's average. The elimination reasoning is written out because on the day, ruling two options out is worth almost as much as solving.

1Mirror formula, run backwards · ~90 seconds

A concave mirror throws a real image twice the size of the object onto a screen placed 45 cm from the mirror. The focal length of the mirror is

115 cm
222.5 cm
330 cm
445 cm
Working

Signs first. The image is real, so it is in front of the mirror: v = −45. A real image is inverted, so the magnification is negative: m = −2.

m = −v/u → −2 = −(−45)/u = 45/u → u = −22.5 cm — negative, as u always is. Good, the signs are consistent.

1/f = 1/v + 1/u = −1/45 − 2/45 = −3/45 = −1/15, so f = −15 cm: 15 cm, and the minus confirms it is concave.

Where each wrong option comes from. (2) 22.5 — the object distance. You did the hard half and answered the wrong question; under time pressure this is the most likely slip of the four. (3) 30 — that is R, not f. Doubling at the end instead of at the start. (4) 45 — two different errors both land here: using the lens formula 1/v − 1/u, or writing m = +2 because you read "twice the size" and forgot that a real image is inverted. Elimination. Once you have |u| = 22.5, stop. The image is real and magnified, so the object must lie between F and C: that means f < 22.5 < 2f, i.e. 11.25 < f < 22.5. Only 15 survives — you never need the mirror formula at all.
2Apparent depth, two layers · ~80 seconds

A one-rupee coin lies at the bottom of a tank holding 12 cm of water (n = 4/3), on top of which floats a 3 cm layer of oil (n = 3/2). Seen from directly above, the coin appears to be below the top surface by

111 cm
211.25 cm
312 cm
413.5 cm
Working

Each layer is shrunk by its own refractive index, and the shrunken depths add.

Water: 12 ÷ (4/3) = 12 × 3/4 = 9 cm.

Oil: 3 ÷ (3/2) = 3 × 2/3 = 2 cm.

Apparent depth = 9 + 2 = 11 cm — against a real depth of 15 cm, so the coin has been raised 4 cm. Every apparent depth must come out smaller than the real one; that is your check.

Where each wrong option comes from. (2) 11.25 — using water's n on the whole 15 cm and ignoring that the oil is a different medium: 15 × 3/4. (3) 12 — shrinking the water correctly and then adding the oil's real 3 cm unshrunk. The commonest error here, because the thin layer feels negligible. (4) 13.5 — multiplying by n for the oil instead of dividing: 9 + 3×1.5. Elimination: the answer must be under 15, and the water alone already accounts for 9, so it lies between 9 and 12. That leaves only 11 and 11.25 before you compute the oil — and 11.25 is what you would get if you treated the oil as though it were more water.
3What changes in a medium · ~45 seconds

Light of wavelength 600 nm in air enters a glass block of refractive index 1.5. Inside the glass,

1both the frequency and the wavelength fall to two-thirds of their air values
2the wavelength becomes 400 nm and the frequency is unchanged
3the wavelength is unchanged and the frequency rises to 1.5 times
4the speed becomes 4.5 × 10⁸ m/s
Working

Frequency is set by the source and never changes. That single fact removes options (1) and (3) instantly.

Speed drops by n: v = c/n = 3×10⁸ / 1.5 = 2×10⁸ m/s — so (4), which has light speeding up inside glass, is impossible before you even divide.

With f fixed and v = fλ, the wavelength must fall by the same factor: λ = 600/1.5 = 400 nm.

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) — "everything shrinks by n", which sounds tidy and is wrong about the one quantity that is protected. (3) — swapping which of the pair is fixed. If frequency changed, the colour would change, and a red laser does not turn orange in a fish tank. (4) — multiplying by n instead of dividing: 3×10⁸ × 1.5. Anything faster than 3×10⁸ is automatically wrong, which makes this the cheapest elimination in the chapter.
4Convex mirror, the whole drill · ~90 seconds

The rear-view mirror of a car is convex with radius of curvature 4.0 m. A motorcycle is 2.0 m behind the car. Its image is

12.0 m behind the mirror, the same size
21.33 m behind the mirror, two-thirds the size
31.0 m behind the mirror, half the size
41.0 m in front of the mirror, half the size
Working

Signs first. Convex, so f = +R/2 = +2.0 m. The motorcycle is in front: u = −2.0 m.

1/v = 1/f − 1/u = 1/2 + 1/2 = 1 → v = +1.0 m. Positive, so the image is behind the mirror — virtual, as a convex mirror's always is.

m = −v/u = −(1.0)/(−2.0) = +0.5. Positive ⇒ erect; magnitude ½ ⇒ half the size. Both agree with the "convex mirrors only do one thing" rule, so the answer is safe.

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) — plane-mirror thinking: image as far behind as the object is in front, same size. True for a flat mirror, never for a curved one. (2) — forgetting f = R/2 and using f = 4.0, which gives v = 4/3 m and m = 2/3. (4) — the right arithmetic with the sign of v misread, putting a virtual image in front of the mirror, where it cannot be. Elimination: a convex mirror always gives a virtual, erect, diminished image, so (1) dies on "same size" and (4) on "in front". You are down to two before touching the formula — and the remaining choice is decided purely by whether you halved R.
5Lens formula and power · ~2 minutes

A convex lens forms a sharp image of a distant tree on a screen 25 cm from the lens. The same lens is then used to image a candle standing 75 cm away. The power of the lens and the new image distance are

1+0.04 D and 37.5 cm
2−4 D and 37.5 cm
3+4 D and 18.75 cm
4+4 D and 37.5 cm
Working

Step 1 — the word "distant" is the gift. Rays from a distant object arrive parallel, so they converge at the focus. The screen is at F: f = +25 cm = +0.25 m.

P = 1/f = 1/0.25 = +4 D — metres, and positive because the lens is converging.

Step 2 — the candle. u = −75 cm, f = +25 cm. Lens formula: 1/v = 1/f + 1/u = 1/25 − 1/75 = (3 − 1)/75 = 2/75, so v = +37.5 cm — positive, so real and on the far side.

Check: 75 cm is beyond 2f (= 50 cm), so the image must land between f and 2f, i.e. between 25 and 50 cm. 37.5 sits there. ✔

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) +0.04 D — f left in centimetres: 1/25 instead of 1/0.25. A slip to check for every time power is asked. (2) −4 D — the right size with a converging lens given a negative focal length. (3) 18.75 cm — the sign of u dropped, or the mirror formula used: 1/25 + 1/75 = 4/75. Note it fails the check above — a convex lens never forms a real image closer to it than f — so you can strike it without redoing the algebra.
