Figures, Dice & Cubes

Official topics: Counting of Figures, Embedded Figure, Dice and Cube. None of this is in your IGCSE course, and almost every question comes with a picture — 14 of the 17 that Allen has set. The mark is for reading the picture exactly: which lines really continue, which faces are really opposite, which small cubes really exist. Every count on this page was checked by exhaustive enumeration, not by eye. A 30-minute read, then Paper 1.

What that tells you:

  1. Three or four of the twenty Mental Ability questions every sitting — 12 to 16 marks (2017-E 4, 2018-S 4, 2024-U 3, 2024-A 3, sample 3). Both 2024 papers put the counting item at Q67, the dice or cube item at Q68 and the embedded figure at Q70, so in a mock you meet all three inside four questions.
  2. Counting of Figures is the biggest share (six), and Allen recycles it: 2024-A Q67 is the sample's Q66, figure and options included. Five of the six ask for triangles, one for rectangles; every one has a figure and the wrong options sit close to the right one (26 / 28 / 32, or 15 / 36 / 38 / 40), so a "roughly" count scores −1.
  3. Embedded Figure is five, always drawn, and 2024-A turned it round — the big figure is given and you find which small answer figure sits inside it. The textbook convention is that the hidden copy has the same size and the same orientation; 2017-E Q72 breaks it — no option contains (X) as drawn and the key credits a copy turned through 90°. Section 2 says what to do about that.
  4. Dice is two items in five papers, Cube is four. No real paper draws a die with three numbered faces showing: 2018-S gives a net, 2024-U numbers two side faces per position (top blank) and says the die is standard. The cube items are the painted-block count (asked twice with the same block), a stack to count and a cube cut by planes — three different machines, section 4.
  5. One key you should know about. 2018-S Q69 asks how many cubes have "at most two faces coloured". Read literally that is every cube except the 8 corners: 60 − 8 = 52 — and 52 is not offered. The key credits 46, the cubes with exactly one or exactly two coloured faces. One item only — but the lesson generalises: when your literal reading of a phrase is not among the options, try the nearest listed reading before bubbling "None of these".
  6. Nothing here needs a formula you cannot rebuild in ten seconds — the pair-of-lines count, the painted-cube classes and the two dice rules are the whole toolkit. What the paper is really buying is the discipline of counting by classes and checking every edge, under the clock.

Budget accordingly. A 12–16-mark unit whose questions arrived in one cluster, Q67–70, in both 2024 sittings. Everything below fits on a few screens; the practice is in doing it systematically on the booklet with no second sheet of paper.

1Counting of Figures — count by class, never by eye

Every counting question is lost the same way: you scan the picture, spot the obvious shapes, and stop. The fix is a routine — decide what family of shapes you are counting, list the classes that family can come in, and count each class completely before moving to the next.

1.1 Triangles made by lines through one point

When several lines fan out from one vertex and other lines cut across them, every triangle with that vertex as its apex is fixed by which two of the fanning lines it uses and which crossing line is its base. So count points on each crossing line and pick pairs.

Pairs from n points

ⁿC₂ = n(n − 1)/2, or just add down: 4 points → 3 + 2 + 1 = 6; 5 points → 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 10; 6 points → 15. The common slip is to count the points instead of the pairs (4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 10 for four points is wrong — there are only 3 + 2 + 1 = 6 pairs to choose from).

1.2 Rectangles and squares in a grid

A rectangle in a grid is fixed by choosing two of the vertical lines and two of the horizontal lines — so long as those lines run the full way between each other. A square needs the two gaps equal, so count squares by size.

1.3 The uneven split — does the line really continue?

Allen's rectangle item (2018-S Q75) is not a grid: it is a rectangle cut unevenly, and the whole question is whether a divider on one side lines up with a divider on the other. If it does, the two pieces make one full-length line and every rectangle that uses it appears; if it does not, they are two separate lines and none of those rectangles exist.

Two traps in every counting figure

  • A line that stops. A rectangle or triangle needs its whole side drawn. If a divider ends at a crossing line, nothing beyond that crossing can use it. Before counting, trace each line with your pencil and mark where it ends.
  • A shape that looks like a triangle and is not. Four regions round a centre can be squares (when the cutting lines are parallel to the diagonals) or triangles (when they pass through the corners). A square with an inner diamond whose sides are parallel to the diagonals is the classic case — the four middle regions are squares, and counting them as triangles adds four that are not there.

1.4 The two figures Allen has set

The star has been set twice, the lattice once. Learn both counts as routines, not as numbers — the options are built so that stopping one class early lands on a wrong option.

The 90-second routine for a counting question

  1. Name the family (triangles? rectangles? squares?) and re-read the stem for a qualifier — "not squares", "at least", "in the whole figure".
  2. Trace every line to its ends. Mark which ones run the full way.
  3. Count the smallest pieces of the family, one per region.
  4. Go up one size at a time: shapes made of 2 pieces, then 3, then 4 … until the whole figure. In a symmetric figure, count one corner or one side and multiply.
  5. Add, then sanity-check against the options. If your total sits between two listed options, you have skipped a class — the options are built from the classes.

2Embedded Figure — every edge, same shape, same way up

A figure (X) is given and one of four answer figures contains it. The hidden copy must have every edge of (X) at the same angle and in the same proportion. Other lines may cross it, touch it or continue past it — that changes nothing. A mirror image, a copy with its proportions changed, or a copy with one corner moved is not (X). (Allen's question figure may be printed at a different scale from the answer boxes — 2024-U Q70 is — so compare proportions, not millimetres.)

The routine

  1. Describe (X) before looking at the options — count its edges and name its one odd feature (the off-centre peak, the notch, the diagonal from this corner). The textbook distractors are the mirror, the near miss and a re-proportioned copy, each changing one feature; Allen's real options are often four unrelated drawings, which makes the odd-feature search faster still.
  2. Search each option for the odd feature first. That kills two options in ten seconds.
  3. Then trace every edge of (X) in the survivor. Lines crossing the figure are camouflage; a line that replaces an edge at a different angle is a changed figure.
  4. The reverse form (2024-A Q70): the big figure is given, four small answer figures, "which is embedded". Same test, run four times: pick the answer figure's most distinctive cell or corner, find where the big figure has it, then check the rest cell by cell. Only one survives.

When no option has it as drawn — the 2017-E exception

The convention says same orientation, and the 2018, 2024 and sample items all match as drawn. 2017-E Q72 does not: (X) is a horizontal notched shape, no option contains it unturned, and the key credits the option with the copy turned through 90°. So the order of preference is: as drawn first; if nothing matches, a turned copy; a mirror image never — no Allen key has taken one. Do not go looking for a turned copy while an unturned one is available: a turned copy planted beside an exact one is a distractor, not a second answer.

3Dice — opposite faces, then handedness

A die has three pairs of opposite faces, and every dice question is one of three jobs: find the pairs from what is shown, find them from a net, or — once the pairs are known — tell a die from its mirror image. Faces that are opposite are never seen together; faces seen together are adjacent.

3.1 The standard die — only when the stem says so

The bottom-face question (2024-U Q68's machine)

Each position shows two side faces of a standard die, the top left blank. The two faces shown and their two opposites are the four side faces, so the top and bottom are the remaining pair. A condition on the bottom ("an odd number") then fixes which of the pair is on top. Fresh numbers: positions showing (1, 2), (3, 5) and (4, 6) with "an even number on the bottom": (1, 2) leaves the pair 3–4, bottom 4, top 3; (3, 5) leaves 1–6, bottom 6, top 1; (4, 6) leaves 2–5, bottom 2, top 5. Sum of tops 3 + 1 + 5 = 9.

3.2 Two positions shown — the two rules

Rule A has a condition — check it

Rule A needs five different numbers across the two positions. If a second number repeats in a different place, the die was turned a quarter, not a half, and the "opposite" reading is false. In that case use rule B on the two shared faces instead. With three positions, the fastest check of any claimed pair is the elimination test: if the other four numbers all appear beside x somewhere, the one that never does is opposite x.

3.3 Nets — which squares fold to opposite faces

Any net at all

Two squares that sit in the same row or column with exactly one square between them fold to opposite faces. Two squares that share an edge are adjacent. Use those two facts to pair up any net; whatever is left over is the third pair.

3.4 Handedness — a die and its mirror image

"Which die can be made from this net?" — the two-step

  1. Opposite pairs first. Any option showing two opposite faces together is out. That usually removes two.
  2. Then handedness. The two survivors show the same three faces, arranged as mirror images. Fold the net in your head with one named face on top — "1 on top, 2 folds down to the right, so 3 wraps to the front" — and read which way round the three faces sit. The survivor that passes every opposite-pair check but has two faces swapped is the mirror die, and it is the planted wrong answer.

4Cube — three machines: painted, stacked, cut

The Cube topic is three unrelated question types that share a word. Each has a five-second formula, and each formula has one place the mark is lost.

4.1 The painted cube and the painted cuboid

3 faces: 8
the corners, for any size of cube or cuboid.
2 faces: 12 (n − 2)
the 12 edges, minus the corner at each end. Cuboid a × b × c: 4(a − 2) + 4(b − 2) + 4(c − 2).
1 face: 6 (n − 2)²
the middle of each face. Cuboid: 2[(a−2)(b−2) + (b−2)(c−2) + (a−2)(c−2)].
0 faces: (n − 2)³
the hidden core. Cuboid: (a − 2)(b − 2)(c − 2). A 1-cm-thick slab has none.

Allen's block: 4 × 3 × 5, each pair of opposite faces a different colour

Set in 2017-E Q67 and again in 2018-S Q69: red on the two 5 × 4 faces, blue on the two 4 × 3 faces, green on the two 5 × 3 faces. Corners 8. Edges: four of length 4 → 4 × 2 = 8; four of length 3 → 4 × 1 = 4; four of length 5 → 4 × 3 = 12; two-face cubes 24. One-face: 2 × (2 × 1 + 2 × 3 + 1 × 3) = 2 × 11 = 22. No face: 2 × 1 × 3 = 6. Check: 8 + 24 + 22 + 6 = 60 = 4 × 3 × 5.

Colour questions are edge questions. The cubes carrying exactly colours X and Y sit on the four edges where face X meets face Y, and those edges have the length of the dimension the two faces share. On Allen's block: green (5 × 3) meets red (5 × 4) along the four 5-cm edges → 4 × (5 − 2) = 12; green meets blue (4 × 3) along the four 3-cm edges → 4 × (3 − 2) = 4; red meets blue along the four 4-cm edges → 4 × (4 − 2) = 8. Check: 12 + 4 + 8 = 24, the two-face total.

Read which form is asked. "Green and red" is one pair of faces: 12. "Exactly two colours, one of them green" is both pairs that contain green: 12 + 4 = 16. The planted wrong answer in the second form is the first form's number — the second green pair forgotten.

Read the counting word

  • "Exactly two" = edge cubes only. "At least two" = edges + corners. "At least one" = everything except the core = n³ − (n − 2)³ (for 4³: 64 − 8 = 56; check 8 + 24 + 24). The three classes plus the core must add to n³ — a statement that is out by two is the usual trap.
  • "At most two" should be everything except the corners — but 2018-S keyed it as "one or two" (46, not 52) because 52 was not offered. If your literal reading is not on the list, it is the phrase that is loose, not your count.
  • Only some faces painted. "Painted on two opposite faces" or "on four side faces" — recount from the definition, the formulas above assume all six.

4.2 A stack of cubes — count columns, not what you can see

Two follow-ups Allen can bolt on

"How many more to make a solid cube?" — count what is there, subtract from n³ (14 here → 27 − 14 = 13 to finish a 3 × 3 × 3). "How many cubes are not visible?" — a cube is hidden when its top, its front-left and its front-right faces are all covered; count the columns from the back. The trap in both is counting only what the drawing shows.

4.3 A cube cut by planes

Planes parallel to a face are simpler

A plane parallel to a face, between two layers, cuts nothing; through the middle of a layer it cuts the whole layer, n² cubes. A cut "into k slices" along one direction needs k − 1 planes. The paper's cut-planes questions are counting questions — draw one layer from above and count what the line crosses.

6Five worked questions — at TALLENTEX pitch

Try each before opening the working and give yourself ninety seconds — the real paper's average. The elimination reasoning is written out because knowing why 16 and 20 are wrong is what stops you bubbling them under pressure.

1Counting of Figures — the spanning class · ~90 seconds

How many triangles are there in the given figure?

18
216
318
420
Working

Classes. Smallest pieces: each square's two diagonals cut it into 4 small triangles — 8. Two pieces: each half of a square along one of its sides — 4 per square, 8. Then the class that is easy to miss: triangles that use both squares. The two diagonals meeting at the top of the middle line, with the whole base as third side, make one; the two meeting at the bottom, with the whole top edge, make another — 2. No other triangle spans both squares, because no line runs from one square's corner to the far corner of the other.

8 + 8 + 2 = 18. Answer (3).

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) 8 counts only the smallest pieces. (2) 16 is 2 × 8 — everything inside each square, and nothing across the middle line: the spanning class skipped. (4) 20 adds two more "big" triangles, the halves of the whole rectangle along a corner-to-corner diagonal — but no such diagonal is drawn. A triangle needs all three sides on the paper.
2Embedded Figure — the four standard fakes · ~60 seconds

Figure (X) is embedded in one of the four answer figures. Find that figure.

1Figure (1)
2Figure (2)
3Figure (3)
4Figure (4)
Working

Describe (X) first: a five-sided shape pointing right — flat top and bottom, a point on the right at mid-height — with a triangular wedge drawn inside it against the left side, its apex at the middle of the height. Seven edges. The odd feature is the wedge: which side it sits on (the left) and where its apex is (mid-height).

Kill by the odd feature. (4) has its wedge on the right — the mirror image. (1) has the wedge at the top — the figure turned through 90°. (3) has the wedge on the left but its apex is well above mid-height; the two arms of the wedge are no longer equal, so an edge has changed.

Trace every edge in (2). Top, right-hand point, bottom, the left side, both arms of the wedge — all seven are there at the same lengths and angles. The vertical through the wedge apex, the long line along the bottom, the vertical joining the two right-hand corners and the tail off the point are crossings, not changes. Answer (2).

On the turned copy in (1). A turned copy is only a candidate when no option has the figure as drawn (2017-E Q72). Here (2) has it as drawn, so (1) is a distractor. The mirror in (4) is never an answer.
3Dice — rule A, then the adjacent-faces sum · ~90 seconds

Two positions of the same die are shown. What is the sum of the numbers on the four faces adjacent to the face numbered 4?

114
216
317
420
Working

Check rule A's condition. 1 is common and in the same place (the left face); the other four numbers — 2, 5, 3, 6 — are all different. So the die was turned half a revolution about the 1: the tops are opposite, 2 ↔ 3, and the rights are opposite, 5 ↔ 6. The pair left over is 1 ↔ 4.

Adjacent to 4 means every face except 4 itself and its opposite, 1: 2 + 3 + 5 + 6 = 16. Answer (2). Note that 4 never appears in either drawing — you find it by elimination.

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) 14 assumes a standard die (4 opposite 3) and sums 1 + 2 + 5 + 6 — the stem never said standard, and the drawings show 2 and 3 as tops of the same die turned over, i.e. opposite. (3) 17 is 21 − 4: the opposite face was not removed. (4) 20 is 21 − 1: the 4 itself was left in the sum.
4Cube — the painted cuboid, four statements · ~2 minutes

A solid cuboid measuring 5 cm × 4 cm × 3 cm is painted: both 5 cm × 4 cm faces red, both 5 cm × 3 cm faces blue and both 4 cm × 3 cm faces green. It is then cut into 1-cm cubes. Consider the statements.

  1. 24 small cubes have exactly two faces painted.
  2. 6 small cubes have no paint on them.
  3. 12 small cubes carry red and green but no blue.
  4. 22 small cubes have exactly one face painted.

Which of the statements are correct?

1I and II only
2I, III and IV only
3I, II, III and IV
4I, II and IV only
Working

I — edges. Four edges of each length: 4 × (5 − 2) + 4 × (4 − 2) + 4 × (3 − 2) = 12 + 8 + 4 = 24. Correct.

II — the core. (5 − 2)(4 − 2)(3 − 2) = 3 × 2 × 1 = 6. Correct.

III — red meets green. Red is on the 5 × 4 faces, green on the 4 × 3 faces; they share the four edges of length 4 (the dimension common to both faces). 4 × (4 − 2) = 8, not 12. Wrong. 12 is what you get by taking the 5-cm edges, which are where red meets blue.

IV — one face. 2 × [(3 × 2) + (2 × 1) + (3 × 1)] = 2 × 11 = 22. Correct. (Check: 8 + 24 + 22 + 6 = 60 = 5 × 4 × 3.)

I, II and IV — answer (4).

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) leaves IV untested because 22 "looks odd" — it is the one that needs the three-term sum. (2) accepts III on the wrong edge and rejects II by assuming a block only 3 cm thick has no unpainted core — it has one layer of 3 × 2 = 6. (3) waves III through: a two-colour count taken on the wrong edge is the standard slip in this item.
5Cube — cut by two diagonal planes · ~75 seconds

343 identical small cubes are glued together to form one large cube. The large cube is then cut by two planes, each passing through a pair of opposite vertical edges. How many of the small cubes are cut?

149
284
391
498
Working

343 = 7³, so n = 7. Look at one horizontal layer from above: a 7 × 7 grid. A vertical plane through two opposite vertical edges is a diagonal of that grid and passes through the 7 cubes on it; 7 layers → 49 per plane.

The two diagonals cross at the centre cell, and n is odd, so the central column of 7 cubes is cut by both planes. Counted once: 49 + 49 − 7 = 91. Answer (3).

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) 49 is one plane. (4) 98 counts the central column twice. (2) 84 subtracts the column twice (98 − 14), as if the overlap were 2n.
