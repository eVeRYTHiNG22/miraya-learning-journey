1Why a body needs pipes at all

Start here even if it looks obvious. Almost every later answer falls out of this one idea, and the paper's hardest items are the ones that test whether you understand it or just memorised it.

Every cell in you is doing the same shopping: it needs glucose for energy, oxygen to burn it, and it needs to get rid of carbon dioxide and nitrogen waste. In a one-celled organism like Amoeba the shopping is trivial — the cell is bathed in pond water, so everything it needs simply diffuses in and everything it dumps diffuses out.

The reason diffusion is not enough for you

Diffusion is molecules spreading out on their own, by random movement, from where there are many of them to where there are few. It is fast over very short distances and catastrophically slow over long ones — doubling the distance more than doubles the time. Over a thousandth of a millimetre it takes a fraction of a second; across a human body it would take years. A cell in your thumb cannot wait years for the oxygen you breathed in this morning.

So any animal bigger than a few millimetres needs bulk transport: a liquid that actually flows, pushed by a pump, along tubes that reach everywhere. Diffusion is still used — but only at the very last step, across the thin wall of the smallest tube, where the distance is tiny again. Remember that division of labour. Bulk flow covers the distance; diffusion does the final handover.

That gives every transport system the same three parts, in you and in a plant:

  • a fluid that carries things (your blood; a plant's sap),
  • tubes to keep the fluid on course (blood vessels; xylem and phloem),
  • and something to make it move (your heart; in a plant, no pump at all — evaporation pulls from the top, as §6 explains).

You actually have two circulating fluids. Blood goes round and round in closed tubes. Lymph is the fluid that leaks out of the smallest blood vessels into the spaces between cells; it bathes them, and is then drained back into the blood through its own one-way vessels. Lymph also carries digested fat from the intestine and is part of the body's defence system. Allen has asked exactly one thing about lymph — what does the lymphatic system do? — and the answer it wants is "drains the tissue fluid back to the circulatory system" (2017-E Q34).

3The heart — four rooms, one rule

If you learn one diagram in Biology, learn this one. Allen has drawn it in three of the five cached papers and asked you to read the labels every time.

The heart is a muscular pump with four chambers. The two on top are atria (singular: atrium) — thin-walled rooms that receive blood arriving from veins. The two below are ventricles — thick-walled rooms that pump blood out into arteries. A solid wall, the septum, runs down the middle, and one-way valves sit between each atrium and its ventricle and at the exit of each ventricle.

The one rule that answers most questions: the right side of the heart handles used (deoxygenated) blood, the left side handles fresh (oxygenated) blood, and the septum stops them ever mixing.

One full journey, in eight steps

used blood arrivesBlood that has been round the body — low in oxygen, carrying carbon dioxide and urea — comes back through the two largest veins, the superior vena cava (from the head and arms) and the inferior vena cava (from everything below), into the right atrium.
down one floorThe right atrium contracts and squeezes the blood through a valve into the right ventricle.
out to the lungsThe right ventricle contracts and pushes the blood into the pulmonary artery, which carries it to both lungs.
the gas swapIn the lungs the blood runs through capillaries wrapped round the air sacs. Oxygen diffuses in, carbon dioxide diffuses out. The blood is now oxygenated.
fresh blood returnsIt returns through the pulmonary veins into the left atrium.
down one floorThe left atrium contracts and squeezes it through a valve into the left ventricle.
out to the whole bodyThe left ventricle — the thickest-walled chamber in the heart — contracts hard and drives the blood into the aorta, the body's largest artery, which branches to every organ.
and round againIn the organs' capillaries the blood hands over oxygen and food and picks up carbon dioxide and urea, collects into veins, and arrives back at the vena cava. Step 1 again.

Double circulation — and why "double"

Count how many times the blood passed through the heart in those eight steps: twice. Once on the way to the lungs (steps 1–3) and once on the way to the body (steps 5–7). That is what double circulation means — one complete round of the body takes the blood through the heart two times, through two separate loops:

  • Pulmonary circulation — heart → lungs → heart. Short, and at lower pressure, because lung capillaries are delicate.
  • Systemic circulation — heart → rest of the body → heart. Long, and at high pressure, because it has to reach your toes and your brain.

A fish has a two-chambered heart and single circulation: blood passes through the heart only once per round. Amphibians and most reptiles have three chambers, so some mixing happens. Birds and mammals have four chambers and no mixing at all — and that matters because they are warm-blooded: keeping a body at a steady temperature burns a lot of energy, which needs a lot of oxygen, which needs blood that is fully oxygenated rather than half-mixed.

The beat, and blood pressure

The chambers do not contract at random. The two atria contract together, then the two ventricles contract together, then everything relaxes — about 72 times a minute at rest, which is a little over 4 300 beats an hour. Contraction is called systole, relaxation diastole. The "lub-dub" you hear through a stethoscope is the valves slamming shut.

Each ventricle squirt sends blood into the arteries under pressure, and that pressure is what a doctor measures with a cuff on your upper arm. A healthy young adult reads about 120/80 mm of mercury: 120 during the ventricle's contraction (systolic pressure) and 80 while it refills (diastolic). It is measured in a large artery precisely because that is where the pressure is highest and steadiest to read.

Six students, three right — the 2024-U Q42 item

Allen printed six one-line statements and asked which three were correct. It is the "multi-statement judgement" format that catches more people than any other, so here is that exact item, decided line by line:

  • "The heart is the major pumping organ and it filters the blood also." ✗ — the heart pumps, the kidney filters. Never let one organ do two jobs.
  • "Arteries have thick walls and a narrow lumen." ✓
  • "At the level of capillaries, gaseous exchange takes place." ✓
  • "In arteries the pressure of the blood is very high." ✓
  • "Veins have a narrow lumen and no valves." ✗ — wide lumen, and they do have valves. Two errors in one short sentence.
  • "A capillary has a wall thicker than a vein." ✗ — a capillary wall is a single cell thick, the thinnest wall in the body.

So the answer is students 2, 3 and 4 — option (2). Read every clause: statement 5 is only wrong because of its second half.

4The three kinds of vessel

Three tubes, one difference that explains all the others: how much pressure the tube has to survive.

Blood leaves the left ventricle in violent surges, so an artery needs a thick, muscular, elastic wall that stretches with each surge and springs back — that spring is what you feel as a pulse. By the time the blood has trickled through millions of hair-thin capillaries, the pressure has almost gone, so a vein needs only a thin wall. But low pressure brings a new problem: blood in your leg veins has to get back up to your heart with almost nothing pushing it. That is why veins — and, among blood vessels, only veins — have valves: flaps that let blood through towards the heart and slam shut if it tries to fall back. (The heart has valves of its own, as §3 said; no artery has any.)

The pressure ladder — learn the order, not the numbers

aorta (highest) → arteries → arterioles → capillaries → veins → vena cava (lowest)

Pressure only ever falls as blood moves away from the heart, and it falls most sharply across the capillaries. 2024-A Q42 is that single idea dressed up: is the pressure in the pulmonary artery more or less than in the pulmonary vein? The pulmonary artery is before the lung capillaries, the pulmonary vein after them — so the artery is higher. Option (3). You do not need a single number.

And the third vessel is where the whole system finally does its job. A capillary wall is one cell thick, so oxygen, glucose, carbon dioxide and urea can diffuse straight across it between the blood and the cells outside. Arteries and veins are merely plumbing; every exchange in your body happens in a capillary.

5The named vessels — and the two that break the rule

Allen's favourite trick in this unit is to name a vessel and ask what is in it. Learn this table and the trick stops working.

The definition that is not about oxygen

An artery carries blood away from the heart. A vein carries blood towards the heart. That is the whole definition — direction, never oxygen. It is true that most arteries happen to carry oxygenated blood, which is why the two exceptions are asked so often:

  • the pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood (heart → lungs), and
  • the pulmonary veins carry oxygenated blood (lungs → heart).

If a question says "the vessel that carries deoxygenated blood from the heart towards the lung", it is simply naming the pulmonary artery the long way round. Translate first, then answer.

An honest note on 2018-S Q34

The question: "Which of the following vessels has more nutrients than the others?" — (1) vein arising from the liver, (2) superior vena cava, (3) vein arising from muscles, (4) vein arising from the kidney. Allen's key is (1). The reasoning behind it: of those four, the vein leaving the liver is the one that has just been loaded with glucose, since the liver is the organ that stores and releases it; the other three drain organs that consume nutrients.

Be aware of a subtlety, because it is the kind of thing a sharper question could turn on: the vessel with the very highest nutrient load after a meal is the hepatic portal vein, which runs intestine → liver. It was not on Allen's option list. If a future question offers it, it is the better answer; among these four options, (1) is right.

6Transport in plants — two pipes, no pump

A tall tree lifts water ten storeys with no heart, no muscle and no energy spent on the lifting. Understanding how is worth several marks, because Allen asks about the mechanism, not just the names.

How water gets to the top — the two forces

at the root, mostly at nightRoot pressure. Root hair cells actively pump mineral ions in from the soil, which makes the cell contents more concentrated than the soil water — so water follows in by osmosis. More and more water pushing in creates a real pressure that shoves water up into the xylem. On its own this only lifts water a short way, and it matters most at night, when the leaves are not pulling.
at the leaf, all dayTranspiration pull. Water evaporates from the leaves and escapes through the stomata. Every molecule that leaves tugs the next one along, because water molecules stick to each other — so an unbroken thread of water is dragged all the way up from the roots. This is the main force, and it costs the plant nothing: the sun does the work.

Transpiration

Transpiration is the loss of water vapour from a plant, mainly through the stomata of leaves. It sounds wasteful, and most of the water a plant absorbs does leave this way — but it buys three things: the pull that lifts the whole water column, a delivery service for minerals (they ride up in that stream), and cooling, exactly as sweat cools you.

Hence Allen's Sample Q34: in which plant is there no transpiration? In one that is completely submerged — under water there is nowhere for vapour to go. Desert, rainforest and hill plants all transpire; the desert one is built to lose as little as it can — thick waxy cuticle, few and sunken stomata — and that is the trap, but little is not none.

Allen printed three statements about stomata and asked which are correct (2018-S Q33). In his order: I — transpiration and gas exchange both happen mainly through the stomata ✓; II — "the cell wall of guard cells is homogeneous" ✗, because it is not uniform, and that unevenness (thick inside, thin outside) is precisely what makes the pore work; III — guard cells are bean-shaped in dicot plants ✓ (in grasses and other monocots they are dumb-bell shaped, which is why he wrote "in dicots"). So the answer is only I and III.

Translocation — food moves both ways

The leaf makes sugar. But the root, a growing bud, a developing fruit and a potato tuber all need it, and they are in different directions. So phloem cannot be one-way. The rule is:

Source → sink

A source is anywhere food is made or released (usually a leaf; in spring, a storage root). A sink is anywhere food is used or stored (a growing tip, a fruit, a root). Translocation always moves food from source to sink — which can be up the plant in spring and down it in summer.

How it works: sucrose is loaded into the sieve tube using energy from ATP. That makes the contents of the sieve tube concentrated, so water rushes in by osmosis, and the extra water builds up pressure inside the tube. The contents are then pushed along from the high-pressure end towards the low-pressure end, where the sucrose is being removed. NCERT puts it in one sentence: "Material like sucrose is transferred into phloem tissue using energy from ATP. This increases the osmotic pressure of the tissue causing water to move into it. This pressure moves the material in the phloem to tissues which have less pressure." Allen's own wording for the same idea — the one he credits as correct in 2024-U Q41 — is that soluble material is transferred "from high osmotic pressure to low osmotic pressure areas".

Three translocation traps, all of which Allen has actually printed

  • "from sink to source" — backwards; it is source to sink. This was statement 1 of 2024-U Q41 — but that item needs care, because Allen counts two of its three statements false and the key is "only statement 3 is correct". Statement 2 reads "translocation… occurs with the consumption of ATP which takes place in the companion cell with the help of sieve tube", and Allen marks it wrong: the ATP is not in dispute, the roles are. NCERT's sentence runs the other way round — the transfer takes place in the sieve tubes, with the help of the adjacent companion cells — so the food travels in the sieve tube and the companion cell is the helper, and Allen's statement has the two swapped. Statement 3, the one Allen credits, is the osmotic-pressure sentence above. The lesson: judge each statement on its own, and never assume one is correct just because a clause of it (here, "uses ATP") is.
  • "transport of glucose into the phloem" — the sugar in phloem is sucrose, not glucose, not starch, not fructose. Asked twice, word for word: Sample Q36 and 2024-A Q40.
  • "tracheids and vessels" vs "sieve tubes" — the ascent of sap is xylem, so the answer is tracheids and vessels (2024-U Q40). And when the question names Pinus or Cycas — cone-bearing plants (gymnosperms), which have no vessels at all and manage with tracheids only — the answer narrows to tracheids alone (2024-A Q41). That is the one place the difference between the two xylem elements has mattered, so keep it straight: vessels lose their end walls, tracheids keep theirs.

The red-water experiment (2017-E Q33)

A white flower stands in red-dyed water; hours later the petals and leaves are tinged red, and cutting the stem shows red tubes inside. What does it show? Not that the plant is alive, not that water goes only to the flower — it shows that water is carried from the roots to all parts of the plant through the stem, in specific tubes. Option (4). The dye is a tracer: it makes the xylem visible.

7Excretion — the word, and what actually counts

Excretion has a narrower meaning in biology than in ordinary speech, and one Allen question turns entirely on that.

Excretion

Excretion is the removal of the harmful or surplus products of the body's own chemistry. Two things matter: it is waste or excess (not something useful being stored), and it comes from the body's own chemistry (not from food that was never absorbed in the first place). "Surplus" is in there because excess water and excess salt are excreted too, and neither is a poison — you simply have more of them than you need.

So undigested food leaving as faeces is egestion, not excretion — it was never part of your chemistry. And glycogen and fat are not excreted at all: they are stores, saved for later.

That is exactly 2018-S Q38: of glycogen, fats, urea and carbon dioxide, which must be eliminated? Only urea and carbon dioxide — option (3). Glycogen and fat are the body's savings account.

Where the nitrogen waste comes from — and where it is made

Your body can store extra sugar and extra fat, but it cannot store extra protein. Surplus amino acids are therefore broken down, and that breakdown releases ammonia, which is very poisonous. The liver immediately converts ammonia into the far safer urea, and dumps the urea into the blood. The kidney's job is only to take it out again.

Urea is MADE in the liver and REMOVED by the kidney

Sample Q35 asks "urea is mainly formed in ___" and the answer is the liver, not the kidney. Getting this backwards is the single most common error in the topic. The sentence to keep: the liver makes it, the blood carries it, the kidney removes it, the bladder stores it.

8The kidney and the nephron — three steps, one great trick

This is the densest figure in the topic and the one Allen letters most often. Take the overview first, then the close-up.

Each kidney holds about a million microscopic filters called nephrons — the structural and functional unit of the kidney, in the same way that the neuron is the unit of the nervous system. Everything below happens in one nephron, a million times over, in each kidney.

step 1 · ultrafiltration · in the glomerulusThe artery branch entering the capsule (the afferent arteriole) is wider than the one leaving it (the efferent arteriole). Squeezing the same blood out through a narrower exit builds up high pressure inside the capillary knot, the glomerulus. That pressure forces water and every small dissolved thing — glucose, amino acids, salts, and the urea — straight out through the capillary wall into the cup around it, Bowman's capsule. (The capsule and the glomerulus together are the renal corpuscle — Allen's name for the filtering unit when he sets a match-the-following, as in 2017-E Q43.) Blood cells and plasma proteins are too big and stay behind. The liquid collected is the glomerular filtrate, and about 180 litres of it is made every day. It is a physical squeeze — no energy is spent and nothing is chosen.
step 2 · selective reabsorption · along the tubule180 litres is roughly forty buckets, and it contains all your glucose. Clearly almost everything must come back. Back into what, though? The efferent arteriole does not simply leave: it breaks up into a net of capillaries that wraps round the whole length of the tubule, and everything reabsorbed passes into that blood, which drains onward into the renal vein. (That is why the renal vein carries the least urea of any vein — it is taking back everything worth keeping and none of the waste.) As the filtrate flows along the tubule, the body takes back what it wants:
• Proximal convoluted tubule (PCT) — all the glucose, all the amino acids, most of the salts and about 70–80 % of the water. This is where most reabsorption happens, and it needs energy: glucose is pulled back by active transport, water follows by osmosis.
• Loop of Henle — the long U-shaped dip into the kidney's inner layer. It pumps salt out into the surrounding tissue, making that tissue very salty — which is what later lets the collecting duct pull yet more water out of the urine. Allen describes its effect as the counter-current mechanism that maintains the concentration gradient in the medulla, and describes the loop itself simply as "the U-shaped portion of the tubule".
• Distal convoluted tubule (DCT) — fine tuning: ionic balance and acidity.
• Collecting duct — the last chance to take water back, controlled by a hormone (step 4).
step 3 · tubular secretionThe reverse of reabsorption: a few unwanted things still left in the blood of those surrounding capillaries — extra potassium ions, extra hydrogen ions, some drugs — are actively added into the tubule. This is how the kidney fine-tunes the blood's composition rather than only filtering it.
step 4 · how much water to keep · ADHIf you are short of water, the pituitary gland releases more ADH (antidiuretic hormone, also called vasopressin — it is made in the hypothalamus just above the pituitary, and released by the pituitary). ADH makes the DCT and the collecting duct take back more water — so the urine is small in volume and concentrated. Drink a lot of water and ADH falls, less is reabsorbed, and you produce a lot of dilute urine.
step 5 · outWhat is left — water, urea, excess salts — is urine. Out of the 180 litres filtered, NCERT says "the volume actually excreted is only a litre or two a day" — which means roughly 99 % of the water was taken back. It trickles down the ureter to the urinary bladder, which is muscular and under nervous control — which is why the urge can be held — and leaves through the urethra.

Why filter everything and then take almost all of it back?

It looks absurdly wasteful, and it is the question that shows whether you have understood the kidney. The answer: filtering is blind but easy; choosing is hard but precise. A blind squeeze needs no machinery to recognise anything — it just pushes out everything small, including whatever new poison you swallowed this morning that the body has never met before. Then the body brings back a short, fixed list of things it knows it wants: glucose, amino acids, salt, water. Anything not on the list — urea, drugs, excess salt — is left behind and leaves. It is far safer than trying to recognise every possible waste.

ADH in one line — 2017-E Q38

"What will happen if ADH secretion increases?" More ADH → more water reabsorbed → the volume of urine decreases. Option (2). The letter A in ADH is anti-diuretic, and a diuretic is something that makes you produce more urine — so ADH is the anti-more-urine hormone.

Renin is not rennin, and a nephron is not a neuron

Renin (one n in the middle) is a substance produced in the kidney that helps control blood pressure — 2018-S Q43's answer. Rennin (two n's) is a digestive enzyme in the stomach of young mammals that curdles milk. The same question offered ADH (released by the pituitary, and only acting on the kidney) and aldosterone (from the adrenal gland) as the other two traps — both hormones that work on the kidney but are not made there.

And 2024-U Q45's incorrect statement is "each kidney has large numbers of filtration units called neurons" — which is NCERT's own sentence, "each kidney has large numbers of these filtration units called nephrons", with one letter changed. Nephron = kidney. Neuron = nerve. Allen asked for the neuron by name in the sample paper and then planted it as the wrong word in a kidney question a year later.

9When the kidneys fail — dialysis

Allen has asked this once, as a photograph: a person sits beside a machine with tubes of blood running into it — name the procedure. The answer is haemodialysis, and it is worth knowing how the machine differs from a real kidney, because that is the obvious next question.

The trick is the dialysing fluid. It is made with the same concentration of glucose and salts as normal blood, but with no nitrogenous waste in it at all. So across the membrane:

  • Urea — plenty in the blood, none in the fluid — diffuses out down its concentration gradient and is carried away.
  • Glucose and salts — equal on both sides — have no gradient, so on balance they stay.

That is the whole design: choose the fluid so that only the waste has anywhere to go. 2024-U Q45 states it as a fact you are expected to accept — "dialysing fluid has the same osmotic pressure as blood, except that it is devoid of nitrogenous wastes."

A machine is not a kidney

The one difference worth a mark, and NCERT says it in as many words — dialysis "is similar to the function of the kidney, but it is different since there is no re-absorption involved": there is no reabsorption step. A nephron filters everything out and then carefully brings the useful things back. A dialysis machine never lets the useful things leave in the first place — it has no way of choosing, so it relies on the fluid's composition instead. It also makes no hormones, which a kidney does.

10Excretion in plants — the lazy version

Short section, occasionally worth a mark, and it makes a nice contrast with the elaborate human kidney.

Plants have no kidney, no blood and no urine — and they need none, because their waste problem is far smaller and they are in no hurry. Their methods:

  • Oxygen — a waste product of photosynthesis, and simply diffuses out of the stomata.
  • Carbon dioxide from respiration — out through the stomata too (and much of it is re-used by photosynthesis during the day).
  • Excess water — lost by transpiration, the same process that lifts the water column.
  • Other wastes — stored rather than removed: in the vacuoles of cells, as resins and gums in old xylem (the dark heartwood in the middle of a tree trunk), or shunted into leaves that are about to fall off. Some waste is released straight into the soil around the roots.

The principle to carry away: a plant can afford to store its waste rather than remove it. It makes nitrogenous waste far more slowly than an animal does — it does not eat protein — and nothing inside it has to move quickly, so there is never any hurry to clear anything out of the way.

13Eight worked questions — at TALLENTEX pitch

Try each one before opening the working, and give yourself 90 seconds — the real paper's average. The elimination reasoning is written out every time, because on the day, ruling two options out is worth almost as much as knowing the answer.

12024-A Q38 / Sample Q32 machine, relettered · ~90 seconds

In the heart below, which of the lettered parts contain oxygenated blood?

1A, E, F and I
2A, B, E and I
3B, C, D, G and H
4E, F and I only
Working

Step 1 — name the letters. A is the big arched vessel leaving the lower chamber on the right of the drawing — which, because the heart faces you, is the heart's own left ventricle. So A is the aorta. B rises from the other lower chamber and heads off to the lungs: the pulmonary artery. C comes down from above and G comes up from below into the same upper chamber: the superior and inferior vena cava. D is that upper chamber, the right atrium; H is the chamber beneath it, the right ventricle. F is the pair of vessels entering the other upper chamber from the side: the pulmonary veins. E is that chamber, the left atrium, and I is the thick-walled one beneath it, the left ventricle.

Step 2 — sort them. Oxygenated blood exists only from the lungs onwards: pulmonary veins (F) → left atrium (E) → left ventricle (I) → aorta (A). Everything else — B, C, D, G, H — is carrying used blood back towards the lungs. So the answer is A, E, F and I, option (1).

Where each wrong option comes from. (2) A, B, E and I — the "all arteries carry oxygenated blood" rule: it swaps the pulmonary artery (B) in and the pulmonary veins (F) out. Allen's own printings of this question do not letter the pulmonary artery at all, so this distractor is one this page adds — because that rule is exactly the misconception the two exceptions exist to break. (3) B, C, D, G and H — the complete deoxygenated set: the answer to the opposite question, offered to catch a misread stem. (4) E, F and I only — the left side of the heart correctly identified, but the aorta forgotten because it is a vessel rather than a chamber. Elimination. The two exceptions do most of the work: any option containing B is wrong, and any option missing F is wrong, which kills (2) and (3) at sight. Option (4) survives both rules — it falls to the third thing to remember, that the answer is not only chambers: A, the aorta, is carrying oxygenated blood too. Two rules and one extra check, instead of nine letters examined one by one.
2a two-step journey · ~2 minutes

A molecule of urea is made in a liver cell and enters the blood there. Before it is filtered out in a kidney, how many heart chambers does it pass through, and which vessel is it in immediately before it reaches the kidney?

12 chambers; renal vein
24 chambers; renal artery
34 chambers; renal vein
42 chambers; renal artery
Working

Step 1 — trace it. Liver → hepatic vein → inferior vena cava → right atrium → right ventricle → pulmonary artery → lungs (a lung can only excrete something that leaves as a gas, and urea is a dissolved solid, so it sails straight through) → pulmonary veins → left atrium → left ventricle → aorta → renal artery → the kidney.

Step 2 — count and name. Four chambers, in that order, and the last vessel before the kidney is the renal artery — blood going in. Option (2).

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) and (4), "2 chambers" — forgetting that the blood must go through the lungs first. There is no shortcut from a vein to an artery: everything arriving in the right atrium is sent to the lungs before it can be pumped to any organ. That is the whole point of double circulation. (1) and (3), "renal vein" — the renal vein carries blood away from the kidney, and it is the one with the least urea in the body. Naming it here reverses the direction. Elimination. Answer the vessel half first: "before it reaches the kidney" must be an artery, so (1) and (3) die instantly and the count decides the rest.
32024-A Q45 / Sample Q41 machine, relettered · ~90 seconds

In the nephron below, in which lettered part is glucose reabsorbed, and in which is the last water taken back under the control of ADH?

1B and E
2A and E
3B and C
4C and E
Working

Step 1 — name the letters. A is the cup at the start with the capillary knot inside: Bowman's capsule. B is the coiled tube leading out of it: the proximal convoluted tubule. C is the long U-shaped dip: the loop of Henle. D is the second coil: the distal convoluted tubule. E is the wide vertical tube everything drains into: the collecting duct.

Step 2 — place the two events. All the glucose is reabsorbed in the PCT, B — by active transport, and none should be left by the time the filtrate reaches the loop. ADH acts on the DCT and the collecting duct, so the last water reabsorption is in E. Option (1).

Where each wrong option comes from. (2) A and E — thinking reabsorption happens where filtration happened. Nothing is taken back in the capsule: A is a blind squeeze, and the filtrate leaves it complete. (3) B and C — right about glucose, but names the loop for ADH. The loop does move water and salt, but it is not hormone-controlled; it is what makes the surrounding tissue salty so that E can pull water out. (4) C and E — from Sample Q31, "the loop of Henle recovers water and sodium chloride". True, but the question here says glucose, and glucose is a PCT job every time. Elimination. Glucose is always PCT, so anything without B is out — (2) and (4) go at once.
4the only arithmetic in the unit · ~2 minutes

A healthy pair of kidneys forms glomerular filtrate at about 125 mL per minute, and about 99 % of it is reabsorbed. Roughly how much urine is produced in a day?

10.18 L
21.8 L
318 L
4180 L
Working

Step 1 — filtrate per day. A day is 24 × 60 = 1440 minutes, so 125 × 1440 = 180 000 mL = 180 litres. (That is the familiar 180 L figure — worth recognising, since it tells you the first step was right.)

Step 2 — what is left. If 99 % goes back, 1 % comes out: 180 × 0.01 = 1.8 litres. Option (2) — and it sits right inside NCERT's own figure, "a litre or two a day". (You will also see 1.5 L quoted; nothing turns on it, because only one option here is anywhere near the right size.)

Where each wrong option comes from. (4) 180 L — stopping after step 1 and quoting the filtrate as the urine. This is the answer to a different question. (3) 18 L — taking 10 % rather than 1 %, an easy slip when converting a percentage this close to 100. (1) 0.18 L — taking 0.1 %, the same slip in the other direction. Elimination. You can bracket the answer before any arithmetic: you know a person passes roughly one to two litres of urine a day, which rules out three of the four options at sight. Use that as a sanity check on your working, not as a substitute for it.
5the multi-statement judgement — Allen's signature trap · ~2 minutes

Consider the following statements.

  1. The pulmonary vein carries blood at a higher pressure than the pulmonary artery.
  2. All of the glucose in the filtrate is reabsorbed in the proximal convoluted tubule.
  3. Translocation moves sucrose from source to sink and uses energy from ATP.
  4. The ascent of sap in a tall tree is driven mainly by root pressure.
  5. The wall of a capillary is one cell thick.

Which statements are correct?

1I, II and V
2II, III and V
3II, IV and V
4III, IV and V
Working

Take them one at a time — that is the only safe method.

I ✗. Pressure always falls as blood moves away from the heart, and it falls hardest across capillaries. The pulmonary artery comes before the lung capillaries and the pulmonary vein after them, so the artery is higher. (This is 2024-A Q42, printed the other way up.)

II ✓. All of it, by active transport, in the PCT. Glucose appearing in urine means the system has been overwhelmed, not that some is normally left.

III ✓. Both halves are right: source → sink, and loading the sieve tube costs ATP.

IV ✗. Root pressure is real but weak and mostly nocturnal; the main force is the transpiration pull from the leaves.

V ✓. One cell thick — which is exactly why exchange happens there.

Correct: II, III and V — option (2).

How to shorten it under the clock. Find one statement you are certain about and use it to cut the options. Statement IV is the easiest certainty here — root pressure is not the main force — and that single call removes (3) and (4). Then decide between I and III: an artery before the capillaries must be at higher pressure, so I is out, and only (2) survives. Two decisions instead of five.
6assertion–reason — at most one per real paper · ~60 seconds

Assertion (A): Veins have valves along their length.
Reason (R): Blood in the veins flows at high pressure.

1Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A
2Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A
3A is true but R is false
4A is false but R is true
Working

Step 1 — judge A on its own. Veins do have valves. A is true.

Step 2 — judge R on its own, ignoring A completely. Blood in veins is at the lowest pressure in the circulation — almost all of it was lost crossing the capillaries. R is false.

So the answer is (3). Note the sting: the real reason veins need valves is the exact opposite of what R claims — the pressure is so low that blood would fall back down your legs without them.

The method, which is worth more than this one item. Always judge the two statements separately before you think about whether one explains the other. Most lost marks in assertion–reason come from reading a true-sounding Reason next to a true Assertion and choosing (1) without testing R by itself. And the moment R is false, options (1) and (2) are dead however good the Reason sounds.
7two-step: what changes at night · ~90 seconds

A well-watered potted plant is left in complete darkness all night. Compared with the same plant at midday, which pair correctly describes it?

1Stomata open; water still lifted mainly by transpiration pull
2Stomata open; water now lifted mainly by root pressure
3Stomata closed; water now lifted mainly by transpiration pull
4Stomata closed; water now lifted mainly by root pressure
Working

Step 1 — the stomata. In darkness the guard cells lose water, go flaccid, straighten, and the pore closes. (Closing at night is useful: no photosynthesis is happening, so there is nothing to be gained by letting carbon dioxide in, and water would be lost for nothing.)

Step 2 — so what still lifts the water? Closed stomata means almost no evaporation from the leaf, which means no transpiration pull. What is left is the push from below — root pressure — which is exactly why it is described as mattering most at night. Option (4).

Where each wrong option comes from. (3) — gets the stomata right, then keeps the daytime force anyway; it is the answer of someone who has learned "transpiration pull is the main force" as an unconditional fact rather than as a daytime one. (1) and (2) — both need the stomata open at night, which is the wrong way round. Elimination. Decide the stomata first: that one call is certain and removes half the options immediately. Never start with the half of the question you are less sure of.
82024-A Q35 machine, carried one step on · ~90 seconds

Which one of these statements about an artificial kidney (dialysis) machine is FALSE?

1The dialysing fluid has the same glucose and salt concentration as normal blood, but contains no nitrogenous waste.
2Urea leaves the blood by diffusion, because there is none of it in the fluid.
3The machine selectively reabsorbs the useful substances back into the blood, exactly as a nephron does.
4Blood is taken from an artery, driven through the machine by a pump and returned to a vein.
Working

Step 1 — read the demand. The question wants the FALSE statement. Three of these are true; you are hunting the odd one.

Step 2 — test each. (1) is true, and it is the machine's whole design. (2) is true and follows from (1): a substance diffuses from where there is more of it to where there is less, and there is none in the fluid. (4) is true — artery out, vein back, with a pump doing the heart's job for that stretch. (3) is false: a dialysis machine has no reabsorption step at all. It cannot select anything. It keeps the useful substances only by never giving them a gradient to leave down.

So the answer is (3).

The reading discipline this item is really testing. In a "which is NOT correct" item, tick each statement you judge true as you go and circle the one you judge false — physically, on the booklet. One common way to lose this mark is not misjudging a statement at all, but finding the false one and then bubbling the option number of the last true one you read. Allen sets negative-demand items in three of the five cached papers — one to three per section in 2024-U, 2018-S and the sample, and none at all in 2024-A or 2017-E — so expect one, but do not assume it. 2024-U Q45 and 2018-S Q31 are two more of them.

15Glossary — every term the real papers use

If any word on this page was new, it is defined here in one line. These are the exact terms the five cached papers print.

DiffusionMolecules spreading from where they are crowded to where they are not, with no energy spent. Fast over microscopic distances, hopeless over long ones.
OsmosisThe diffusion of water across a selectively permeable membrane — one that lets water through but holds the dissolved substances back — from the more watery side to the less watery (more concentrated) side.
Active transportMoving a substance against its gradient — from where there is less to where there is more — which costs energy (ATP). How glucose is reabsorbed and how root hairs take up minerals.
PlasmaThe straw-coloured liquid part of blood. Carries dissolved food, salts, hormones, most carbon dioxide and all the urea.
HaemoglobinThe red, iron-containing pigment inside red blood cells that carries oxygen.
LymphocyteA white blood cell responsible for the immune response.
Thrombocyte (platelet)A cell fragment in blood that makes blood clot at a wound.
LymphThe fluid that leaks out of capillaries and bathes the cells; drained back to the blood by lymph vessels. Also carries digested fat and takes part in defence.
AtriumAn upper chamber of the heart. It receives blood from a vein. Plural: atria.
VentricleA lower chamber of the heart. It pumps blood out into an artery; the left one has the thickest wall.
SeptumThe solid muscular wall between the right and left sides of the heart, which stops oxygenated and deoxygenated blood mixing.
Systole / diastoleContraction of a heart chamber / its relaxation. The two numbers in a blood-pressure reading, e.g. 120/80 mm Hg.
Double circulationBlood passing through the heart twice in one complete circuit of the body — once for the lung loop, once for the body loop.
Pulmonary"To do with the lungs". Pulmonary artery: heart → lungs (deoxygenated). Pulmonary veins: lungs → heart (oxygenated).
Hepatic"To do with the liver". The hepatic portal vein carries digested food from the intestine to the liver.
Renal"To do with the kidney". Renal artery in (with urea), renal vein out (with the least urea of any vein).
CapillaryThe smallest blood vessel, one cell thick, where all exchange between blood and cells happens.
XylemThe plant tissue that carries water and minerals upward. Made of dead, hollow vessels (end walls dissolved away, so they form a continuous pipe) and tracheids (end walls kept, so water seeps through pits). Gymnosperms such as Pinus and Cycas have tracheids only.
PhloemThe living plant tissue that carries food. Made of sieve tubes and companion cells.
Ascent of sapThe upward movement of water and minerals through the xylem, from root to leaf.
TranspirationThe loss of water vapour from a plant, mainly through the stomata of leaves. Creates the pull that lifts the water column.
Root pressureThe push generated at the root when ions are actively taken in and water follows by osmosis. Matters most at night.
Stoma (plural stomata)A pore in a leaf surface, between two guard cells, through which water vapour and gases pass.
Guard cellsThe two cells around a stoma — bean-shaped in dicots, dumb-bell shaped in grasses and other monocots. Turgid (swollen) → pore open; flaccid (limp) → pore closed.
Turgid / flaccidA cell swollen with water and pressing on its wall / a cell that has lost water and gone limp.
TranslocationThe transport of food (sucrose) in the phloem, from source to sink, using ATP.
Source / sinkWhere food is made or released (a leaf, a storage root in spring) / where it is used or stored (a growing tip, a fruit, a root).
ExcretionThe removal of the harmful waste products of the body's own chemistry. Not the same as egestion, which is the removal of undigested food.
UreaThe main nitrogenous waste in humans. Made in the liver from surplus amino acids (via ammonia), removed by the kidneys.
NephronThe structural and functional unit of the kidney — about a million in each. Not to be confused with a neuron, the unit of the nervous system.
Selectively (semi-) permeable membraneA membrane with holes small enough to stop some substances crossing while letting others through. A cell membrane is one; so are the tubes in a dialysis machine.
Osmotic pressureA measure of how strongly a solution pulls water in by osmosis. The more dissolved material it holds, the higher its osmotic pressure — which is why loading sucrose into a sieve tube makes water follow.
ElectrolytesThe dissolved salts of the body — sodium, potassium, chloride and the rest — so called because they carry an electric charge in solution.
Nitrogenous wasteWaste containing nitrogen. In us it is ammonia, made when surplus amino acids are broken down, and the urea the liver makes from it. (Uric acid is the equivalent waste in birds and reptiles, which pass it as a paste rather than in water.)
Cortex and medullaThe two layers of a kidney. The cortex is the outer layer, where every Bowman's capsule sits; the medulla is the inner layer that the loops of Henle dip down into, and which the loop keeps salty.
Peritubular capillariesThe net of capillaries wrapped round a nephron's tubule, formed by the efferent arteriole. Everything reabsorbed goes into this blood, which drains into the renal vein.
VacuoleA fluid-filled bag inside a cell. Plant cells have a large one, and it is where a plant parks waste it has no intention of removing.
AldosteroneA hormone from the adrenal gland that acts on the kidney to control salt and water balance. It is made outside the kidney — which is why it is the wrong answer to "what is produced in the kidney".
PitsThin, unthickened patches in the wall of a tracheid. Water seeps through them from one tracheid to the next, because a tracheid's end walls are never dissolved away as a vessel's are.
GlomerulusThe knot of capillaries inside Bowman's capsule where the blood is filtered under pressure.
Bowman's capsuleThe cup-shaped start of a nephron that surrounds the glomerulus and collects the filtrate.
Renal corpuscleThe glomerulus and Bowman's capsule together — the filtering unit, as Allen names it in its 2017 match item.
UltrafiltrationFiltering under pressure: everything small enough is squeezed out of the blood into the capsule. Blood cells and proteins stay behind.
Glomerular filtrateThe liquid collected in Bowman's capsule — about 180 litres a day. Blood minus its cells and proteins.
Selective reabsorptionTaking back from the filtrate the things the body wants: all glucose and amino acids, most salts and water.
PCTProximal convoluted tubule — the first coil, where most reabsorption happens (all glucose, 70–80 % of water and electrolytes).
Loop of HenleThe U-shaped portion of the tubule. Recovers water and sodium chloride and makes the medulla salty.
Counter-current mechanismThe loop's trick of running its two limbs in opposite directions, which maintains the salt concentration gradient in the medulla.
DCTDistal convoluted tubule — the second coil, which fine-tunes ionic balance and pH.
Collecting ductThe wide tube that several nephrons drain into; the last place water is reabsorbed, under ADH. What leaves it is urine.
Tubular secretionThe opposite of reabsorption — unwanted ions and substances are actively added from the blood into the tubule.
ADH (vasopressin)The antidiuretic hormone — made in the hypothalamus and released by the pituitary. More ADH → more water reabsorbed → less urine, and more concentrated.
ReninA substance produced in the kidney that helps regulate blood pressure. Not rennin, the milk-curdling stomach enzyme.
Ureter / urethraThe tube from each kidney to the bladder / the single tube from the bladder to the outside.
DialysisCleaning blood with a machine: it flows past a semi-permeable membrane bathed in fluid that contains no nitrogenous waste, so urea diffuses out.
