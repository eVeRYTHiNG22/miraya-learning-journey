1The words NCERT uses — and what is new since IGCSE

Same particles, different vocabulary. IGCSE says relative formula mass, proton number and charge on the ion; NCERT says formula unit mass, atomic number and valency. Allen's stems use the NCERT words, and a few of them are traps in themselves.

Definitions, NCERT wording

  • Atom — NCERT calls atoms "the building blocks of all matter"; its summary line: the smallest particle of the element that cannot usually exist independently and retains all its chemical properties. Atoms of most elements cannot exist on their own — they form molecules or ions, which gather in huge numbers into the matter we see (argon and helium are the exceptions that do exist as single atoms).
  • Molecule — a group of two or more atoms chemically bonded together; the smallest particle of an element or a compound that can exist independently and shows all its properties. A molecule of an element (O₂, S₈) or of a compound (H₂O, CO₂).
  • Atomicity — the number of atoms in one molecule. Monoatomic: the noble gases He, Ne, Ar; diatomic H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂ (and HCl, CO, NO); triatomic O₃ (ozone — three oxygens instead of the usual two), H₂O, CO₂ — and SiO₂, which is why 2017-E Q16 keys "silica is a triatomic compound"; tetra-atomic P₄; sulphur NCERT just calls poly-atomic (S₈ has eight). Metals and carbon have no small molecule at all — NCERT: "a very large and indefinite number of atoms bonded together" — so their formula is just the symbol (Na, Fe, C); that is why some books loosely call metals monoatomic.
  • Ion — a charged particle, one atom or a group of atoms. Positive = cation, negative = anion; a charged group of atoms is a polyatomic ion (NH₄⁺, SO₄²⁻), and it carries a fixed charge. NCERT: compounds made of metals and non-metals contain charged species — sodium chloride is Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions, not molecules.
  • Chemical formula — the symbolic representation of a compound's composition: it shows the elements in it and the number of atoms of each (H₂S: two hydrogen, one sulphur).
  • Valency — the combining capacity of an element: the number of electrons an atom gains, loses or shares to complete its outer shell. A number, never signed (Mg has valency 2; the Mg²⁺ ion has charge +2). NCERT's picture: valency is an atom's "hands or arms" — an octopus (eight arms) holding people (two arms each) with every arm locked holds four, which it writes OH₄ (O for octopus, H for human — not oxygen and hydrogen).
  • Atomic mass unit, u — one-twelfth of the mass of one carbon-12 atom (the older symbol amu is the same thing). Atomic mass of an element = the mass of its atom in u, relative to that.
  • Molecular mass — the sum of the atomic masses of all atoms in a molecule, in u. Formula unit mass — the same sum for the formula of an ionic compound (NaCl = 58.5 u), because an ionic solid has no molecules. Allen ignores the distinction and writes "molecular mass of XCl₂" even after calling it ionic (2024-U Q16) — do not let it stop you.
  • Mole beyond NCERT — cut from the chapter in 2023 (only exercise 6 still asks for molar masses) but still asked by Allen (section 5): the amount of a substance containing 6.022 × 10²³ particles (the Avogadro number). Molar mass — the mass of one mole, in grams: numerically the atomic, molecular or formula unit mass with g/mol on it.

How big is an atom?

Smaller than anything you can compare it with: NCERT says more than millions of atoms stacked up would make a layer barely as thick as a sheet of paper. So no one can see an atom with the naked eye — only modern techniques can image a surface atom by atom (NCERT's Fig 3.2 is the surface of silicon). Atomic radius is measured in nanometres: 1 nm = 1/10⁹ m, so 1 m = 10⁹ nm.

Symbols — from Dalton's circles to IUPAC's letters

  • Berzelius (NCERT prints "Berzilius") suggested making the symbol from one or two letters of the element's name. Names themselves once came from the place an element was first found (copper from Cyprus) or from a colour (gold from an English word meaning yellow). Today IUPAC — the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry — approves names, symbols and units.
  • First letter capital, second letter small: Al, not AL; Co is cobalt, while CO is carbon monoxide — a compound of two elements. Often the first one or two letters (H, Al), sometimes the first letter plus a later one (Cl chlorine, Zn zinc).
  • Latin (or German, Greek) names — NCERT gives three: Fe from ferrum (iron), Na from natrium (sodium), K from kalium (potassium). The others worth knowing beyond NCERT: Cu cuprum, Ag argentum, Au aurum, Pb plumbum, Hg hydrargyrum, Sn stannum — the roots of cupric, plumbous and stannic in section 4.

Two laws, one man each, and Dalton's theory that explained them. 2018-S Q16 asks you to name the law from one fact; 2024-A Q19 (and Sample Q20) asks you to use it. NCERT opens the chapter with the people, so a "who said it" statement is fair game too.

Two laws, NCERT wording

  • Law of conservation of mass (Lavoisier) — mass can neither be created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction. Total mass of reactants = total mass of products.
  • Law of constant proportions (Proust) — in a chemical substance the elements are always present in definite proportions by mass, whatever the source or the method of preparation. Also called the law of definite proportions. NCERT's ratios: water H : O = 1 : 8 (so 9 g of water always gives 1 g of hydrogen and 8 g of oxygen); ammonia N : H = 14 : 3; and, in its Table 3.4, carbon dioxide C : O = 3 : 8 (3 g of carbon in 8 g of oxygen gives 11 g of CO₂).

NCERT's experiment for the first law (Activity 3.1)

Two solutions that react — one of the pairs copper sulphate + sodium carbonate, barium chloride + sodium sulphate or lead nitrate + sodium chloride — are kept apart: one in a small ignition tube hung inside a conical flask that holds the other. The flask is corked and weighed, tilted and swirled so that the two mix and react — a precipitate appears (white barium sulphate from barium chloride + sodium sulphate, for instance), the sign that a reaction has happened — and weighed again. The mass does not change. The cork is the point of the design: it makes the flask a closed system, so nothing can escape into the air or come in from it while the reaction runs.

NCERT's numbers to check the law with: 5.3 g of sodium carbonate + 6 g of acetic acid → 2.2 g of carbon dioxide + 0.9 g of water + 8.2 g of sodium acetate. In: 5.3 + 6 = 11.3 g. Out: 2.2 + 0.9 + 8.2 = 11.3 g.

Dalton's atomic theory (1808) — NCERT's six postulates

#Postulate, in NCERT's wordsWhat it explains — or where it fails
(i)All matter is made of very tiny particles called atoms, which participate in chemical reactions.—
(ii)Atoms are indivisible particles, which cannot be created or destroyed in a chemical reaction.Explains the law of conservation of mass — NCERT's own question. But "indivisible" is wrong: atoms contain electrons, protons and neutrons (section 6).
(iii)Atoms of a given element are identical in mass and chemical properties."Identical in mass" is wrong: isotopes (section 8). Identical chemical properties still holds.
(iv)Atoms of different elements have different masses and chemical properties.Still a good rule. Some books add isobars (⁴⁰Ar and ⁴⁰Ca, two elements with the same mass number) as a third dent in the theory.
(v)Atoms combine in the ratio of small whole numbers to form compounds.Why formulae are small whole numbers — H₂O is 2 : 1.
(vi)The relative number and kinds of atoms are constant in a given compound.Explains the law of constant (definite) proportions — NCERT's other question.

Two postulates are now known to be wrong — atoms are divisible, and atoms of one element are not all identical in mass — ready-made material for a "which statement is false" item. NCERT's own in-text questions ask which postulate stands behind which law: (ii) → conservation of mass, (vi) → constant proportions.

Using the law — the "how much" machine

"CO₂ contains 27.2 % carbon by mass. What mass of oxygen combines with 10 g of carbon?" (2024-A Q19, Sample Q20). Oxygen is 100 − 27.2 = 72.8 %, so the C : O ratio is 27.2 : 72.8 in every sample. Oxygen for 10 g of carbon = 10 × 72.8 / 27.2 = 26.7 g — option (1). The distractor 3.74 g is the ratio upside down (10 × 27.2 / 72.8); 7.28 g is 10 × 72.8 % — the percentage of oxygen applied to the carbon as if 10 g were the whole; 4.28 g has no route worth knowing. Check with NCERT's 3 : 8 — 10 g of carbon takes 26.67 g of oxygen. Same answer, which is the whole point of the law.

The other direction: "3 g of hydrogen burns in 30 g of oxygen — how much water forms?" Water is 1 : 8, so 3 g of hydrogen uses 24 g of oxygen and gives 27 g of water, with 6 g of oxygen left unreacted. The reactant that runs out first fixes the product (that is the limiting reagent, section 5, in disguise).

NCERT's own exercise is the same idea: 3 g of carbon burnt in 8 g of oxygen gives 11 g of CO₂; burnt in 50 g of oxygen it still gives only 11 g — the other 42 g of oxygen is left over. The law that decides it is constant proportions; conservation of mass only says nothing disappears (11 + 42 = 53 g).

Where the unit "u" came from

Nobody could weigh a single atom, so relative masses were worked out from how elements combine. NCERT's example: 3 g of carbon combines with 4 g of oxygen to form CO — so if one carbon atom were the unit (1 u), an oxygen atom would be 4/3 = 1.33 u. Whole numbers were wanted, so chemists first took 1/16 of the mass of a naturally occurring oxygen atom as the unit, for two reasons: oxygen combines with a great many elements, and this unit made most atomic masses whole numbers. In 1961 the standard became carbon-12: 1 u = exactly 1/12 of the mass of one ¹²C atom ("u", unified mass, is IUPAC's name for the old "amu"). The relative atomic mass of an element is the average mass of its atom compared with that 1/12. NCERT's picture: a fruit seller calls a watermelon "12 units", cuts it into twelve equal pieces, and weighs every other fruit against one piece.

Ratio by mass → ratio by atoms (NCERT Activity 3.2)

Divide each element's share of the mass by its atomic mass, then scale to the smallest whole numbers. Water is H : O = 1 : 8 by mass → 1/1 : 8/16 = 1 : ½ = 2 : 1 by atoms — H₂O.

The "find X" machine (2024-U Q16) — subtract, identify, rebuild

"An ionic compound of X and chlorine has the formula XCl₂ and molecular mass 95 u. What is the molecular mass of the oxide of X?" Three steps, each one line:

  1. Subtract what you know. XCl₂ = X + 2 × 35.5 = X + 71 = 95, so X = 24.
  2. Identify. 24 u is magnesium. (Not strictly needed — but it tells you X is divalent, which the formula XCl₂ already said.)
  3. Rebuild. X is divalent, oxygen is divalent, so the oxide is XO = 24 + 16 = 40 u. Option (2).

The distractors are the three slips: 56 is 24 + 32 — the oxide written XO₂ by copying the chloride's subscript; 64 is 48 + 16 — X₂O, the oxide written as if X were monovalent like sodium; 80 is 2 × 40 — X₂O₂, the criss-cross never cancelled. The valency of X is fixed by the chloride: two chlorides (valency 1 each) mean X is 2, and one oxygen (valency 2) then balances it. Formula-writing is section 4.

The naming rules the old names follow

  • -ous / -ic for a metal with two valencies: -ous is the lower, -ic the higher. Ferrous Fe²⁺ / ferric Fe³⁺; cuprous Cu⁺ / cupric Cu²⁺; plumbous Pb²⁺ / plumbic Pb⁴⁺; stannous Sn²⁺ / stannic Sn⁴⁺; mercurous Hg₂²⁺ / mercuric Hg²⁺. IGCSE writes iron(II) and iron(III); Allen writes both.
  • -ate / -ite for an oxo-anion: -ate has one more oxygen than -ite, same charge. Sulphate SO₄²⁻ / sulphite SO₃²⁻; nitrate NO₃⁻ / nitrite NO₂⁻; chlorate ClO₃⁻ / chlorite ClO₂⁻; phosphate PO₄³⁻ / phosphite PO₃³⁻. -ide is a single element (chloride, oxide, sulphide) — with two exceptions you must know as words, hydroxide OH⁻ and cyanide CN⁻.
  • Hydrogen- in front (bi- in the old style) adds an H⁺ and drops one charge: carbonate CO₃²⁻ → hydrogencarbonate HCO₃⁻; sulphate SO₄²⁻ → hydrogensulphate HSO₄⁻.

NCERT's rules for writing a formula

  • The valencies (or the charges on the ions) must balance — the formula as a whole is neutral, and the charges are not written in it (MgCl₂, never Mg²⁺Cl⁻₂).
  • When a metal combines with a non-metal, the metal is written first: CaO, NaCl, FeS, CuO.
  • A polyatomic ion that appears more than once goes in brackets with the number outside: Mg(OH)₂, Ca(OH)₂ — not CaOH₂, which would mean one oxygen and two hydrogens. A single polyatomic ion needs no bracket: NaOH, Na₂CO₃, NaNO₃.
  • A compound of just two elements is a binary compound — HCl, H₂S, CCl₄, MgCl₂, Al₂O₃, CaO.
  • Count atoms by multiplying through the brackets: H₂S has 3 atoms; the PO₄³⁻ ion has 5; (NH₄)₂SO₄ has 2 × 5 + 5 = 15.

Criss-cross. Write the symbols, write each valency above, swap them down as subscripts, cancel a common factor, bracket a polyatomic ion that gets a subscript bigger than 1. So cupric nitrite is Cu²⁺ and NO₂⁻ → Cu(NO₂)₂ (4 oxygens); ferric sulphite is Fe³⁺ and SO₃²⁻ → Fe₂(SO₃)₃ (9 oxygens); the ratio 2017-E Q19 wants is 4 : 9. Aluminium sulphate Al₂(SO₄)₃; calcium phosphate Ca₃(PO₄)₂; sodium phosphate Na₃PO₄ (NCERT's "do it yourself"); ammonium sulphate (NH₄)₂SO₄; magnesium oxide Mg²⁺O²⁻ → Mg₂O₂ → cancel → MgO; ferric oxide Fe₂O₃, ferrous oxide FeO. The formula of a covalent compound comes from the same valencies — H₂O (H 1, O 2), NH₃ (N 3), CH₄ (C 4), CO₂, SO₂, H₂S, HCl, N₂O₅ (N 5 here, O 2).

The rest of the mole toolkit — the words coaching books use beyond NCERT

  • Gram atomic mass and gram molecular mass — the atomic or molecular mass written in grams: 12 g of carbon, 18 g of water. Each is exactly one mole.
  • Mass of one atom or molecule = molar mass ÷ 6.022 × 10²³. One carbon atom = 12 ÷ 6.022 × 10²³ ≈ 1.99 × 10⁻²³ g; one water molecule ≈ 2.99 × 10⁻²³ g. And 1 u = 1 ÷ 6.022 × 10²³ ≈ 1.66 × 10⁻²⁴ g.
  • Avogadro's law — equal volumes of all gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules; that is why one mole of any gas fills 22.4 L at STP. Equal molecules is not equal atoms: 22.4 L of O₂ holds 2 mol of atoms, 22.4 L of NH₃ holds 4.
  • Particles inside particles: 1.8 g of water = 0.1 mol of H₂O = 6.022 × 10²² molecules, and each molecule has 10 electrons (1 + 1 + 8), so the sample holds 6.022 × 10²³ electrons. Moles first, then multiply by what each particle carries.

  • Limiting reagent and percentage yield (2017-E Q17): 2x + 3y + 4z → 5w, with 1 mol x, 3 mol y, 4 mol z. Counting atoms, not molecules. "Which contains the most atoms: 4 g He, 16 g O₂, 18 g H₂O, 22 g CO₂?" He: 1 mol of atoms. O₂: 0.5 mol of molecules × 2 = 1 mol of atoms. H₂O: 1 mol × 3 = 3 mol of atoms. CO₂: 0.5 mol × 3 = 1.5 mol. Water wins, though it has the smallest molecules — the question said atoms. Read the noun.

6Inside the atom — three particles, three models, one gold foil

Your IGCSE course starts with the answer (nucleus, shells). NCERT tells the story of how it was found, and Allen tests the story: which ray found which particle, what each model says, what the gold-foil experiment showed and what it could not.

How we know an atom can be divided. NCERT starts with static electricity (Activity 4.1): a comb run through dry hair picks up scraps of paper; a glass rod rubbed with silk attracts an inflated balloon. Rubbing makes objects charged, so the charge must already be in the atoms — an atom is divisible and contains charged particles. By 1900 the electron had been identified by J. J. Thomson. Even before that, in 1886, E. Goldstein found new radiations in a gas discharge and called them canal rays: positively charged radiations, which led to the proton — a charge equal and opposite to the electron's, and about 2000 times its mass. Electrons could be removed from atoms easily but protons could not, so the protons seemed to sit in the interior. (An atom with one proton and one electron carries no net charge — NCERT's own check.)

Why the rebounds were a shock. Rutherford expected only small deflections: α-particles are much heavier than protons, and fast. In his words the rebounds were "almost as incredible as if you fire a 15-inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and it comes back and hits you". NCERT's picture for the reasoning: a blindfolded child throwing stones at a wall hears every stone hit; throwing them at a barbed-wire fence, most pass through the gaps and only a few hit. The atom is the fence. And if the experiment were done with a foil of some other metal (NCERT's question)? Broadly the same pattern, because every atom has a nucleus — though a lighter metal, with less charge on each nucleus, would turn back fewer α-particles. Gold was chosen chiefly because it beats out into the thinnest foil.

Rutherford's model: a positively charged nucleus at the centre carrying nearly all the mass; electrons revolving round it in well-defined orbits; the nucleus tiny compared with the atom. Its drawback — NCERT's exact objection, and a favourite statement item: an electron on a circular orbit is accelerating (its direction keeps changing); an accelerating charge radiates energy; the electron should lose energy, spiral inwards and fall into the nucleus in a fraction of a second. Atoms would be unstable — and they are not.

Bohr's answer (1913): only certain orbits, called discrete orbits or energy levels (shells K, L, M, N…), are allowed; an electron on one of them does not radiate. Energy changes only when it jumps between shells. Note what Bohr keeps — the nucleus, the revolving electrons — and what he adds: the fixed orbits and the no-radiation rule. A statement item can swap the two names (worked question 5 below does); "electrons revolve in discrete orbits without radiating" is Bohr, not Rutherford.

The α-particle — three facts, two real questions

An α-particle is a helium nucleus: 2 protons + 2 neutrons, charge +2, mass 4 u, no electrons at all (2018-S Q18, option (4) "0"). Its charge-to-mass ratio is 2/4 = 0.5 per u; a proton's is 1/1 = 1 per u — so the α's is half the proton's (2017-E Q26, option (2)). The distractor "twice" reads the charge alone. Rutherford chose α-particles because they are heavy and fast, and positively charged so that the nucleus repels them.

The neutron. In 1932 J. Chadwick found a third particle with no charge and a mass nearly equal to a proton's. Neutrons are in the nucleus of every atom except ordinary hydrogen (protium), so the mass of an atom is the mass of its protons plus its neutrons — together called nucleons (section 7).

7Shells, configurations, valency — and counting p, e, n in ions

The 2n² rule gives the configuration, the configuration gives the valency, and the valency tells you what ion forms. Then the counting items: an ion keeps its protons and changes only its electrons.

Bohr–Bury rules, NCERT wording

  • A shell holds at most 2n² electrons: K (n = 1) 2, L (n = 2) 8, M (n = 3) 18, N (n = 4) 32.
  • The outermost shell holds at most 8, whatever 2n² says. That is why potassium is 2, 8, 8, 1 and calcium 2, 8, 8, 2, not 2, 8, 9 and 2, 8, 10 — the M shell could hold 18, but not while it is the outer one (2024-A Q27 plants "2, 8, 10" for calcium as a false statement).
  • Shells are filled stepwise — the next one is not used until the inner one is full. So an atom whose K and L shells are both full has 2 + 8 = 10 electrons (neon). Shells are written K, L, M, N… or n = 1, 2, 3, 4…
  • Valence electrons = the electrons in the outermost shell. Valency = that number if it is 1–4, or 8 − that number if it is 5–7; a full outer shell (2 for He, 8 for Ne, Ar) gives valency 0. Sodium 2, 8, 1 — one valence electron, valency 1 (2024-A Q27's "six" is false).
  • Octet — an outermost shell of eight electrons. Atoms whose outer shell is full show little chemical activity (the noble gases; helium's full shell is just two), so other atoms react — by sharing, gaining or losing electrons — to reach an octet, and the number of electrons gained, lost or shared is the valency.

Isotopes have the same chemical properties — same electrons, same configuration (2024-A Q27's true statement) — but different physical ones (mass, density, radioactivity). Sample Q25's distractors are the three things isotopes are not: allotropes (O₂ and O₃), states of one substance (ice and steam), two compounds (NO and NO₂). Each isotope is a pure substance, and many elements occur as a mixture of isotopes — which is why the atomic mass of an element is an average. No single chlorine atom weighs 35.5 u: a sample is 3 parts ³⁵Cl to 1 part ³⁷Cl, and 35.5 u is the average over the sample. Average atomic mass — forwards and backwards

Forwards: chlorine is 75 % ³⁵Cl and 25 % ³⁷Cl, so its atomic mass is 35 × 0.75 + 37 × 0.25 = 26.25 + 9.25 = 35.5 u — NCERT's own example, and the reason 35.5 sits in the mass table. The average is a weighted average: it lies nearer the commoner isotope.

Backwards (2018-S Q26): boron is 10.01 and 11.01, natural boron is 10.81. Let the fraction of the heavier be f: 10.01(1 − f) + 11.01 f = 10.81 → 10.01 + f = 10.81 → f = 0.80. So 20 % of the lighter, 80 % of the heavier — option (2), "20 %, 80 %", in the order the stem listed them. The distractor (3) "80 %, 20 %" is the same numbers the wrong way round: 10.81 is close to 11, so the heavier isotope must dominate. Sanity-check the direction before you bubble.

The shortcut, and where it breaks. In general the heavier isotope's fraction is f = (average − lighter) ÷ (heavier − lighter). When the two masses differ by 1 (10 and 11) the bottom is 1, so f is just "average − lighter" — the boron shortcut above. When they differ by 2, divide by 2. NCERT's own exercise: element X averages 16.2 u from ¹⁶X and ¹⁸X, so f = 0.2 ÷ 2 = 0.1 — 90 % ¹⁶X and 10 % ¹⁸X, not 80 : 20. And NCERT's bromine, forwards: 79 × 0.497 + 81 × 0.503 = 39.26 + 40.74 = 80.0 u.

The isotone item (2017-E Q18): X and Y have equal neutrons; A = 70 and 72; Z of X = 34. Neutrons in X = 70 − 34 = 36; so Y has 36 neutrons and Z = 72 − 36 = 36. Option (3). 12024-U Q16 machine, carried one step on · ~90 seconds

The carbonate of a metal X has the formula X₂CO₃ and a formula unit mass of 138 u. The formula unit mass of the nitrate of X is

1101 u
2109 u
3140 u
4163 u
Working

Step 1 — subtract the known part. CO₃ = 12 + 48 = 60, so 2X = 138 − 60 = 78 and X = 39: potassium.

Step 2 — read the valency off the formula. X₂CO₃ has two X per carbonate (valency 2), so X is monovalent — which potassium is.

Step 3 — rebuild. A monovalent metal with nitrate (NO₃⁻, valency 1) gives XNO₃ = 39 + 14 + 48 = 101 u. Option (1).

Where each wrong option comes from. (2) 109 — CO₃ taken as 44 (the mass of CO₂): 2X = 94, X = 47, then 47 + 62. (3) 140 — the 2 forgotten: X = 78, then 78 + 62. (4) 163 — X = 39 but the nitrate written X(NO₃)₂ as if X were divalent: 39 + 124. Elimination. X₂CO₃ says "valency 1" before any arithmetic, so the nitrate is XNO₃ and X = (option − 62): 39, 47, 78 or 101. Only one of those is a monovalent metal's mass — 39, potassium. 47 and 78 are no light metal, and 101 would be the nitrate's own mass. Knowing the mass table is the elimination; the valency only tells you which subtraction to do.
22018-S Q17 machine, with the particle count added · ~2 minutes

A sample of a gas weighs 16 g and occupies 11.2 L at STP. The gas, and the number of molecules in the sample, are

1CH₄, 6.022 × 10²³
2O₂, 3.011 × 10²³
3O₂, 6.022 × 10²³
4SO₂, 1.506 × 10²³
Working

Step 1 — volume to moles. n = 11.2 / 22.4 = 0.5 mol.

Step 2 — moles to molar mass. M = 16 / 0.5 = 32 g/mol → O₂ (CH₄ is 16, SO₂ is 64).

Step 3 — moles to molecules. 0.5 × 6.022 × 10²³ = 3.011 × 10²³. Option (2).

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) CH₄ — "16 g, so the molar mass is 16": the volume never used; and then a full mole of molecules. (3) O₂, 6.022 × 10²³ — right gas, but "one sample = one mole". (4) SO₂, 1.506 × 10²³ — M doubled to 64 (16 ÷ 0.25, with 11.2 L read as a quarter of 22.4), then 0.25 mol counted. Elimination. 11.2 L is half of 22.4 L, so the count must be half of the Avogadro number whatever the gas — (1) and (3) die on that alone, and (4) needs a quarter. Then 16 g ÷ ½ mol = 32.
32018-S Q27 + 2017-E Q18 in one item · ~2 minutes

An ion X²⁻ has 18 electrons and a mass number of 34. Which of the following atoms is an isotone of X?

13216S
23517Cl
34018Ar
44020Ca
Working

Step 1 — undo the charge. A 2− ion has gained two electrons, so the atom has 18 − 2 = 16 protons: X is sulphur.

Step 2 — neutrons. n = A − Z = 34 − 16 = 18.

Step 3 — find the atom with 18 neutrons. ³²S: 32 − 16 = 16. ³⁵Cl: 35 − 17 = 18 ✓. ⁴⁰Ar: 40 − 18 = 22. ⁴⁰Ca: 40 − 20 = 20. Option (2).

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) ³²S — "same element, so it must be related": that is an isotope, not an isotone. It is also where you land if you take the 18 electrons as 18 protons and then hunt for 34 − 18 = 16 neutrons. (3) ⁴⁰Ar — 18 electrons read as Z = 18, "so X is argon", and the argon option taken. (4) ⁴⁰Ca — the charge added instead of subtracted: 18 + 2 = 20, "so X is calcium". Elimination. Isotone means same neutrons, different element — so (1), the same element, is out at once. Then one subtraction per option — and remember the charge is subtracted from 18, not added, before any of them.
42018-S Q26 machine, carried into the nucleus · ~2 minutes

Lithium (Z = 3) has two isotopes, of mass numbers 6 and 7, and an atomic mass of 6.9 u. In a sample of 100 lithium atoms, the total number of neutrons in the atoms of the heavier isotope is

140
2270
3360
4630
Working

Step 1 — abundance. Fraction of ⁷Li = f: 6(1 − f) + 7f = 6.9 → 6 + f = 6.9 → f = 0.9. So 90 of the 100 atoms are ⁷Li (the average 6.9 sits near 7, so the heavier must dominate — sanity check).

Step 2 — neutrons per ⁷Li atom. n = A − Z = 7 − 3 = 4.

Step 3 — 90 × 4 = 360. Option (3).

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) 40 — the 10 lighter atoms' count, × 4: abundance the wrong way round. (2) 270 — 90 × 3: protons counted instead of neutrons. (4) 630 — 90 × 7: the whole mass number counted as neutrons. Elimination. Each ⁷Li atom has 4 neutrons and there are at most 100 atoms, so the answer is ≤ 400 — (4) is out; 6.9 near 7 means most atoms are ⁷Li, so (1) is out. Between (2) and (3), is it p or n?
5Statement format — 2024-U Q18 / 2024-A Q27 style · ~90 seconds

Consider the statements:

  1. The charge-to-mass ratio of an α-particle is half that of a proton.
  2. The discovery of canal rays led to the discovery of the proton.
  3. In Rutherford's model of the atom, electrons revolve in discrete orbits without radiating energy.
  4. The mass of a neutron is approximately equal to the mass of an electron.

The correct statements are

1I and II only
2I, II and III only
3II and IV only
4I, II, III and IV
Working

I — true. α: charge 2, mass 4 → ½ per u. Proton: 1 per u. Half.

II — true. Goldstein's canal rays (1886) were positively charged; the particle behind them was named the proton.

III — false. Discrete, non-radiating orbits are Bohr's postulate — they are precisely what Rutherford's model lacked, and the reason it predicted an unstable atom.

IV — false. A neutron has about the mass of a proton, ~1840 times an electron's.

I and II only: option (1).

Elimination. Statement IV is the 2024-U Q18 trap in another coat and is plainly false, so (3) and (4) go first. Between (1) and (2) the only question is whose orbits are "discrete" — the word belongs to Bohr. Statement items are four mini-questions with −1 attached: judge each clause on its own, and do not let two true ones carry a third.
