1The words NCERT uses — and what is new since IGCSE

Same particles, different vocabulary. IGCSE says relative formula mass, proton number and charge on the ion; NCERT says formula unit mass, atomic number and valency. Allen's stems use the NCERT words, and a few of them are traps in themselves.

Definitions, NCERT wording

  • Atom — NCERT calls atoms "the building blocks of all matter"; the working definition is the smallest particle of an element that takes part in a chemical reaction. It may or may not exist on its own (argon does; hydrogen does not).
  • Molecule — a group of two or more atoms chemically bonded together; the smallest particle of an element or a compound that can exist independently and shows all its properties. A molecule of an element (O₂, S₈) or of a compound (H₂O, CO₂).
  • Atomicity — the number of atoms in one molecule. Monoatomic He, Ne, Ar and the metals; diatomic H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂ (and HCl, CO, NO); triatomic O₃, H₂O, CO₂ — and SiO₂, which is why 2017-E Q16 keys "silica is a triatomic compound"; tetra-atomic P₄; S₈ has eight.
  • Ion — a charged particle, one atom or a group of atoms. Positive = cation, negative = anion; a charged group of atoms is a polyatomic ion (NH₄⁺, SO₄²⁻).
  • Valency — the combining capacity of an element: the number of electrons an atom gains, loses or shares to complete its outer shell. A number, never signed (Mg has valency 2; the Mg²⁺ ion has charge +2).
  • Atomic mass unit, u — one-twelfth of the mass of one carbon-12 atom (the older symbol amu is the same thing). Atomic mass of an element = the mass of its atom in u, relative to that.
  • Molecular mass — the sum of the atomic masses of all atoms in a molecule, in u. Formula unit mass — the same sum for the formula of an ionic compound (NaCl = 58.5 u), because an ionic solid has no molecules. Allen ignores the distinction and writes "molecular mass of XCl₂" even after calling it ionic (2024-U Q16) — do not let it stop you.
  • Mole — the amount of a substance containing 6.022 × 10²³ particles (the Avogadro number). Molar mass — the mass of one mole, in grams: numerically the atomic, molecular or formula unit mass with g/mol on it.

New since IGCSE — the parts of this page you have not met

  • The laws of chemical combination by name (Lavoisier, Proust) and Dalton's postulates — section 2.
  • Valency as a word, the NCERT ion table, and the old -ous / -ic metal names and -ite / -ate anion names — section 4.
  • 22.4 L at STP — NCERT's molar gas volume (0 °C, 1 atm), not IGCSE's 24 dm³ at r.t.p. — section 5.
  • The discovery story: cathode rays → electron (Thomson), canal rays → proton (Goldstein), Chadwick's neutron; the Thomson, Rutherford and Bohr models and the gold-foil experiment with its three observations and three conclusions; the 2n² (Bohr–Bury) rule — sections 6–7.
  • Isobars and isotones next to isotopes — section 8. IGCSE stops at isotopes.

2The laws of chemical combination — the "which law" and "how much" items

Two laws, one man each, and Dalton's theory that explained them. 2018-S Q16 asks you to name the law from one fact; 2024-A Q19 (and Sample Q20) asks you to use it.

Two laws, NCERT wording

  • Law of conservation of mass (Lavoisier) — mass can neither be created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction. Total mass of reactants = total mass of products.
  • Law of constant proportions (Proust) — in a chemical substance the elements are always present in definite proportions by mass, whatever the source or the method of preparation. Also called the law of definite proportions. NCERT's two ratios: water H : O = 1 : 8; ammonia N : H = 14 : 3 — and from its exercises, carbon dioxide C : O = 3 : 8 (3 g of carbon in 8 g of oxygen gives 11 g of CO₂).

Dalton's atomic theory (1808) explained both: matter is made of tiny indivisible atoms; atoms can be neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction (→ conservation of mass); atoms of one element are identical in mass and chemical properties, and differ from those of other elements; atoms combine in ratios of small whole numbers, and the relative number and kinds of atoms in a compound are constant (→ constant proportions). Two postulates are now known to be wrong — atoms are divisible (section 6) and atoms of one element are not all identical in mass (isotopes, section 8). Allen's statement items like to plant exactly those two.

Using the law — the "how much" machine

"CO₂ contains 27.2 % carbon by mass. What mass of oxygen combines with 10 g of carbon?" (2024-A Q19, Sample Q20). Oxygen is 100 − 27.2 = 72.8 %, so the C : O ratio is 27.2 : 72.8 in every sample. Oxygen for 10 g of carbon = 10 × 72.8 / 27.2 = 26.7 g — option (1). The distractor 3.74 g is the ratio upside down (10 × 27.2 / 72.8); 7.28 g is 10 × 72.8 % — the percentage of oxygen applied to the carbon as if 10 g were the whole; 4.28 g has no route worth knowing. Check with NCERT's 3 : 8 — 10 g of carbon takes 26.67 g of oxygen. Same answer, which is the whole point of the law.

The other direction: "3 g of hydrogen burns in 30 g of oxygen — how much water forms?" Water is 1 : 8, so 3 g of hydrogen uses 24 g of oxygen and gives 27 g of water, with 6 g of oxygen left unreacted. The reactant that runs out first fixes the product (that is the limiting reagent, section 5, in disguise).

The "find X" machine (2024-U Q16) — subtract, identify, rebuild

"An ionic compound of X and chlorine has the formula XCl₂ and molecular mass 95 u. What is the molecular mass of the oxide of X?" Three steps, each one line:

  1. Subtract what you know. XCl₂ = X + 2 × 35.5 = X + 71 = 95, so X = 24.
  2. Identify. 24 u is magnesium. (Not strictly needed — but it tells you X is divalent, which the formula XCl₂ already said.)
  3. Rebuild. X is divalent, oxygen is divalent, so the oxide is XO = 24 + 16 = 40 u. Option (2).

The distractors are the three slips: 56 is 24 + 32 — the oxide written XO₂ by copying the chloride's subscript; 64 is 48 + 16 — X₂O, the oxide written as if X were monovalent like sodium; 80 is 2 × 40 — X₂O₂, the criss-cross never cancelled. The valency of X is fixed by the chloride: two chlorides (valency 1 each) mean X is 2, and one oxygen (valency 2) then balances it. Formula-writing is section 4.

The naming rules the old names follow

  • -ous / -ic for a metal with two valencies: -ous is the lower, -ic the higher. Ferrous Fe²⁺ / ferric Fe³⁺; cuprous Cu⁺ / cupric Cu²⁺; plumbous Pb²⁺ / plumbic Pb⁴⁺; stannous Sn²⁺ / stannic Sn⁴⁺; mercurous Hg₂²⁺ / mercuric Hg²⁺. IGCSE writes iron(II) and iron(III); Allen writes both.
  • -ate / -ite for an oxo-anion: -ate has one more oxygen than -ite, same charge. Sulphate SO₄²⁻ / sulphite SO₃²⁻; nitrate NO₃⁻ / nitrite NO₂⁻; chlorate ClO₃⁻ / chlorite ClO₂⁻; phosphate PO₄³⁻ / phosphite PO₃³⁻. -ide is a single element (chloride, oxide, sulphide) — with two exceptions you must know as words, hydroxide OH⁻ and cyanide CN⁻.
  • Hydrogen- in front (bi- in the old style) adds an H⁺ and drops one charge: carbonate CO₃²⁻ → hydrogencarbonate HCO₃⁻; sulphate SO₄²⁻ → hydrogensulphate HSO₄⁻.

Criss-cross. Write the symbols, write each valency above, swap them down as subscripts, cancel a common factor, bracket a polyatomic ion that gets a subscript bigger than 1. So cupric nitrite is Cu²⁺ and NO₂⁻ → Cu(NO₂)₂ (4 oxygens); ferric sulphite is Fe³⁺ and SO₃²⁻ → Fe₂(SO₃)₃ (9 oxygens); the ratio 2017-E Q19 wants is 4 : 9. Aluminium sulphate Al₂(SO₄)₃; calcium phosphate Ca₃(PO₄)₂; ammonium sulphate (NH₄)₂SO₄; magnesium oxide Mg²⁺O²⁻ → Mg₂O₂ → cancel → MgO; ferric oxide Fe₂O₃, ferrous oxide FeO. The formula of a covalent compound comes from the same valencies — H₂O (H 1, O 2), NH₃ (N 3), CH₄ (C 4), CO₂, SO₂, H₂S, HCl, N₂O₅ (N 5 here, O 2).

5The mole — mass, particles, and 22.4 litres at STP

The mole is the bridge between the mass you weigh and the atoms you count. NCERT gives two arms; Allen adds a third, the volume of a gas at STP, and two 2018 items stand on it.

n = m / M
moles = mass in g ÷ molar mass in g/mol. 4.4 g CO₂ = 4.4/44 = 0.1 mol.
N = n × 6.022 × 10²³
particles — atoms, molecules or ions, whichever the formula counts. 0.1 mol CO₂ = 6.022 × 10²² molecules = 1.8 × 10²³ atoms.
V = n × 22.4 L (gas, STP)
STP = 0 °C, 1 atm. 0.1 mol of any gas = 2.24 L (2018-S Q19). Not IGCSE's 24 dm³ at r.t.p. — Allen's options are built on 22.4.
% of E = (atoms × AE / M) × 100
C in CO₂ = 12/44 = 27.3 %; H in H₂O = 2/18 = 11.1 %, O = 88.9 %.

The two 2018 items — and the one 2017 item past the chapter

  • "7.5 g of a gas occupies 5.6 L at STP. The gas is?" (2018-S Q17.) n = 5.6/22.4 = 0.25 mol; M = 7.5/0.25 = 30 g/mol; NO is 14 + 16 = 30 → option (1). CO is 28, N₂O and CO₂ are both 44 — Allen planted two 44s so that "about 30" cannot be guessed as "about 44".
  • "Volume occupied by 4.4 g of CO₂" (2018-S Q19) — STP is not even stated; assume it. 0.1 mol × 22.4 = 2.24 L. The distractor 22.4 L is one mole's volume; 224 L is a decimal slip; and the fourth option is "None of these", which the unstated STP is there to tempt you into — do not take it. With 24 dm³ you would get 2.4 L, which is not an option — that is your signal that Allen means 22.4.
  • Limiting reagent and percentage yield (2017-E Q17): 2x + 3y + 4z → 5w, with 1 mol x, 3 mol y, 4 mol z. Divide each amount by its coefficient: x 1/2 = 0.5, y 3/3 = 1, z 4/4 = 1 — x is the smallest, so x limits. 1 mol of x makes 5/2 = 2.5 mol of w in theory; 1.25 mol was obtained; yield = 1.25/2.5 = 50 %. The trap is y and z, which are exactly stoichiometric and tempt you to say "100 %". This is Class 11 material and IGCSE Supplement; Allen asked it anyway.

Counting atoms, not molecules. "Which contains the most atoms: 4 g He, 16 g O₂, 18 g H₂O, 22 g CO₂?" He: 1 mol of atoms. O₂: 0.5 mol of molecules × 2 = 1 mol of atoms. H₂O: 1 mol × 3 = 3 mol of atoms. CO₂: 0.5 mol × 3 = 1.5 mol. Water wins, though it has the smallest molecules — the question said atoms. Read the noun.

6Inside the atom — three particles, three models, one gold foil

Your IGCSE course starts with the answer (nucleus, shells). NCERT tells the story of how it was found, and Allen tests the story: which ray found which particle, what each model says, what the gold-foil experiment showed and what it could not.

ParticleChargeMassWhereFound by
Electron, e⁻−1≈ 1/2000 of a hydrogen atom (about 1/1840 of a proton) — "negligible"Outside the nucleus, in shellsJ. J. Thomson, 1897 — from cathode rays in a discharge tube

So the mass of an atom sits in the nucleus (mass number A = p + n), its identity is its proton count (atomic number Z = p), and its chemistry is its electrons. 2024-U Q18 turns on the mass row: "the mass of an electron is equal to the mass of a neutron" is the incorrect statement — it is about 1/1840 of it. The other three (negative, present in every atom, the constituent of cathode rays) are all true.

Rutherford's gold-foil experiment (1911)

ObservationConclusion
Most α-particles passed straight through the foil.Most of the space inside an atom is empty.
Some were deflected through small angles.The positive charge occupies very little space — a passing α is repelled only when it comes close.
Very few — about 1 in 12 000 — bounced straight back.All the positive charge and (nearly all) the mass are concentrated in a very small volume: the nucleus. Its radius is about 10⁵ times smaller than the atom's.

Rutherford's model: a positively charged nucleus at the centre carrying nearly all the mass; electrons revolving round it in well-defined orbits; the nucleus tiny compared with the atom. Its drawback — NCERT's exact objection, and a favourite statement item: an electron on a circular orbit is accelerating (its direction keeps changing); an accelerating charge radiates energy; the electron should lose energy, spiral inwards and fall into the nucleus in a fraction of a second. Atoms would be unstable — and they are not.

Bohr's answer (1913): only certain orbits, called discrete orbits or energy levels (shells K, L, M, N…), are allowed; an electron on one of them does not radiate. Energy changes only when it jumps between shells. Note what Bohr keeps — the nucleus, the revolving electrons — and what he adds: the fixed orbits and the no-radiation rule. A statement item can swap the two names (worked question 5 below does); "electrons revolve in discrete orbits without radiating" is Bohr, not Rutherford.

The α-particle — three facts, two real questions

An α-particle is a helium nucleus: 2 protons + 2 neutrons, charge +2, mass 4 u, no electrons at all (2018-S Q18, option (4) "0"). Its charge-to-mass ratio is 2/4 = 0.5 per u; a proton's is 1/1 = 1 per u — so the α's is half the proton's (2017-E Q26, option (2)). The distractor "twice" reads the charge alone. Rutherford chose α-particles because they are heavy and fast, and positively charged so that the nucleus repels them.

7Shells, configurations, valency — and counting p, e, n in ions

The 2n² rule gives the configuration, the configuration gives the valency, and the valency tells you what ion forms. Then the counting items: an ion keeps its protons and changes only its electrons.

Bohr–Bury rules, NCERT wording

  • A shell holds at most 2n² electrons: K (n = 1) 2, L (n = 2) 8, M (n = 3) 18, N (n = 4) 32.
  • The outermost shell holds at most 8, whatever 2n² says. That is why potassium is 2, 8, 8, 1 and calcium 2, 8, 8, 2, not 2, 8, 9 and 2, 8, 10 — the M shell could hold 18, but not while it is the outer one (2024-A Q27 plants "2, 8, 10" for calcium as a false statement).
  • Shells are filled stepwise — the next one is not used until the inner one is full.
  • Valence electrons = the electrons in the outermost shell. Valency = that number if it is 1–4, or 8 − that number if it is 5–7; a full outer shell (2 for He, 8 for Ne, Ar) gives valency 0. Sodium 2, 8, 1 — one valence electron, valency 1 (2024-A Q27's "six" is false).

Lose to go positive, gain to go negative. Neutrons untouched.

The four counting items, worked

  • 2024-U Q26: X, Y, Z have 8, 16, 17 protons — oxygen, sulphur, chlorine. Their most stable anions reach the nearest full shell: O²⁻ (2,8) has 10, S²⁻ (2,8,8) has 18, Cl⁻ (2,8,8) has 18. Option (2). The distractor (3) "8, 18, 18" forgot that oxygen gains two.
  • 2024-A Q18: same number of electrons? O²⁻ 8 + 2 = 10, F⁻ 9 + 1 = 10 ✓ (option 1). Mg²⁺ 10 but Ar 18; P⁵⁺ 10 but S²⁻ 18; Na 11 but Al³⁺ 10. Species with equal electron counts are isoelectronic — Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺, F⁻, O²⁻, N³⁻ and Ne are the classic ten-electron set.
  • 2018-S Q27: a monoatomic anion of unit charge with 18 electrons and 18 neutrons. One extra electron, so p = 17; A = 17 + 18 = 35. Option (2) — it is ³⁵Cl⁻. The distractor "18, 18" used the electron count as the atomic number; "17, 18" gets Z right and then writes the neutron count where the mass number goes.
  • Sample Q17: read the table. A: 10 e, 12 p → Mg²⁺, a cation ✓; C: 18 e, 17 p → Cl⁻, an anion ✓ — so statement (1) is true. B and C both have 17 protons → the same element (Cl and Cl⁻), so statement (2) "different elements" is the incorrect one. E: 12 p + 12 n = 24, magnesium, a metal ✓.

Isotopes have the same chemical properties — same electrons, same configuration (2024-A Q27's true statement) — but different physical ones (mass, density, radioactivity). Sample Q25's distractors are the three things isotopes are not: allotropes (O₂ and O₃), states of one substance (ice and steam), two compounds (NO and NO₂). NCERT's three uses — an isotope of uranium as a nuclear-reactor fuel, of cobalt in cancer treatment, of iodine for goitre (the book names no mass numbers; they are U-235, Co-60 and I-131).

Average atomic mass — forwards and backwards

Forwards: chlorine is 75 % ³⁵Cl and 25 % ³⁷Cl, so its atomic mass is 35 × 0.75 + 37 × 0.25 = 26.25 + 9.25 = 35.5 u — NCERT's own example, and the reason 35.5 sits in the mass table. The average is a weighted average: it lies nearer the commoner isotope.

Backwards (2018-S Q26): boron is 10.01 and 11.01, natural boron is 10.81. Let the fraction of the heavier be f: 10.01(1 − f) + 11.01 f = 10.81 → 10.01 + f = 10.81 → f = 0.80. So 20 % of the lighter, 80 % of the heavier — option (2), "20 %, 80 %", in the order the stem listed them. The distractor (3) "80 %, 20 %" is the same numbers the wrong way round: 10.81 is close to 11, so the heavier isotope must dominate. Sanity-check the direction before you bubble.

The isotone item (2017-E Q18): X and Y have equal neutrons; A = 70 and 72; Z of X = 34. Neutrons in X = 70 − 34 = 36; so Y has 36 neutrons and Z = 72 − 36 = 36. Option (3). The distractor 34 assumes "same Z" (isotopes); 38 is 72 − 34 — Y's mass number minus X's atomic number, two nuclides mixed in one subtraction.

10Five worked questions — at TALLENTEX pitch

Try each before opening the working and give yourself 90 seconds — the real paper's average. The elimination reasoning is written out because on the day, ruling two options out is worth almost as much as solving.

12024-U Q16 machine, carried one step on · ~90 seconds

The carbonate of a metal X has the formula X₂CO₃ and a formula unit mass of 138 u. The formula unit mass of the nitrate of X is

1101 u
2109 u
3140 u
4163 u
Working

Step 1 — subtract the known part. CO₃ = 12 + 48 = 60, so 2X = 138 − 60 = 78 and X = 39: potassium.

Step 2 — read the valency off the formula. X₂CO₃ has two X per carbonate (valency 2), so X is monovalent — which potassium is.

Step 3 — rebuild. A monovalent metal with nitrate (NO₃⁻, valency 1) gives XNO₃ = 39 + 14 + 48 = 101 u. Option (1).

Where each wrong option comes from. (2) 109 — CO₃ taken as 44 (the mass of CO₂): 2X = 94, X = 47, then 47 + 62. (3) 140 — the 2 forgotten: X = 78, then 78 + 62. (4) 163 — X = 39 but the nitrate written X(NO₃)₂ as if X were divalent: 39 + 124. Elimination. X₂CO₃ says "valency 1" before any arithmetic, so the nitrate is XNO₃ and X = (option − 62): 39, 47, 78 or 101. Only one of those is a monovalent metal's mass — 39, potassium. 47 and 78 are no light metal, and 101 would be the nitrate's own mass. Knowing the mass table is the elimination; the valency only tells you which subtraction to do.
22018-S Q17 machine, with the particle count added · ~2 minutes

A sample of a gas weighs 16 g and occupies 11.2 L at STP. The gas, and the number of molecules in the sample, are

1CH₄, 6.022 × 10²³
2O₂, 3.011 × 10²³
3O₂, 6.022 × 10²³
4SO₂, 1.506 × 10²³
Working

Step 1 — volume to moles. n = 11.2 / 22.4 = 0.5 mol.

Step 2 — moles to molar mass. M = 16 / 0.5 = 32 g/mol → O₂ (CH₄ is 16, SO₂ is 64).

Step 3 — moles to molecules. 0.5 × 6.022 × 10²³ = 3.011 × 10²³. Option (2).

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) CH₄ — "16 g, so the molar mass is 16": the volume never used; and then a full mole of molecules. (3) O₂, 6.022 × 10²³ — right gas, but "one sample = one mole". (4) SO₂, 1.506 × 10²³ — M doubled to 64 (16 ÷ 0.25, with 11.2 L read as a quarter of 22.4), then 0.25 mol counted. Elimination. 11.2 L is half of 22.4 L, so the count must be half of the Avogadro number whatever the gas — (1) and (3) die on that alone, and (4) needs a quarter. Then 16 g ÷ ½ mol = 32.
32018-S Q27 + 2017-E Q18 in one item · ~2 minutes

An ion X²⁻ has 18 electrons and a mass number of 34. Which of the following atoms is an isotone of X?

13216S
23517Cl
34018Ar
44020Ca
Working

Step 1 — undo the charge. A 2− ion has gained two electrons, so the atom has 18 − 2 = 16 protons: X is sulphur.

Step 2 — neutrons. n = A − Z = 34 − 16 = 18.

Step 3 — find the atom with 18 neutrons. ³²S: 32 − 16 = 16. ³⁵Cl: 35 − 17 = 18 ✓. ⁴⁰Ar: 40 − 18 = 22. ⁴⁰Ca: 40 − 20 = 20. Option (2).

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) ³²S — "same element, so it must be related": that is an isotope, not an isotone. It is also where you land if you take the 18 electrons as 18 protons and then hunt for 34 − 18 = 16 neutrons. (3) ⁴⁰Ar — 18 electrons read as Z = 18, "so X is argon", and the argon option taken. (4) ⁴⁰Ca — the charge added instead of subtracted: 18 + 2 = 20, "so X is calcium". Elimination. Isotone means same neutrons, different element — so (1), the same element, is out at once. Then one subtraction per option — and remember the charge is subtracted from 18, not added, before any of them.
42018-S Q26 machine, carried into the nucleus · ~2 minutes

Lithium (Z = 3) has two isotopes, of mass numbers 6 and 7, and an atomic mass of 6.9 u. In a sample of 100 lithium atoms, the total number of neutrons in the atoms of the heavier isotope is

140
2270
3360
4630
Working

Step 1 — abundance. Fraction of ⁷Li = f: 6(1 − f) + 7f = 6.9 → 6 + f = 6.9 → f = 0.9. So 90 of the 100 atoms are ⁷Li (the average 6.9 sits near 7, so the heavier must dominate — sanity check).

Step 2 — neutrons per ⁷Li atom. n = A − Z = 7 − 3 = 4.

Step 3 — 90 × 4 = 360. Option (3).

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) 40 — the 10 lighter atoms' count, × 4: abundance the wrong way round. (2) 270 — 90 × 3: protons counted instead of neutrons. (4) 630 — 90 × 7: the whole mass number counted as neutrons. Elimination. Each ⁷Li atom has 4 neutrons and there are at most 100 atoms, so the answer is ≤ 400 — (4) is out; 6.9 near 7 means most atoms are ⁷Li, so (1) is out. Between (2) and (3), is it p or n?
5Statement format — 2024-U Q18 / 2024-A Q27 style · ~90 seconds

Consider the statements:

  1. The charge-to-mass ratio of an α-particle is half that of a proton.
  2. The discovery of canal rays led to the discovery of the proton.
  3. In Rutherford's model of the atom, electrons revolve in discrete orbits without radiating energy.
  4. The mass of a neutron is approximately equal to the mass of an electron.

The correct statements are

1I and II only
2I, II and III only
3II and IV only
4I, II, III and IV
Working

I — true. α: charge 2, mass 4 → ½ per u. Proton: 1 per u. Half.

II — true. Goldstein's canal rays (1886) were positively charged; the particle behind them was named the proton.

III — false. Discrete, non-radiating orbits are Bohr's postulate — they are precisely what Rutherford's model lacked, and the reason it predicted an unstable atom.

IV — false. A neutron has about the mass of a proton, ~1840 times an electron's.

I and II only: option (1).

Elimination. Statement IV is the 2024-U Q18 trap in another coat and is plainly false, so (3) and (4) go first. Between (1) and (2) the only question is whose orbits are "discrete" — the word belongs to Bohr. Statement items are four mini-questions with −1 attached: judge each clause on its own, and do not let two true ones carry a third.
