Sound

Official topic: Sound (Class 9) — one topic, one card. Your IGCSE course already has most of it (0625 §3.4): vibrating sources, longitudinal waves, compressions and rarefactions, the need for a medium, solid > liquid > gas, the 20 Hz – 20 kHz range, pitch and loudness, echoes, ultrasound and sonar. What this page adds is the NCERT framing Allen writes in — the wave drawn as a pressure graph with a sign to read, mechanical waves, quality, tone and note, the 0.1 s / 17.2 m echo rule, reverberation and the uses of multiple reflection, infrasound and its named animals — plus the two counting traps and the one humidity fact the real papers have used. Four things marked “cut from NCERT” below were dropped from the rationalised NCERT book in 2022; they stay here, briefly, because the official topic is simply “Sound” and your IGCSE course and the hub's Physics paper use them. A 25-minute read; the paper is the point.

1How sound is made, and how it travels

Two sentences carry the whole chapter: sound is produced by vibrating objects, and sound needs a medium — it travels as a chain of pushes from particle to particle.

NCERT's words

Sound is a form of energy which produces a sensation of hearing in our ears. It is produced by vibration — a rapid to-and-fro motion of an object: a struck tuning fork, a plucked string, the stretched skin of a drum, our vocal cords, the wings of a bee.

The matter or substance through which sound is transmitted is called a medium. It can be a solid, a liquid or a gas. Sound cannot travel through a vacuum — the bell-jar experiment: pump the air out of a jar with an electric bell ringing inside and the sound fades although you can still see the hammer striking. That is also why astronauts on the Moon talk by radio. (NCERT's “Sound needs a medium to travel” section was cut from the book in 2022 — but the fact is in your IGCSE syllabus, and it is exactly what 2017-E Q1's “mechanical” means.)

The particles of the medium do not travel from the source to your ear. Each one vibrates about its own resting place and pushes its neighbour; it is the disturbance that travels. When the prong of a tuning fork moves forward it squashes the air in front into a region of high pressure and high density — a compression (C); when it moves back it leaves a region of low pressure and low density — a rarefaction (R). The fork keeps vibrating, so a train of C, R, C, R … moves out through the air.

Mechanical — and reading the wave picture (2024-U Q13)

A wave that needs a material medium is a mechanical wave. Sound is mechanical and longitudinal — 2017-E Q1's answer, word for word. Light is non-mechanical (it crosses the vacuum of space) and transverse.

When the wave is drawn as a curve over a dashed mean line, the mean line is the undisturbed (atmospheric) pressure. A point above it — a crest — is increased pressure, a compression; a point below it — a trough — is decreased pressure, a rarefaction. A point on the line itself is at atmospheric pressure. Read the side of the line, not just the distance from it.

Longitudinal and transverse

Longitudinal wave: the particles of the medium move parallel to the direction in which the disturbance travels. Sound in air (and in any fluid) is longitudinal.

Transverse wave: the particles move perpendicular to the direction of travel — a ripple on water, a wave along a shaken rope. Light is a transverse wave, but it is not mechanical: it needs no medium.

Three slips Allen plants here

  • "The air particles travel from the source to the ear" — false; only the disturbance (and the energy) travels.
  • "Sound can travel through vacuum, only more slowly" — false; it cannot travel at all.
  • "The distance between a compression and the next rarefaction is one wavelength" — false; it is half a wavelength. This single slip doubles or halves every number that follows from it.

The two counting traps (2018-S Q1, Sample Q1)

  • "Per minute." Frequency is per second. 54 waves a minute is 54/60 = 0.9 Hz, not 54 Hz — with λ = 10 m, v = 9 m/s, not 540 m/s.
  • "Three consecutive troughs." Count the gaps, not the troughs: three troughs have two gaps between them, so they span 2λ. Four consecutive compressions span 3λ. n crests span (n − 1)λ.
  • A snapshot with a marked span. Count the complete waves (one crest + one trough each) inside the span: λ = span ÷ number of complete waves. Three waves in 1.5 m → λ = 0.5 m.

v = fλ holds for every wave — Allen set both of these on water and string waves, inside the Sound topic.

Pitch, loudness and quality

  • Pitch is how the brain reads frequency: faster vibration → higher frequency → higher pitch. A woman's or a child's voice is usually higher-pitched than a man's; a bird's note higher than a lion's roar.
  • Loudness is how the ear senses the amplitude: a harder strike moves more energy, gives a larger amplitude and a louder sound. NCERT: loudness is a measure of the response of the ear to the sound. Loudness is usually expressed in decibels (dB). As a sound travels away from its source, its amplitude — and loudness — falls.
  • Quality or timbre is what lets you tell two sources apart when they play the same pitch at the same loudness — a sitar and a flute on the same note. It depends on the shape of the wave.
  • A sound of a single frequency is a tone; a sound made of several frequencies is a note and is pleasant to listen to. Noise is unpleasant to the ear. Music is pleasant to hear and of rich quality.

The pairing Allen tests — and swaps

Pitch ↔ frequency. Loudness ↔ amplitude. Quality ↔ waveform. "Loudness depends on frequency" and "pitch depends on amplitude" are the planted false statements. And none of the three changes the speed: the speed of sound is set by the medium, so a high note and a low note from the same band reach you together. If f goes up in the same medium, λ goes down — v = fλ stays fixed.

3Speed of sound — the medium decides it

Sound travels fastest in solids, slower in liquids, slowest in gases — and faster in warmer air. When sound changes medium, the frequency stays; the speed and the wavelength change.

Values from NCERT's table of speeds (at 25 °C unless stated). Papers round them: air ≈ 340 m/s, water ≈ 1500 m/s — always use the value the question gives. Notice hydrogen: still slower than any solid or liquid in the table, but about 3.7 times faster than air — a lighter gas carries sound faster. The ordering to remember is solid > liquid > gas, and the speed in a gas rises with temperature (331 → 346 m/s from 0 °C to 25 °C).

Rainy day or dry day? (2024-A Q13)

At the same temperature, sound travels faster in humid air than in dry air. Water vapour molecules (molar mass 18) are lighter than the nitrogen and oxygen they replace (about 29 on average), so moist air is less dense, and a lighter gas carries sound faster — the same reason hydrogen beats air in the table. So Vrainy > Vdry. This is beyond NCERT's text, which names only the medium and the temperature; Allen asked it anyway. The trap is "wet air feels heavier, so it must be slower".

Crossing into a new medium — the rule every two-medium item turns on

The frequency is fixed by the source: the source makes a set number of compressions each second, and the boundary cannot create or destroy any of them. So when sound passes from air into water, f stays the same, v rises, and λ = v/f rises in the same ratio.

680 Hz in air (340 m/s): λ = 0.5 m  →  in water (1500 m/s): λ = 1500/680 ≈ 2.2 m, still 680 Hz

The planted slip is to keep λ and change f (1500/0.5 = 3000 Hz). A frequency never changes with the medium; the pitch you hear under water is the pitch of the source.

Light first, sound later

Light travels at 3 × 10⁸ m/s — practically instantly over any distance on Earth — so you see lightning before you hear thunder, and the delay is the sound's travel time: distance = vsound × delay. A 3 s delay in 340 m/s air puts the storm about 1 km away.

Sonic boom · cut from NCERT

An object moving faster than sound is travelling at a supersonic speed. Bullets and jet aircraft often do. It produces shock waves in air, which carry a large amount of energy; the sharp, loud sound they cause is a sonic boom — strong enough to shatter glass and damage buildings.

4Reflection, echo and reverberation

Sound reflects off a solid or liquid surface by the same laws as light. An echo is a reflection you can hear separately — which needs a gap of 0.1 s, and so a distance of about 17 m.

Laws of reflection of sound (NCERT)

The directions in which the sound is incident and reflected make equal angles with the normal to the reflecting surface at the point of incidence, and the three are in the same plane. NCERT's reflector is "an obstacle of large size which may be polished or rough" — large, that is, compared with the wavelength.

Echo — the 0.1 s rule

The sensation of a sound persists in our brain for about 0.1 s. To hear a distinct echo the reflected sound must arrive at least 0.1 s after the original. In that 0.1 s the sound makes a round trip, so at 344 m/s (air at 22 °C, NCERT's figure):

total path = 344 × 0.1 = 34.4 m → minimum distance of the obstacle = 34.4 / 2 = 17.2 m

The minimum distance changes with temperature, because the speed does: warmer air, faster sound, a larger minimum distance (at 340 m/s it is 17 m; at 350 m/s, 17.5 m).

d = v t / 2
t is the time from the sound to its echo — a round trip. The single most-missed halving in the chapter.
dmin = v × 0.1 / 2
= 17.2 m at 344 m/s. Scales with v.

Reverberation

In a big hall, repeated reflections from the walls, ceiling and floor make a sound persist after the source has stopped. This persistence by repeated reflection is reverberation; too much of it makes speech blurred. It is reduced by covering the roof and walls with sound-absorbent material — compressed fibreboard, rough plaster, draperies — and by seats made of materials that absorb sound.

Uses of multiple reflection of sound

  • Megaphones, loudhailers, horns, trumpets and shehanais are shaped as a tube with a flared (conical) opening, so that sound is reflected successively and sent forward in one direction instead of spreading out.
  • Stethoscope: the sound of the patient's heartbeat reaches the doctor's ears by multiple reflection along the tube.
  • Curved ceilings of concert halls and auditoriums send the reflected sound to every corner of the hall.
  • A soundboard — a curved board placed behind the stage — reflects the speaker's sound evenly across the hall.

5Range of hearing — infrasound, ultrasound and SONAR

Humans hear from about 20 Hz to 20 kHz. Everything below is infrasound, everything above is ultrasound — and NCERT names the animals and the machines, which is what a recall item would ask.

RangeFrequencyWho / what
Infrasonic (infrasound)below 20 Hza vibrating simple pendulum; rhinoceroses communicate using infrasound as low as 5 Hz; whales and elephants produce it; earthquakes produce low-frequency infrasound before the main shock waves begin, which may alert animals
Audible20 Hz – 20 kHzthe normal human ear
Ultrasonic (ultrasound)above 20 kHzdolphins, bats and porpoises produce it; some moths can hear it — which helps them escape a hunting bat; rats play games using ultrasound. Children under five and some animals, such as dogs, can hear up to 25 kHz — into this range

Why ultrasound is useful — and what it is used for (NCERT's list)

Ultrasound has a high frequency and so a short wavelength, so it can travel along well-defined paths even in the presence of obstacles. Its uses:

  • cleaning parts in hard-to-reach places — spiral tubes, odd-shaped parts, electronic components — dipped in a cleaning solution through which ultrasound is sent;
  • detecting cracks and flaws in metal blocks used in bridges and buildings — the ultrasound is reflected back from a defect instead of passing through;
  • echocardiography — ultrasound reflected from parts of the heart forms an image of the heart;
  • ultrasonography — imaging the liver, gall bladder, uterus, kidney, and a foetus during pregnancy;
  • breaking small stones formed in the kidney into fine grains that are flushed out with urine.

SONAR — SOund Navigation And Ranging · cut from NCERT, in your IGCSE

A transmitter on the ship sends pulses of ultrasound down; a detector picks up the echo from the seabed or an underwater object and converts it to an electrical signal. From the speed of sound in water and the echo time: 2d = v × t. Uses: finding the depth of the sea and locating underwater hills, valleys, submarines, icebergs and sunken ships. Bats find their prey the same way: they emit ultrasonic squeaks and locate an object from the reflection. This measuring-by-echo is called echo-ranging.

6The human ear — the one-line chain

The pre-2022 NCERT book described how the ear turns pressure variations into electrical signals; the section was cut from NCERT in the rationalisation and no cached paper asks it. One minute: learn the order of the parts, nothing more.

  1. Outer ear (pinna) collects sound; it passes down the auditory canal.
  2. At the end of the canal the eardrum (tympanic membrane), a thin membrane, is pushed in by each compression and out by each rarefaction — it vibrates.
  3. Three bones in the middle ear — hammer, anvil and stirrup — amplify the vibrations several times and pass them to the inner ear.
  4. In the inner ear the cochlea converts the pressure variations into electrical signals, which the auditory nerve carries to the brain, where they are interpreted as sound.

pinna → auditory canal → eardrum → hammer → anvil → stirrup → cochlea → auditory nerve → brain

8Five worked questions — at TALLENTEX pitch

Try each before opening the working and give yourself 90 seconds — the real paper's average. The elimination reasoning is written out because on the day, ruling two options out is worth almost as much as solving.

1C-to-R, then v = fλ, then count · ~60 seconds

In a sound wave travelling through air at 340 m/s, the distance between a compression and the nearest rarefaction is 0.5 m. How many compressions pass a fixed point in 0.25 s?

142.5
285
3170
41360
Working

Step 1 — the wavelength. C to the nearest R is half a wave, so λ = 2 × 0.5 = 1.0 m.

Step 2 — the frequency. f = v/λ = 340/1.0 = 340 Hz: 340 compressions pass each second.

Step 3 — in 0.25 s. 340 × 0.25 = 85. Option (2).

Where each wrong option comes from. (3) 170 — 0.5 m taken as the whole wavelength: f = 680 Hz, × 0.25. (1) 42.5 — C to R taken as a quarter wave: λ = 2 m, f = 170 Hz, × 0.25. (4) 1360 — the right frequency divided by 0.25 instead of multiplied by it. Elimination. In a quarter of a second you must count fewer compressions than pass in a whole second — so the answer is below f, whatever f is. That kills (4) on sight; the C-to-R step decides the rest.
2Free fall, then the sound comes back up · ~60 seconds

A stone is dropped from rest into a well. The water surface is 45 m below the top. Taking g = 10 m/s² and the speed of sound in air as 340 m/s, the splash is heard at the top of the well after about

10.13 s
23.00 s
33.13 s
43.26 s
Working

Step 1 — the fall. h = ½ g t² → 45 = 5t² → t = 3 s.

Step 2 — the sound, one way only. The splash is made at the water and travels up 45 m: 45/340 ≈ 0.13 s.

Step 3 — 3 + 0.13 = 3.13 s. Option (3).

Where each wrong option comes from. (2) 3.00 s — the sound treated as instantaneous (the "light" habit). (4) 3.26 s — the sound's path doubled as if it were an echo: 90/340 ≈ 0.26 s. There is no round trip here — the stone carried the "down" leg. (1) 0.13 s — only the sound's time, the fall forgotten. Elimination. The answer must be a little more than the fall time: (1) and (2) go at once. Then ask "who travels down?" — the stone, not the sound — and (4) goes too.
3The five-statement judgement · ~90 seconds

Consider the following statements.

  1. When a sound passes from air into water, its frequency stays the same and its wavelength increases.
  2. Two sounds of the same loudness and the same pitch from a sitar and a flute can be told apart because they differ in quality.
  3. Doubling the frequency of a sound in air doubles its speed.
  4. An echo can be heard from a wall 10 m away if the speed of sound is 344 m/s.
  5. Ultrasound is used to detect cracks in metal blocks and to break stones formed in the kidney.

Which of the statements are correct?

1I and V only
2I, II and V only
3II, III and V only
4I, II, IV and V only
Working

I — true. f is fixed by the source; v rises in water, so λ = v/f rises.

II — true. Same pitch and loudness, different waveform: that is quality (timbre).

III — false. The speed is set by the medium. Doubling f in the same air halves λ; v = fλ is unchanged.

IV — false. A distinct echo needs the wall at least 344 × 0.1 / 2 = 17.2 m away. At 10 m the echo returns after 20/344 ≈ 0.058 s — inside the 0.1 s the ear still holds the original, so it merges with it.

V — true. Both are on NCERT's list of ultrasound uses.

True: I, II, V — option (2).

Where each wrong option comes from. (1) — II rejected: "same pitch, same loudness, so they must sound the same" — quality forgotten. (3) — I rejected (wavelength kept, frequency changed) and III believed (speed ∝ frequency, a v = fλ misreading). (4) — everything right except IV (the halving in 17.2 m forgotten, or 10 m simply "felt far enough"). Elimination. III removes (3); IV removes (4). That still leaves (1) against (2), and only statement II separates them — so you must know what quality is. Two quick kills get you to a coin-toss, not to the mark.
4SONAR — wavelength and depth together · ~60 seconds

A ship's SONAR sends ultrasonic pulses of frequency 40 kHz straight down. The echo from the seabed is received 1.2 s after a pulse is sent. The speed of sound in sea water is 1500 m/s. The wavelength of the pulse in the water and the depth of the sea are

10.85 cm and 900 m
23.75 cm and 900 m
33.75 cm and 1800 m
437.5 cm and 900 m
Working

Step 1 — wavelength. λ = v/f = 1500/40 000 = 0.0375 m = 3.75 cm.

Step 2 — depth. Round trip: 2d = 1500 × 1.2 = 1800 m → d = 900 m. Option (2).

Where each wrong option comes from. (3) — the echo time not halved. (1) — the speed of sound in air (340 m/s) used for the wavelength: 340/40 000 = 0.85 cm. The pulse is in the water. (4) — 40 kHz read as 4000 Hz: 1500/4000 = 0.375 m. Elimination. The depth half splits the options 3–1: anyone who halves has (1), (2) or (4). Then 1500/40 000 — count the zeros carefully; it is the unit slip, not the physics, that loses this mark.
5Two cliffs, then the echo off both · ~90 seconds · the third echo is reasoning, not an NCERT rule

A man standing between two parallel cliffs fires a gun. He hears the first echo 2 s after the shot and the second echo 3 s after the shot. After how long does he hear the third echo, and how far apart are the cliffs? (Speed of sound = 340 m/s.)

11 s and 850 m
24 s and 850 m
35 s and 850 m
45 s and 1700 m
Working

Step 1 — distances. Nearer cliff: 340 × 2/2 = 340 m. Farther cliff: 340 × 3/2 = 510 m. He stands between them, so they are 340 + 510 = 850 m apart.

Step 2 — the third echo. The first echo does not stop at the man: it passes him, reaches the far cliff and comes back. Its extra journey is exactly the far cliff's round trip, 3 s, so it arrives at 2 + 3 = 5 s. (The sound that hit the far cliff first and then the near one takes the same path in reverse, so it arrives at 5 s too.) Option (3).

Where each wrong option comes from. (2) — the third echo taken as the near cliff's echo "again", 2 × 2 s. A second bounce off the same cliff is impossible without the far cliff in between. (1) — the third echo taken as the gap between the first two, 3 − 2 = 1 s: a time that has already gone by before the second echo arrived. (4) — the timing right, the distances not halved: 680 + 1020. Elimination. Distance first — it is the certain half. v(t₁ + t₂)/2 = 340 × 5/2 = 850 m removes (4). Every echo comes after the ones before it, so the third is later than 3 s — (1) goes. Then ask what the third echo is: a sound that has touched both cliffs. Its path is one near-cliff round trip plus one far-cliff round trip, 2 + 3 = 5 s; (2)'s 4 s would need two bounces off the same cliff in a row, which cannot happen.
