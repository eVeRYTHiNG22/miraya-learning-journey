Nuclear Physics

Inside the atom is a tiny, dense, charged nucleus — and some nuclei are unstable. This whole topic is the story of why they break apart, what shoots out, and how we count it. Tap the panels — they move.

01 / 11 · 5.1.1 The Atom

The atom — almost entirely empty space

An atom is a positively charged nucleus at the centre, with negatively charged electrons in orbit around it. The nucleus is unbelievably small compared with the whole atom — if the atom were a cricket stadium, the nucleus would be a grain of rice on the centre spot. Everything else is empty space the electrons sweep through.

⚛️ The core picture
Tiny dense centre, mostly empty outside. Almost all the mass sits in the nucleus; almost all the volume is the electron cloud. Hold that image — every later idea hangs off it.

Ions — atoms that have gained or lost electrons

A neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons. Knock an electron off and the atom is left with more protons than electrons → a positive ion. Add an electron and it becomes a negative ion.

Lose electron(s) → positive ion
Fewer negatives than positives, so the overall charge is +. E.g. a sodium atom loses one electron to become Na⁺.
Gain electron(s) → negative ion
More negatives than positives, so the overall charge is −. E.g. a chlorine atom gains one electron to become Cl⁻.
Almost all of the mass and all of the positive charge sit in the nucleus; almost all of the volume is the empty space the electrons orbit through (nowhere near to scale — a real nucleus is about 100 000 times smaller than the atom). A neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons, so losing an electron leaves the positives in front and gives a positive ion, while gaining one gives a negative ion.

How we know: the α-particle scattering experiment (Extended)

Geiger and Marsden (directed by Rutherford) fired fast alpha particles at an extremely thin sheet of gold foil and watched where they went.

ObservationWhat it proves about the nucleus
Most α-particles passed straight through undeflectedThe atom is mostly empty space
A few were deflected through large anglesThe nucleus is positively charged (it repels the positive α)
A very few bounced almost straight backThe nucleus is very small and holds most of the atom's mass
🎯 Why "bouncing back" is the killer clue
Firing a cannonball at tissue paper and having it ricochet back would stun you — it can only happen if something tiny, hard and massive is hidden in there. Rutherford said exactly that. A spread-out "plum-pudding" of charge could never throw a fast α straight back; only a concentrated nucleus can.
Three observations, three conclusions — learn them as pairs, because the exam gives you one and asks for the other. Nothing in the "plum-pudding" picture of spread-out charge could turn a fast α right round; only a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus can.
02 / 11 · 5.1.2 The Nucleus

Inside the nucleus — protons & neutrons

The nucleus is a bundle of two particles, together called nucleons:

ParticleRelative chargeRelative massWhere
Proton+11in the nucleus
Neutron01in the nucleus
Electron−1≈ 1/1840 (negligible)orbiting outside
Proton number, Z (atomic number) — the number of protons in the nucleus. It alone decides which element the atom is. relative charge on nucleus = +Z.
Nucleon number, A (mass number) — the total number of protons + neutrons. relative mass of nucleus ≈ A, and the number of neutrons is N = A − Z.

Nuclide notation

Every nuclide is written with both numbers stacked before its chemical symbol:

⚠️ Read the notation
 AZX  — top = nucleon number A, bottom = proton number Z. Example: 2311Na has 11 protons, 23 nucleons, so 23 − 11 = 12 neutrons.

Isotopes

Isotopes — atoms of the same element (same proton number Z) but with different numbers of neutrons (different nucleon number A). An element can have several isotopes.
🧪 Same element, different weight
Carbon is always Z = 6. But ¹²₆C, ¹³₆C and ¹⁴₆C have 6, 7 and 8 neutrons. They behave identically in chemistry (the electrons decide that) but differ in mass and in nuclear stability — ¹⁴₆C is radioactive and is what archaeologists use for carbon-dating.
The top number is always the bigger one: A counts all the nucleons, Z counts only the protons, so N = A − Z. Z alone decides the element. Isotopes are atoms of the same element (same Z) with different numbers of neutrons (different A) — chemically identical, because chemistry is decided by the electrons, but different in mass and in nuclear stability.
⚠️ Don't confuse the numbers
Change Z and you change the element. Change only the neutron count (so A changes but Z stays) and you get an isotope of the same element. The element's identity is fixed by protons, never by neutrons.
03 / 11 · 5.1.2 Fission & Fusion (Extended)

Splitting and joining nuclei

Nuclear fission — splitting
A large, heavy nucleus splits into two smaller nuclei (plus a few neutrons). Used in nuclear power stations. Releases energy.
Nuclear fusion — joining
Two small, light nuclei join to form a larger nucleus. Powers the Sun and stars (hydrogen → helium). Releases energy.
Fission (example, qualitative) — a uranium-235 nucleus absorbs a neutron and splits: ²³⁵₉₂U + ¹₀n → ¹⁴¹₅₆Ba + ⁹²₃₆Kr + 3 ¹₀n. The spare neutrons can split more nuclei — a chain reaction.
Fusion (example, qualitative) — light hydrogen nuclei fuse into helium: ²₁H + ³₁H → ⁴₂He + ¹₀n. This needs enormous temperatures and pressures — found in the core of stars.
Fission splits one heavy nucleus into two lighter ones plus a few spare neutrons — those neutrons can split further nuclei, which is the chain reaction a reactor controls. Fusion joins two light nuclei into a heavier one. Both release energy, and in both the nucleon numbers (top) and the proton numbers (bottom) must balance — that is how a missing particle is found. The uranium and fission-product nuclei are drawn as plain circles because their nucleons are far too many to show; only the small nuclei have their protons and neutrons drawn one for one. You are never asked for numerical mass or energy values here, only the equation and a qualitative description.
✅ What the syllabus actually asks
You describe fission/fusion as the splitting or joining of nuclei, give the nuclide equation, and say qualitatively that mass changes into energy — but you are not asked for any numerical mass or energy values here. Both processes release energy.
04 / 11 · 5.2.1 Detecting Radioactivity

Background radiation & count rate

Radioactivity is everywhere at a low level, all the time — this is background radiation. The main sources the syllabus names:

Radon gas (in the air)
Rocks & buildings
Food & drink
Cosmic rays (from space)

We measure ionising radiation with a detector connected to a counter (e.g. a Geiger–Müller tube and counter). The reading is a count rate, in counts / s or counts / minute.

Ionising radiation enters through the thin mica window, ionises the gas in the tube and gives a pulse the counter records; counts ÷ time is the count rate. The counter never falls silent, because background radiation from the four sources on the right is always arriving — so an Extended calculation makes you subtract the background first to get the corrected count rate from the source alone.
Corrected count rate (Extended) — because the background is always being counted too, you must subtract it: corrected = measured − background.
✅ Worked example
A detector near a source reads 510 counts/min. With the source removed, the background reads 30 counts/min. The true count rate from the source is 510 − 30 = 480 counts/min. Always correct for background before any half-life calculation that asks for it.
05 / 11 · 5.2.2 The Three Emissions

Alpha, beta & gamma — three very different things

Emission from a nucleus is spontaneous (you can't make it happen or stop it) and random in direction. Three kinds come out, and they differ in nature, ionising power and how far they penetrate. Pick one below and watch what stops it:

α — alpha
β — beta
γ — gamma
Alpha (α)
An alpha particle is a helium nucleus — 2 protons + 2 neutrons. It is heavy and highly charged, so it ionises strongly but is stopped by just a sheet of paper (or a few cm of air).
PropertyAlpha (α)Beta (β⁻)Gamma (γ)
NatureHelium nucleus ⁴₂He (2p + 2n)Fast electron ⁰₋₁eHigh-frequency EM wave ⁰₀γ
Relative charge+2−10
Ionising effectStrong (most)ModerateWeak (least)
PenetrationLeast — stopped by paper / a few cm of airMedium — stopped by a few mm of aluminiumMost — only greatly reduced by thick lead / concrete
The order never changes: α is stopped by a sheet of paper or a few cm of air, β gets through paper but is stopped by a few mm of aluminium, and γ gets through both and is only greatly reduced — never completely stopped — by several cm of lead or thick concrete. It is the exact reverse of the ionising order, because a heavily ionising particle spends its energy fast.
🔄 The inverse rule that ties it together
Most ionising = least penetrating, and vice-versa. Alpha is big and double-charged, so it crashes into atoms constantly — ionising hard but using up its energy fast (low penetration). Gamma has no charge and no mass, barely interacts, so it ionises weakly but sails a long way.

Deflection in fields (Extended)

Because α and β are charged, they curve in electric and magnetic fields; γ (no charge) goes straight.

  • α (+2) deflects slightly — it is heavy, so hard to push.
  • β (−1) deflects a lot, the opposite way to α — it is very light, and has opposite charge.
  • γ (0) is not deflected at all.
Charge decides the direction, mass decides the amount. Between the plates the positive α is pulled towards the negative plate and the negative β towards the positive plate. In the magnetic field — drawn into the page, with the beam travelling left to right — the same opposite-charge rule pushes α up and β down. In both fields β bends far more than α, because a β-particle is an electron roughly 7000× lighter than an α-particle. γ has no charge, so no field touches it.
✅ Explaining ionising effect (Extended)
Account for ionising power using kinetic energy and electric charge: α has the most charge (+2) and is slow and massive, so it interacts most strongly and ionises the most; β has less charge; γ has no charge, so it ionises the least.
06 / 11 · 5.2.3 Radioactive Decay

Decay — how the nucleus changes

Radioactive decay is a change in an unstable nucleus that emits α, β and/or γ. It is spontaneous and random — you cannot predict when any one nucleus will decay, and nothing (heat, pressure, chemistry) speeds it up or slows it down.

🍿 Why "random" still gives a smooth curve
One popcorn kernel pops at an unpredictable moment — random. But a whole bag pops in a smooth, predictable pattern over time. Same here: individual decays are random, yet a huge sample decays in a beautifully regular way. That regularity is the half-life.

What each decay does to the nucleus (Extended)

Isotopes are radioactive when the nucleus has too many neutrons and/or is simply too heavy. Decay nudges the nucleus toward stability:

DecayNucleon no. AProton no. ZEmitted
Alpha (α)− 4− 2⁴₂α (helium nucleus)
Beta (β⁻)unchanged+ 1⁰₋₁β (electron)
Gamma (γ)unchangedunchangedγ (energy only)
The beta decay trick — a neutron turns into a proton plus the electron that is fired out: neutron → proton + electron. That is why Z goes up by 1 while A stays the same. After α or β decay the nucleus is a different element.
⚠️ Say what each decay does for the nucleus
Every decay leaves the nucleus more stable than it was, and the syllabus wants you to say how:
  • β-emission converts a neutron into a proton, so it reduces the number of excess neutrons — this is the decay of a nucleus that has too many neutrons.
  • α-emission throws out two protons and two neutrons at once, so it makes a too-heavy nucleus smaller and lighter.
  • γ-emission changes no particles at all — the nucleus simply loses excess energy, which is why γ usually follows an α or β decay that has left the nucleus in an excited state.

Decay equations (Extended)

✅ Balancing rule — top and bottom must each total

Alpha:  ²³⁸₉₂U → ²³⁴₉₀Th + ⁴₂α   (238 = 234 + 4; 92 = 90 + 2)

Beta:  ¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + ⁰₋₁β   (14 = 14 + 0; 6 = 7 + (−1))

Check both the top row (nucleon numbers) and bottom row (proton numbers) balance on each side — that is how you find the unknown product.
Write the equation, then check the two rows separately: the top row (nucleon numbers) must total the same on each side, and so must the bottom row (proton numbers). That is how a missing product is found. Note that a β-particle is written 0−1β, so its −1 on the bottom row is what pushes the daughter's proton number up by one. After α- or β-decay the nucleus is a different element; after γ-emission it is the same element with less energy.
07 / 11 · 5.2.4 Half-life

Half-life — the clock of the nucleus

Half-life — the time taken for half the (undecayed) nuclei of an isotope in a sample to decay. Equivalently, the time for the count rate (activity) to fall to half its value.

Each half-life that passes, whatever is left halves again: 100% → 50% → 25% → 12.5% … It never quite reaches zero. Drag the slider and watch the sample decay:

After 0 half-lives the fraction left is (½)0 = 1. To find a half-life from data, count how many times the activity has halved, then divide the total time by that number.
Read a half-life off a curve like this: go half way down the activity axis, across to the curve, then straight down to the time axis. Here 800 → 400 takes 6 h, and the very next halving 400 → 200 takes the same 6 h — the half-life is constant, whichever point you start from. Equivalently, count the halvings and divide the total time by that number. The curve gets closer and closer to the time axis but never reaches zero.
✅ Worked example — find the half-life
A source's corrected count rate falls from 800 → 400 → 200 → 100 counts/s over 18 hours. That is 3 halvings (800→400→200→100). So one half-life = 18 ÷ 3 = 6 hours.
⚠️ Two classic slips
(1) Half-life is the time to halve again from wherever you are — it is constant, not "time to reach zero". (2) For a half-life from real data, subtract the background first (the Extended version uses data from which background has not already been removed).
A real detector counts the background too, so the measured curve levels off at the background instead of falling to zero. Subtract first: the source alone starts at 800 − 40 = 760, half of that is 380, and 380 counts/s from the source reads as 380 + 40 = 420 on the meter. The half-life is the time to reach 420, not 400 — halving the measured number without correcting it always gives a half-life that is too long.

Choosing the right isotope for a job (Extended)

UseRadiation + half-life chosen because…
Smoke alarmsα source — its radiation is stopped by smoke (so the alarm "sees" smoke); long half-life so it lasts for years
Irradiating food to kill bacteriaγ — penetrates the packaging and kills microbes without making the food radioactive
Sterilising equipmentγ — penetrates sealed packaging to kill all microbes
Measuring/controlling thickness (paper, foil)β — partly absorbed by a thin sheet, so the amount getting through reveals the thickness (a really thick sheet needs γ)
Diagnosis & treatment of cancerγ — penetrates the body to image or to destroy tumour cells
Both the type of radiation and the half-life are chosen for the job. In a smoke alarm the α-particles ionise the air between two plates so a small current flows; smoke absorbs the α, the current falls and the alarm sounds — and a long half-life means the source never needs replacing. In a thickness gauge for paper or thin metal foil only β is sensitive to the thickness — α is stopped by the sheet altogether and γ passes almost unchanged (a really thick sheet is gauged with γ instead). Again a long half-life keeps the reading steady.
08 / 11 · 5.2.5 Safety Precautions

Why radiation is dangerous — and how to stay safe

Effects on living things — ionising radiation can cause cell death, mutations (damaged DNA), and cancer. The more dose received, the greater the risk.

The three ways to reduce exposure (Extended)

⏱️ Time
Spend less time near a source — total dose builds up with exposure time.
📏 Distance
Stay further away — intensity falls off quickly with distance. Use tongs/handling tools, never bare hands.
🧱 Shielding
Put absorbing material between you and the source — lead, thick concrete, or lead-lined aprons absorb the radiation before it reaches you.
⚠️ Handling, using & storing safely
Keep sources in lead-lined containers, handle with long tongs (never bare hands), point them away from people, keep exposure brief, and store them locked in a clearly-labelled shielded box. Time + distance + shielding, every time.

Q1In the α-scattering experiment, state the observation that shows each of these: (a) the atom is mostly empty space, (b) the nucleus is positively charged, (c) the nucleus is very small and holds most of the mass. [3]
Tap to reveal
(a) Most α-particles pass straight through the foil undeflected. (b) A few are deflected through large angles — they are repelled by a concentrated positive charge. (c) A very few are bounced almost straight back, which needs something tiny, dense and massive to hit.
Q2A nuclide is written ²²²₈₆Rn. State the number of protons, neutrons and electrons in one neutral atom of it. [3]
Tap to reveal
Protons = Z = 86. Neutrons = A − Z = 222 − 86 = 136. Electrons in a neutral atom = number of protons = 86.
Q3Lead-210 decays by beta emission: ²¹⁰₈₂Pb → ? + ⁰₋₁β. Give the nucleon number and the proton number of the daughter nucleus, and say whether it is the same element as lead. [3]
Tap to reveal
Top row: 210 = A + 0, so A = 210 (unchanged). Bottom row: 82 = Z + (−1), so Z = 83. It is a different element (bismuth) — β-decay always changes the element because Z changes.
Q4Next to a source a detector reads 620 counts/min. With the source taken away it reads 20 counts/min. The source's half-life is 3 hours. What will the detector read 6 hours later? [3]
Tap to reveal
Corrected count rate now = 620 − 20 = 600 counts/min. 6 h is 2 half-lives, so the source's rate falls to 600 × ¼ = 150 counts/min. The detector still counts the background, so it will read 150 + 20 = 170 counts/min.
Q5Explain, in terms of electric charge and kinetic energy, why α-particles are the most ionising but the least penetrating. [3]
Tap to reveal
An α-particle has the largest charge (+2) and is heavy and comparatively slow, so it pulls electrons off the atoms it passes very strongly — it ionises the most. Every ionisation takes some of its kinetic energy, so it loses its energy over a very short distance and is stopped by a sheet of paper or a few cm of air.
Q6A beam containing α, β and γ travels from left to right into a magnetic field directed into the page. Describe the deflection of each. [3]
Tap to reveal
The α (charge +2) is deflected towards the top of the page, but only slightly because it is heavy. The β (charge −1) is deflected the opposite way, towards the bottom, and by a much larger amount because an electron is thousands of times lighter. γ has no charge, so it is not deflected at all.
Q7A student measuring 800 counts/s says "one half-life has passed when the meter reads 400 counts/s." The background is 40 counts/s. Explain why the student is wrong, and state the reading to look for instead. [3]
Tap to reveal
It is the corrected count rate that halves, not the meter reading, because the meter is also counting the background. Corrected now = 800 − 40 = 760, so after one half-life the source gives 380 counts/s and the meter will read 380 + 40 = 420 counts/s. Waiting for 400 would give a half-life that is too long.
Q8Explain why a smoke alarm uses an α source with a long half-life, and why a gauge measuring the thickness of paper or thin metal foil uses β rather than α or γ. [4]
Tap to reveal
Smoke alarm: α ionises the air strongly, so a small current flows between the plates; smoke absorbs the α easily, the current falls and the alarm sounds. A long half-life means the activity barely changes, so the alarm keeps working for years without the source being replaced. Thickness gauge: the thin sheet absorbs some of the β, so the count rate getting through changes as the thickness changes. α would be stopped completely (no reading at all) and γ would pass through almost unchanged (no measurable difference). Only a very thick sheet would need γ.
