Particles, Heat & Temperature

Thermal physics, built around one mental model: everything is tiny particles, and heat is their hidden motion. Get that, and expansion, heat capacity, latent heat and every transfer method fall out of it. Tap the panels — the particles move.

01 / 12 · 2.1.1–2.1.2 Kinetic Particle Model

The one idea behind everything: matter is particles

Every solid, liquid and gas is made of enormous numbers of tiny particles in constant motion. You can't see them, but their behaviour explains the entire topic. The difference between ice, water and steam is not the particles — they're the same H₂O — it's how much energy those particles have and how freely they can move.

💡 The whole topic in one sentence
Heat is just particle movement you can't see. Add energy → particles move faster and push further apart. That single idea explains why things expand when heated, why gases push on their containers, and why a kettle stays at 100 °C while it boils. Hold onto it.

Pick a state below. Watch how the same particles arrange and move differently — that's the only thing that changes:

Liquid

Particles are packed in a fixed, regular pattern, held by strong forces. They cannot move past each other — they only vibrate about fixed positions. Result: a solid has a fixed shape and a fixed volume, and is almost impossible to compress.

🕺 The dance-floor analogy
A solid is people standing in a tight grid, swaying on the spot — no one moves seats. A liquid is a packed dance floor: everyone's touching, but you can squeeze past to the bar. A gas is a few people sprinting around an empty hall, bouncing off the walls. Same people, three energies.
⚠️ Common wrong intuition
"Gas particles repel each other, that's why they spread out." No — in the IGCSE model, gas particles barely interact at all; they spread out simply because nothing holds them back and they're moving fast in random directions. Forces between gas particles are treated as negligible.

Brownian motion — the evidence that particles are really there

All of this would be a nice story if we couldn't see any sign of it. We can. Shine a bright light into a small glass cell of smoke and look down a microscope: the smoke specks show up as tiny points of light, and they never sit still. They jiggle about in a random, jerky, zig-zag path. That is Brownian motion, and it is the direct experimental evidence for the kinetic particle model.

Fig · The smoke-cell experiment — light scattered off smoke particles makes them visible, and their random zig-zag path is caused by uneven bombardment from the much smaller, much faster air molecules you cannot see.
⚠️ The exact wording that scores
The smoke particles are hit unevenly and randomly by fast-moving air molecules. Because the molecules are so small and so numerous, at any instant slightly more of them strike one side than the other, so the smoke particle is knocked in a random direction — and a moment later in a different one. Say random and say collisions with molecules, or the mark doesn't come.
⚠️ Two words the examiner separates
A microscopic particle is the thing you can see (the smoke speck, a pollen grain). The things doing the hitting are molecules (or atoms) of the surrounding gas or liquid — far smaller and far faster. Never write "the smoke particles collide with each other"; write "the light, fast-moving air molecules collide with the smoke particles". The molecules are light but fast, so each impact gives a visible kick to the much heavier speck.
02 / 12 · 2.1.2 · 2.2.2 Temperature & Internal Energy

What temperature actually is

Temperature is not "amount of heat". Temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles. Hotter means the particles are jiggling, sliding or flying around faster, on average.

Internal energy — the total energy of all the particles in an object: the kinetic energy of their motion plus the potential energy stored in the forces (bonds) between them. Heating an object increases its internal energy.
🔥 Temperature vs. energy — the bathtub trick
A sparkler at 1000 °C and a bathtub of warm water at 40 °C: the sparkler is far hotter (its particles are faster), but the bath holds far more thermal energy overall, because it has vastly more particles. High temperature ≠ lots of energy. This is exactly why a tiny spark doesn't burn you but a bath can warm a room.
⚠️ Mark-winning one-liner
"Temperature measures the average kinetic energy of the particles." When you heat something and its temperature rises, you are saying its particles are moving faster on average.
03 / 12 · 2.1.3 Gases & the Absolute Scale

Gas pressure, and why physicists count from −273 °C

A gas pushes outward on its container. Why? Its particles are constantly colliding with the walls, and every collision is a tiny push. Billions per second add up to a steady pressure.

🏓 Pressure = a hail of tiny impacts
Picture popcorn popping inside a sealed pan, hammering the lid from below. More pops per second, or harder pops, means more force on the lid. Gas pressure is exactly that: the combined drumming of particles on the walls. Anything that makes them hit more often or harder raises the pressure.

The two ways to change gas pressure

Heat it (volume fixed)
More energy → faster particles → they hit the walls harder and more often → pressure rises. So pressure increases with temperature: p ∝ T (T in kelvin).
Squeeze it (temperature fixed)
Smaller volume → particles reach the walls sooner → more collisions per second → pressure rises. So pV = constant (Boyle's law).
Boyle's Law (Extended) — at constant temperature, pV = constant, so p₁V₁ = p₂V₂. Halve the volume and you double the pressure. The graph of p against V is a curve; p against 1/V is a straight line.

You are expected to know both graph shapes and what each proves:

Fig · Boyle's law for a fixed mass of gas at constant temperature. The p–V curve tells you pressure and volume are inversely related; plotting p against 1/V turns it into a straight line through the origin, which is how you prove the relationship from data.
✅ Working a Boyle's law question
A sealed syringe holds 60 cm³ of air at 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa. The plunger is pushed in until the volume is 25 cm³ at the same temperature. Find the new pressure.
p₁V₁ = p₂V₂ → p₂ = p₁V₁ / V₂ = (1.0 × 10⁵ × 60) / 25 = 2.4 × 10⁵ Pa.
Because it's a ratio, the volumes can stay in cm³ — they cancel. But the temperature must be unchanged for this to be valid, and you should say so.

The Kelvin (absolute) temperature scale

Cool a gas and its particles slow down. Keep cooling and there must be a point where motion is at its absolute minimum. That point is −273 °C = absolute zero = 0 K. You cannot get colder, because particles can't have less than zero motion.

Kelvin conversion — T (in K) = θ (in °C) + 273. A change of 1 °C equals a change of 1 K — the scale is just shifted, not stretched. Always use kelvin in gas-law calculations.
⚠️ The trap that costs marks
In any pressure–temperature gas calculation, temperature must be in kelvin. Doubling from 20 °C to 40 °C does not double the pressure — in kelvin that's 293 K → 313 K, a rise of only ~7%. Convert first, every time.
04 / 12 · 2.2.1 Thermal Expansion

Why things grow when you heat them

Heat almost anything and it expands; cool it and it contracts. The particle model explains it instantly: hotter particles vibrate (or move) more vigorously, so on average they push each other slightly further apart — the object takes up more space.

↔️ Same particles, bigger gaps
Expansion is not the particles themselves getting bigger. It's the average spacing between them increasing because they're swinging through a wider arc. The number of particles never changes — only the room they need.

Gases expand most, liquids less, solids least — because the freer the particles, the more the extra energy spreads them out:

Gases — most
Particles already free and fast; a little heat sends them much further apart.
Liquids — medium
Used in thermometers: the liquid rises up a thin tube as it expands.
Solids — least
Strong forces resist; expansion is small but powerful enough to matter.
✅ Real-world applications to quote
Expansion gaps in bridges and railway tracks stop them buckling in summer. Overhead power cables are hung with slack so they don't snap when they contract in winter. Liquid-in-glass thermometers work because liquids expand more than the glass around them.
⚠️ The famous exception
Water is unusual: between 0 °C and 4 °C it expands as it cools, and ice is less dense than liquid water — which is why ice floats and pipes burst in winter. (You won't be asked to explain this in detail, but don't claim "everything contracts when cooled".)
05 / 12 · 2.2.2 Specific Heat Capacity

Specific heat capacity — why some things heat up slowly

Pour the same energy into a kilogram of water and a kilogram of iron, and the iron gets far hotter. Water needs a lot of energy to change its temperature. That "stubbornness" is measured by the specific heat capacity.

Specific heat capacity, c — the energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 °C: E = m·c·Δθ, units J/(kg·°C). Water's is huge: 4200 J/(kg·°C).

✅ Why this matters in the real world
Water's enormous c is why it's used in central-heating radiators and car coolant (it carries lots of energy), and why coastal towns like Chennai and Mumbai have milder weather than inland ones such as Nagpur — the sea soaks up summer heat and releases it slowly in winter.

Measuring c — the experiment you must be able to describe

The method is the same for a solid and a liquid, and both versions are examinable. You supply a measured amount of electrical energy and see how much the temperature rises:

Fig · Measuring specific heat capacity. Both set-ups are the same idea: a known mass, a heater of known power, a timer and a thermometer. The lagging matters — it is the main way energy escapes and spoils the result.
✅ The method, in the order the mark scheme wants it
1 Find the mass m of the block (or liquid) on a balance. 2 Put the heater in one hole and the thermometer in the other, with a drop of oil in the thermometer hole for good thermal contact; lag the block. 3 Record the starting temperature θ₁. 4 Switch on the heater and the timer together for a measured time t; use the power P marked on the heater (if instead you are given the current and voltage, add an ammeter in series and a voltmeter across the heater and use P = VI). 5 Switch off, wait for the temperature to peak, and record θ₂. 6 Energy supplied ΔE = P × t, temperature rise Δθ = θ₂ − θ₁, so c = ΔE / (m Δθ). For the liquid, stir continuously so the whole liquid is at the same temperature when you read it.
⚠️ Why your answer always comes out too big
Some of the energy escapes to the surroundings instead of heating the block, so the measured Δθ is smaller than it should be — and since c = ΔE/(mΔθ), a too-small Δθ makes the calculated c too large. Reduce it by lagging the block, starting a little below room temperature and finishing a little above, and reading the highest temperature reached after switching off (energy is still spreading through the block). This is the standard 2–3 mark "suggest an improvement" answer.
⚠️ Worked example — the numbers examiners like
A 2.0 kg aluminium block is heated by a 50 W heater for 4.0 minutes and its temperature rises from 19.0 °C to 25.0 °C.
ΔE = P × t = 50 × (4.0 × 60) = 12 000 J · Δθ = 25.0 − 19.0 = 6.0 °C
c = ΔE / (m Δθ) = 12 000 / (2.0 × 6.0) = 1000 J/(kg·°C)
The accepted value for aluminium is 900 J/(kg·°C), so the experiment has come out about 11% high — exactly what energy loss to the surroundings does, as the box above explains. Note the two traps: convert minutes to seconds, and use the temperature rise, never the final temperature.
06 / 12 · 2.2.3 Melting, Boiling & Evaporation

Melting, boiling, and the energy that hides

⚠️ Two numbers you are expected to know
At standard atmospheric pressure, water melts at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C. These are the melting point and the boiling point of water, and they are the two temperatures the heating curve below levels off at. Learn them as figures, not as "cold" and "hot".

Keep heating a block of ice and something strange happens: while it melts, the thermometer stops rising even though you're still adding energy. Where does the energy go? Into breaking the bonds between particles, not speeding them up. That hidden energy is latent heat.

Press play and follow the tracer up the heating curve. Watch the two flat plateaus — that's latent heat at work:

Temperature vs. energy supplied (heating ice → water → steam)
Solid (ice) warming up
Energy raises the particles' kinetic energy, so the temperature climbs steadily. Press play to begin the journey from ice to steam.
🧊 Why the temperature pauses while it melts
During a change of state, every joule you add goes into pulling particles apart against their bonds, not into making them move faster. Average kinetic energy stays the same → temperature stays the same. The thermometer only climbs again once the change is complete.
Specific latent heat, L (background — removed from 0625 for 2026–28) — the energy needed to change the state of 1 kg without any temperature change: E = m·L. You will not be asked to calculate with this in your exam, but you will meet it in pre-2026 past papers, and the idea behind it — energy in, no temperature rise — is examinable.

Run it backwards: condensation and solidification

Cool something and every step reverses. The exam asks for these in particle terms, so learn the two sentences:

Condensation (gas → liquid)
The gas particles slow down and lose kinetic energy. When they are slow enough, the forces between them can hold them together, so they move close together and start to touch — a liquid. Energy is given out to the surroundings.
Solidification / freezing (liquid → solid)
The liquid particles slow further until they can no longer slide past one another. The forces lock them into fixed positions in a regular pattern, where they only vibrate — a solid. Energy is again given out.
⚠️ Name all four, in both directions
solid —melting→ liquid —boiling / evaporating→ gas, and back again gas —condensing→ liquid —solidifying (freezing)→ solid. Note the symmetry that earns marks: the changes going up (melting, boiling) take energy in; the changes coming back down (condensing, solidifying) give energy out — and, just like melting and boiling, they happen at a constant temperature.

Boiling vs. evaporation — not the same thing

Boiling
Happens at one fixed temperature (the boiling point), throughout the whole liquid, with bubbles. Energy supplied from outside.
Evaporation
Happens at any temperature, only at the surface, with no bubbles. The fastest surface particles escape.
💧 Why evaporation cools — the marathon effect
Only the fastest particles have enough energy to escape from the surface. When they leave, the ones left behind are the slower ones — so the average kinetic energy of what remains drops, and the liquid cools. It's like the fastest runners leaving a group: the average speed of those left falls.

And that is how it cools you: the sweat on your skin is now colder than you are, so thermal energy is conducted from your skin into the sweat to replace what the escaping particles carried away. Your skin loses internal energy, so your temperature falls. The same argument answers any "explain why the object cools" question — a wet cloth on a fever, water in an earthen matka seeping through and evaporating, spirit dabbed on the back of your hand.
✅ Faster evaporation — three levers
Evaporation speeds up with higher temperature, larger surface area, and more draught / wind (which sweeps escaped particles away). That's why wet washing dries fastest on a warm, breezy day spread out wide.
07 / 12 · 2.3.1 Thermal Energy Transfer — Conduction

Conduction — heat passed particle to particle

Hold a metal spoon in hot soup and the handle soon warms, even though you never dipped it. That's conduction: thermal energy passing along a material without the material itself flowing. It's the main way heat moves through solids.

🔗 Hand it down the line
Heat one end of a solid: those particles vibrate harder and knock into their neighbours, passing energy along like a bucket chain. In metals there's a turbo boost — free electrons zip through the structure carrying energy quickly, which is why metals are the best conductors by far.
Fig · Conduction in a metal rod — vibrating particles pass energy neighbour-to-neighbour (harder at the hot end), while free electrons drift right through the lattice, carrying energy fast. Both send thermal energy from the hot end to the cool end.
Good conductors
Metals (copper, aluminium) — thanks to free electrons. Used for saucepans, heat sinks, radiators.
Insulators (poor conductors)
Air, plastic, wood, wool — no free electrons to carry energy, and in gases the particles are too far apart to pass it on. Used for handles, clothing, lagging.

The experiment that ranks conductors

You are expected to be able to describe an experiment that shows which materials conduct well. The classic one uses rods of different materials, all heated at one end, each with a pin stuck on the far end with a blob of wax:

Fig · Comparing thermal conductors. Identical rods of different materials share one heat source, so the only thing that differs is the material — the rod that drops its pin first conducted energy along fastest.
✅ Describing it in the exam, and the fair-test marks
Method: take rods of copper, iron and glass that are the same length and same thickness; stick a pin to the far end of each with a blob of wax; put all their other ends into the same trough of hot water at the same time; record which pin falls first. Result: copper's pin falls first, then iron's; glass's may never fall — so copper is the best conductor and glass the worst.
The marks live in the controls: same rod dimensions, same size of wax blob, same starting temperature, all started together. Change any of those and the comparison proves nothing.
⚠️ Why liquids and gases conduct so badly
In a liquid or gas the particles are much further apart and are not locked into a lattice, so vibrations don't get handed on efficiently, and there are no free electrons to carry energy. That's why, if you heat the top of a test tube of water, it can boil there while a lump of ice held down at the bottom by a metal gauze stays unmelted — the heat simply cannot conduct down, and convection can't help because the hot water is already on top. (Note there's a middle group too: solids like glass, brick and concrete conduct better than wool or air, but far worse than metals.)
✅ Why trapped air is the secret
Most insulators work by trapping air (a poor conductor) in small pockets so it can't move: wool, foam, double glazing, fibreglass loft lagging. Liquids and gases are generally poor conductors because their particles are far apart.
08 / 12 · 2.3.2 Thermal Energy Transfer — Convection

Convection — hot fluid floats, cool fluid sinks

Convection moves heat through fluids (liquids and gases) by the fluid itself flowing. It cannot happen in solids — the particles can't travel.

Convection current — heat a fluid and it expands, becoming less dense, so it rises. Cooler, denser fluid sinks to take its place, gets heated in turn, and rises too. This circulating loop carries thermal energy through the whole fluid.
Fig · Convection current — coloured water heated at one corner rises (less dense), cools and spreads across the top, then sinks on the far side and returns to the flame, circulating heat through the whole beaker.
✅ Describing the experiment — the version they mark
In a liquid: fill a beaker with water and drop a single crystal of potassium manganate(VII) down a tube so it settles in one bottom corner. Heat gently under that corner. The purple colour rises above the flame, spreads across the top, sinks down the far side and returns along the base — tracing the convection current for you.
In a gas: hold a paper spiral on a pin above a candle and it spins, because the warm air rising past it is moving. (Or use a smoke box with two chimneys: smoke from a smouldering taper is drawn down the cool chimney and out of the one above the candle.)
The marks are for naming what you see and linking it to density: warm fluid expands → less dense → rises; cool fluid is denser → sinks.
🎈 It's really just floating
Hot fluid rises for the exact same reason a hot-air balloon rises: heating spreads the particles out, so the same mass takes up more volume → lower density → it floats up through the denser cool fluid around it. "Hot rises" is just "less dense floats" wearing a different hat.
✅ Everyday convection to quote
Radiators are placed low so warm air rises and circulates a whole room. Sea breezes: land heats faster than sea by day, warm air over land rises, cool air flows in off the sea. The freezer compartment sits at the top of a fridge so cold, dense air sinks down through the food below.
09 / 12 · 2.3.3 Thermal Energy Transfer — Radiation

Radiation — heat that needs nothing to travel through

The Sun's warmth reaches us across empty space, where there are no particles to conduct or convect. It arrives as infrared radiation — a type of electromagnetic wave. Every object emits it; the hotter the object, the more it pours out.

🌞 No particles required
Conduction and convection both need matter to pass energy along. Radiation doesn't — it's a wave, so it crosses a vacuum. That's the one thing that makes radiation special, and it's the favourite exam question: how does heat get from the Sun to Earth? Answer: radiation, because space is empty.
Emitting & absorbing — a surface that is matt (dull) black is the best emitter and the best absorber of infrared radiation. A shiny, white or silvery surface is the worst emitter and absorber — it reflects radiation instead.
Fig · Leslie cube — the same hot water behind every face, yet the matt-black surface emits far more infrared than the polished-silver one, so its detector reads higher. Surface finish, not temperature, makes the difference here.

What increases the radiation emitted?

Higher temperature
A hotter surface emits infrared at a greater rate, and the rate climbs steeply as the surface temperature rises.
Larger / darker surface
Bigger surface area and a dull-black colour both increase the rate of emission.
✅ Testing absorbers — the other experiment
The Leslie cube above compares emitters. To compare absorbers, take two identical metal plates, one painted matt black and one polished silver, and stand them the same distance either side of a radiant heater. Stick a coin to the back of each with a blob of wax. The coin on the matt-black plate falls first, because that surface absorbed infrared faster. (Same result with a thermometer on the back of each: the black plate's temperature rises faster.) The controls are what earn the marks — same plates, same distance, same heater, started together.

Energy in, energy out — the balance that fixes temperature

Every object is doing two things at once: emitting infrared and absorbing the infrared arriving from its surroundings. Which one wins decides what happens to its temperature:

in = out
The object absorbs energy at exactly the rate it emits it, so its temperature stays constant. That is what "constant temperature" actually means — not "nothing is happening".
in > out
It receives faster than it loses, so its internal energy rises and it gets hotter — like a black car left in the Delhi sun.
in < out
It loses faster than it receives, so it cools down — like a cup of chai left out overnight, or the desert at night.
🌍 The same balance sets the temperature of the Earth
The Earth absorbs radiation from the Sun and emits infrared back out into space. Averaged over a long time these two rates match, and the Earth sits at a steady temperature. Anything that changes the balance changes that temperature: more greenhouse gases (carbon dioxide, methane) absorb some of the outgoing infrared and re-emit part of it back down, so less escapes — energy out falls below energy in and the Earth warms until a new balance is reached. Changing how much sunlight is reflected (ice and clouds reflect; dark ocean and bare soil absorb) shifts the same balance the other way.
✅ Applications that test all three
Houses painted white in hot Indian summers reflect radiation and stay cooler inside. Shiny emergency "space" blankets (used in disaster relief) reflect body radiation back in. A vacuum flask beats all three transfers at once — see the next section.
⚠️ Don't mix the three up
Conduction = through solids, particle to particle. Convection = fluids flowing in currents. Radiation = waves, no medium needed. If a question mentions a vacuum or space, the answer can only be radiation.
10 / 12 · 2.3.4 Consequences of Thermal Energy Transfer

Putting the three together

In the exam the three transfers rarely arrive one at a time. Real objects are designed either to help heat move or to stop it, and the good answers name which transfer is doing what — and which one is being blocked.

The simple ones

A kitchen pan
The base is metal so energy conducts quickly from the flame into the food; it is often thick and dark so it also absorbs radiation well. Inside, the food or water is stirred by convection currents. The handle is plastic or wood — a poor conductor, so little energy reaches your hand.
Heating a room
A heater is placed low down. It warms the air beside it, which expands, becomes less dense and rises; cooler denser air sinks to take its place and is warmed in turn. That convection current circulates warm air through the whole room — which is why putting a heater on a high shelf warms only the ceiling.

The two that need more than one transfer (Extended)

A fire burning wood or coal
Radiation is what you feel across the room — infrared travels straight to you through the air without warming it much (air is a poor absorber). Convection carries hot gases and smoke up the chimney, and draws fresh cool air in at the bottom to keep it burning. Conduction matters least: it warms the fireplace bricks and grate, and air itself is such a poor conductor that almost nothing reaches you that way.
A car radiator
Hot water is pumped from the engine, so the water carries energy by forced convection (a pump, not just floating). Energy conducts from the water through the thin metal walls and fins — thin metal, large surface area. Then the fins, painted matt black, lose it to the air by radiation and to the air stream by convection. Radiators are put at the front of the car so moving air sweeps the warm air away continuously.

The vacuum flask — the classic "block all three" question

A vacuum (Thermos) flask keeps a drink hot for hours by attacking every route at once. Learn the labels; the question is nearly always "explain how each feature reduces energy transfer".

Fig · Vacuum flask, cut away. Vacuum kills conduction and convection through the walls, silvering reflects the radiation back, the stopper closes the convection route out of the top, and small supports leave almost no solid path for conduction.
⚠️ The transfer-spotting rule
Ask what is between the hot thing and the cold thing? A solid → conduction. A fluid that can flow → convection. Nothing (a vacuum), or a gap you can see across → radiation. Then say which one the design is trying to stop and how. That single question answers almost every "explain how this reduces energy transfer" part.

Q4Explain, in terms of particles, why the pressure of a fixed mass of gas rises when it is heated at constant volume. [3]
Tap to reveal

Q5A 0.45 kg copper block is heated by a 60 W heater for 3.0 minutes. Its temperature rises by 55 °C. Calculate the specific heat capacity obtained from this experiment, and state whether it is likely to be above or below the true value. [4]
Tap to reveal
ΔE = P × t = 60 × (3.0 × 60) = 10 800 J (1). c = ΔE / (m Δθ) = 10 800 / (0.45 × 55) (1) = 10 800 / 24.75 = 436 J/(kg·°C) (1). It is above the true value (385 J/(kg·°C) for copper), because some of the supplied energy is lost to the surroundings, so the measured Δθ is smaller than it should be — and dividing by a too-small Δθ gives a too-large c (1).

Tap to reveal

Cooling: only the most energetic particles have enough energy to escape from the surface (1), so the average kinetic energy of the particles left behind falls, and lower average kinetic energy means a lower temperature (1).

Tap to reveal

Q9An object is at a constant temperature. State what this tells you about the rates at which it receives and transfers energy, and describe what happens if the rate it receives energy then increases. [3]
Tap to reveal
At constant temperature it is transferring energy away at the same rate as it is receiving it (1) — the two are balanced, not zero. If the rate of receiving rises above the rate of transferring away (1), its internal energy increases and its temperature rises — until it emits fast enough for the rates to balance again at a new, higher temperature (1).

Two more appear in the bank above but are not examinable in the 2026–28 cycle: E = mL (specific latent heat was removed from 0625 for 2026–28) and p₁/T₁ = p₂/T₂ (the temperature effect on gas pressure is examined qualitatively only). ✅ The model that answers half the paper
Almost every Topic 2 question is really one idea in disguise: "add energy → particles move faster / push apart." Faster particles = higher temperature, harder wall-hits = more pressure, wider spacing = expansion, broken bonds = change of state. Name the particles and you've started the answer.
