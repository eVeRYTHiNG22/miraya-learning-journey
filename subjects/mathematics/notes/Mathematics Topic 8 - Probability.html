01 / 10 · E8.1 · The big picture

Probability is just a share of the outcomes

A probability measures how likely something is, on a fixed scale from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain). Nothing in this topic can ever be negative, and nothing can ever be more than 1 — if your answer is, you have made an arithmetic slip, not a discovery.

💡 The core idea
List every way the situation could turn out, making sure each way is equally likely. Count the ways you care about, and divide by the total. That fraction is the probability. Everything else in Topic 8 — Venns, trees, conditional probability — is just a smarter way of doing that same counting when the list gets too long to write out.
🎯 A picture you know
Picture a one-metre ribbon laid on a table, and you drop a pin on it blindly. Each possible outcome is given a length of ribbon in proportion to how often it happens, and the whole ribbon is exactly 1 metre — no gaps, no overlaps. "P(barfi) = 0.25" means barfi owns 25 cm of the ribbon. Now you can see why probabilities must add to 1 and why none can exceed 1: there is only one ribbon.
Probability of a single event
P(A) = number of favourable outcomes ÷ total number of equally likely outcomes
Give the answer as a fraction, decimal or percentage — all three are accepted. Never as a ratio ("1 in 4" is not a probability) and never as "1 out of 4 chances".

Worked example

A box on the counter at a Diwali sweet shop holds 20 mithai: 8 kaju katli, 7 gulab jamun and 5 barfi. Diya takes one without looking.

total equally likely outcomes = 20 sweets
P(barfi) = 5/20 = 1/4 = 0.25 = 25%
P(gulab jamun) = 7/20 = 0.35
check — every sweet is one of the three: 8/20 + 7/20 + 5/20 = 20/20 = 1 ✓
The probability scale. Rolling a 7 is impossible (0); the sun rising is certain (1); heads on a fair coin is an even chance (½). A fair die shows more than 2 in 4 of its 6 faces, so that event sits at ⅔ ≈ 0.67 — likely, but nowhere near certain
⚠️ The classic wrong intuition
"There are two outcomes — it rains or it doesn't — so P(rain) = ½." No. The counting rule only works when the outcomes are equally likely. Two outcomes does not mean fifty-fifty: a drawing pin can land point-up or point-down, but it is not a fair coin. When outcomes are not equally likely the counting formula simply does not apply — you must be given the probabilities, or estimate them by experiment (Section 3).
✅ Notation you are expected to use
P(A) means "the probability of event A". Extended papers use it freely, and also P(A′) for "not A". Leave fractions unsimplified if you like — 5/20 scores the same as 1/4 — but if you switch to a decimal it must be exact, or correct to at least 3 significant figures: 1/3 becomes 0.333, never 0.33. The exact fraction is always the safest answer.
02 / 10 · E8.1 · Not A

The complement — the cheapest mark in the topic

Every event splits the world in two: it happens, or it doesn't. Those two possibilities cover everything and overlap nowhere, so their probabilities must add to 1.

The complement rule
P(A′) = 1 − P(A)
Read A′ as "not A" — sometimes written "A dash" or "A complement".
🎒 A picture you know
Back to the one-metre ribbon. Event A owns a stretch of it; "not A" is everything else on the ribbon. You don't have to measure the rest piece by piece — just take the whole metre and cut off A's piece.

Worked example

The probability that Aarav's school bus is late on a given morning is 0.15. A spinner used at a school mela has red, green, blue and yellow sectors with P(red) = 0.4, P(green) = 0.25 and P(blue) = 0.2.

bus: P(not late) = 1 − 0.15 = 0.85
spinner — all four sectors must total 1:
P(yellow) = 1 − (0.4 + 0.25 + 0.2) = 1 − 0.85 = 0.15
✅ Why this rule earns marks everywhere
Whenever a question says "at least one", stop and use the complement. The opposite of "at least one red" is the single, simple event "no reds at all". Counting "exactly one red, exactly two reds, exactly three reds…" is three calculations and three chances to slip; 1 − P(none) is one. You will use this in Section 7.
⚠️ Two traps
(1) The complement of "at least one" is not "none of them except one" — it is simply "none". (2) Probabilities of a full outcome list add to 1, not to 100, unless you are working in percentages throughout. Don't mix: 0.4 + 25% is a mark lost.
03 / 10 · E8.2 · Experiment

Relative frequency & expected frequency

Sometimes you cannot count equally likely outcomes — a drawing pin, a bent spinner, a bottle cap. So you experiment: do the trial many times and see what fraction of the time the event actually happened. That fraction is the relative frequency, and it is your best estimate of the probability.

The two frequency formulas
relative frequency = (times the event happened) ÷ (total trials)
expected frequency = P(event) × (number of trials)
One goes from data to probability; the other from probability back to a predicted count. They are inverses of each other.
💡 Why more trials means a better estimate
Toss a coin 10 times and 7 heads would not surprise you — relative frequency 0.7, miles from 0.5. Toss it 10 000 times and 7000 heads would be astonishing. Each extra trial adds evidence but the random wobble grows more slowly than the number of trials does, so dividing one by the other settles down. That is the whole reason experiments work.

Spin the wheel below. On the fair setting all four sectors are 0.25; on the biased setting red owns half the wheel. Watch how wildly the bars swing for the first few spins, then how firmly they lock on to the dashed marker (the true probability) once you have hundreds.

Worked example — expected frequency

A survey finds that 0.35 of students at a school of 1200 travel to school by bus. At the school fete, 500 raffle tickets are sold at ₹20 each and Meera buys 4 of them.

expected number of bus travellers = P × trials
= 0.35 × 1200 = 420 students

raffle — one ticket is drawn from 500, all equally likely
P(Meera wins) = 4/500 = 1/125 = 0.008
expected number of wins if the same raffle ran 250 times
= 0.008 × 250 = 2
Fair
Every outcome is equally likely. A fair die gives each face probability ⅙.
Biased
Some outcome is more likely than it should be. You detect bias when the relative frequency stays far from the expected value after many trials.
Random
The outcome of any one trial cannot be predicted — even from a fair, well-understood process.
⚠️ Expected ≠ guaranteed (and needn't be a whole number)
"Expected frequency 67.5" is a perfectly good answer — do not round it unless the question explicitly tells you to. Mark schemes print the exact non-integer value even when the quantity is a number of people or objects. And an expected 420 bus travellers does not mean exactly 420 will turn up; it is the long-run average. Equally, "the coin landed heads 5 times in a row, so tails is due" is wrong — the coin has no memory.
✅ Judging bias in words
If asked "is the dice fair? Give a reason", compare the relative frequency with the expected value and mention the number of trials. "Relative frequency 0.30 is much higher than the expected ⅙ ≈ 0.17, over 600 throws, so the dice is probably biased" scores; "it looks biased" does not.
04 / 10 · E8.3 · Listing everything

Sample space diagrams

When two things happen together — two dice, a coin and a spinner, two draws from a bag — the safest method is still to list every outcome. A sample space diagram is that list arranged as a grid, so you cannot miss any.

💡 The core idea
One die has 6 outcomes. A second die has 6 outcomes for each of those, so together there are 6 × 6 = 36 — and because both dice are fair, all 36 are equally likely. That is the crucial part: once every cell of the grid is equally likely, probability is pure cell-counting.
The sum of two fair dice. Because each of the 36 cells is equally likely, every probability here is a count divided by 36. Seven is the most likely total — it fills a whole diagonal (6 cells); 2 and 12 each fill only one

Reading the grid

P(total = 7) = 6/36 = 1/6
P(total = 12) = 1/36
P(total > 9) = (10:3 cells) + (11:2) + (12:1) = 6/36 = 1/6
P(a double) = 6/36 = 1/6  the cells 1&1, 2&2, … 6&6
P(total is even)= 18/36 = 1/2
⚠️ Order matters — 36 outcomes, not 21
A 2 on the first die and a 5 on the second is a different outcome from a 5 then a 2, even though the total is the same. Students who "don't count the same pair twice" get 21 outcomes and every answer comes out wrong. Two dice, two ordered results: 6 × 6 = 36.
✅ When the two things are different
A grid works for any pair. Coin (H, T) with a die (1–6) gives a 2 × 6 = 12-cell grid. If the question gives you an empty grid, fill it in completely before answering — the filled grid is usually worth marks on its own.
05 / 10 · E8.3 · Overlapping groups

Venn diagrams

Use a Venn diagram when two events can both happen — a student who plays cricket and badminton, a number that is both even and prime. The rectangle is everything (the universal set, ℰ); each circle is one event; the overlap is where both are true.

💡 Always fill the middle first
A question saying "18 play cricket, 14 play badminton, 6 play both" does not mean 18 sit in the cricket-only region. The 18 already includes the 6. So write the 6 in the overlap first, then subtract: cricket-only = 18 − 6 = 12, badminton-only = 14 − 6 = 8. Start anywhere else and every region is wrong.
The two symbols
A ∩ B — intersection, "A and B" — the overlap only.
A ∪ B — union, "A or B (or both)" — everything inside either circle.
Memory hook: ∩ looks like the letter n for "and"; ∪ is a cup that scoops everything up.
30 students: 18 play cricket, 14 play badminton, 6 play both. The 6 goes in the middle first; then 18 − 6 = 12 and 14 − 6 = 8. The four regions must add to the total, so "neither" = 30 − (12 + 6 + 8) = 4

Reading probabilities off the diagram

divide every region count by the total, 30
P(C ∩ B) = 6/30 = 1/5
P(C ∪ B) = (12 + 6 + 8)/30 = 26/30 = 13/15
P(C′) = (8 + 4)/30 = 12/30 = 2/5  everything outside circle C
P(neither) = 4/30 = 2/15
check with the union rule: 18/30 + 14/30 − 6/30 = 26/30 ✓
⚠️ Know this cold
n(A ∪ B) = n(A) + n(B) − n(A ∩ B) — you subtract the overlap because adding the two circles counts it twice. The same rule works with probabilities: P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B).

✅ Mutually exclusive
Two events are mutually exclusive when they cannot both happen — the circles don't overlap, so P(A ∩ B) = 0 and the union rule collapses to P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B). "Rolling a 3" and "rolling an even number" are mutually exclusive; "rolling a 3" and "rolling an odd number" are not.
06 / 10 · E8.3 · Two stages

Tree diagrams — multiply along, add down

When something happens in stages — pick a counter, then pick another; spin, then spin again — a tree diagram lays out every path. Probabilities go beside the branches; outcomes go at the ends.

💡 Why you multiply along a branch
Think of the ribbon again. The first pick cuts the metre into pieces — 5/8 of it is "first counter red". Now the second pick cuts that piece up in the same proportions: 5/8 of the red piece is "red again". A fraction of a fraction is a product. That is all "multiply along the branches" means.
The two tree rules
AND → multiply along a path (this happens and then that)
OR → add the finished paths (this outcome or that one)
Branches leaving any one node always add to 1 — a free check as you fill the tree in.

Worked example — with replacement

A bag holds 5 red and 3 blue counters. Rohan takes one at random, puts it back, and takes another.

Two picks with replacement: the counter goes back, so the bag is identical for the second pick and every branch fraction is eighths again. The two events are independent — the first result tells you nothing about the second
P(both red) = 5/8 × 5/8 = 25/64
P(one of each colour) = P(RB) + P(BR) = 15/64 + 15/64 = 30/64 = 15/32
"at least one blue" — use the complement (Section 2):
P(at least one blue) = 1 − P(no blue) = 1 − P(RR) = 1 − 25/64 = 39/64
⚠️ One of each colour is not one path
"One red and one blue" happens two ways — red-then-blue and blue-then-red. Forgetting the second path halves your answer. Whenever the question doesn't say which came first, check whether more than one path lands on that outcome.
✅ Don't cancel too early
Keep the products over their common denominator while you add paths, and simplify only at the very end. In the next section you will meet 20/56 + 6/56 — trivial. Simplify each one first and you are adding 5/14 + 3/28, which needs a fresh common denominator for no reason. Then check your four path probabilities total 1 before you go on.
07 / 10 · E8.3 · Extended

Without replacement — the bag changes

Extended papers almost always take the counter out and keep it out. That single change makes the second pick depend on the first — and it is where most marks in this topic are won or lost.

💡 One counter fewer — in two places
If the first counter drawn was red, then for the second draw there is one fewer red and one fewer counter altogether. So the fraction changes on top and bottom: 5/8 becomes 4/7. The denominator always drops by one; the numerator drops only for the colour you removed. Students who change only the bottom lose the marks.
The same bag, without replacement. Only 7 counters remain for the second pick, so every second-stage denominator is 7. After a red the reds fall to 4; after a blue they stay at 5 — the branch you are on decides the numerator
P(both red) = 5/8 × 4/7 = 20/56 = 5/14
P(one of each) = 15/56 + 15/56 = 30/56 = 15/28
P(at least one blue) = 1 − P(RR) = 1 − 20/56 = 36/56 = 9/14

Change the bag below and flip between the two settings. Watch the second-stage fractions: on with replacement they never move; on without replacement the denominator drops to one less, and the numerator drops only on the branch whose colour was taken.

⚠️ "Independent" only survives replacement
With replacement the two picks are independent — the first result changes nothing. Without replacement they are not: the second-stage fractions are already conditional probabilities (Section 8). A question that says the counter "is not replaced", or that two people each take a sweet, or that describes picking "two counters at the same time", all mean the same thing: without replacement.
✅ Three picks
A third pick is the same idea one step further: denominators 8, 7, 6. P(three reds) from this bag = 5/8 × 4/7 × 3/6 = 60/336 = 5/28. You are not usually asked to draw a three-stage tree, but you are expected to extend the multiplication.
08 / 10 · E8.4 · Extended only

Conditional probability — shrink the pool

"Given that the student cycles, what is the probability she is in Grade 9?" The word given tells you something has already been ruled out. You are no longer choosing from everybody — only from the cyclists.

💡 The whole idea in one line
Conditional probability is ordinary probability with a smaller denominator. Cross out everyone the condition excludes, then count what's left as if it were the whole world. No formula is needed — and the syllabus explicitly does not require one.
The shorthand P(A | B) ("the probability of A given B") is used below as a compact label. You are not required to know this notation for 0580 — exam questions always say "given that…" in words — but it is handy for keeping the two directions apart.
🔦 A picture you know
Imagine shining a torch on just one row of a two-way table. Everything outside the beam has stopped existing. Your fraction is now "the cell I want ÷ the row total" — the row total, not the grand total.

From a two-way table

How 80 students of a school travel in:

 BusCycleWalkTotal
Grade 918121040
Grade 1014161040
Total32282080
no condition — denominator is the grand total, 80
P(cycles) = 28/80 = 7/20

"given the student is in Grade 10" — denominator becomes that row, 40
P(cycles | Grade 10) = 16/40 = 2/5

"given the student cycles" — denominator becomes that column, 28
P(Grade 9 | cycles) = 12/28 = 3/7
⚠️ The two conditionals are different questions
P(cycles | Grade 10) = 2/5 but P(Grade 10 | cycles) = 16/28 = 4/7. Same cell on top, different denominator — and different answers. Read carefully which group the question has already restricted you to; that group is your denominator.

From a Venn diagram

Using the cricket/badminton diagram from Section 5 (12 cricket only, 6 both, 8 badminton only, 4 neither):

given a student plays cricket — only the 18 in circle C exist now
P(plays badminton | plays cricket) = 6/18 = 1/3
given a student plays badminton — only the 14 in circle B
P(plays cricket | plays badminton) = 6/14 = 3/7
✅ You have already done this on a tree
In the without-replacement tree, 4/7 was the probability of red on the second pick given red on the first. Every second-stage branch of a without-replacement tree is a conditional probability — which is exactly why those trees are the natural home for E8.4 questions. If a question gives you a completed tree and asks for a conditional, the answer is often just a branch you can read off.
The three-step method that always works
1. Identify the condition — the group after the word "given" (or "of those who…").
2. That group's total becomes your denominator.
3. Count only the members of that group who also satisfy the event — that's the numerator.
09 / 10 · Retrieval

Check yourself — try each one on paper first

Eight questions on the places Topic 8 marks are actually lost. Work each out before you tap.

Q1A biased spinner has four sectors: red, green, blue, yellow. P(red) = 0.3, P(green) = 0.25, P(blue) = 0.18. Find P(yellow), then the expected number of yellows in 250 spins.
Tap to reveal
All four must total 1, so P(yellow) = 1 − (0.3 + 0.25 + 0.18) = 1 − 0.73 = 0.27. Expected frequency = 0.27 × 250 = 67.5. Leave it as 67.5 — an expected frequency is a long-run average and need not be a whole number. Rounding to 68 without being asked can cost the mark.
Q2Two fair dice are thrown and their scores added. Find the probability that the total is a prime number.
Tap to reveal
Prime totals are 2, 3, 5, 7 and 11. From the 6 × 6 grid the counts are 2→1, 3→2, 5→4, 7→6, 11→2, giving 1 + 2 + 4 + 6 + 2 = 15 cells. So P = 15/36 = 5/12. Traps: 1 is not prime (and is impossible anyway), and 9 is not prime (3 × 3).
Q3In a group of 40 people, 25 like tea, 18 like coffee and 5 like neither. How many like both? Then find the probability that a person chosen at random likes tea only.
Tap to reveal
People who like at least one drink: 40 − 5 = 35, so n(T ∪ C) = 35. Using n(T ∪ C) = n(T) + n(C) − n(both): 35 = 25 + 18 − n(both), so n(both) = 43 − 35 = 8. Tea only = 25 − 8 = 17, so P(tea only) = 17/40. The trap is treating the 25 as "tea only" — it already includes the 8.
Q4A box holds 12 pens, of which 4 are faulty. Two pens are taken at random without replacement. Find the probability that at least one is faulty.
Tap to reveal
Use the complement: the opposite of "at least one faulty" is "both fine". P(both fine) = 8/12 × 7/11 = 56/132 = 14/33. So P(at least one faulty) = 1 − 14/33 = 19/33. Doing it the long way (exactly one + exactly two) also gives 19/33, but is three times the work and three times the risk.
Q5Explain why an estimate of a probability found from 500 trials is more reliable than one found from 20 trials.
Tap to reveal
Because relative frequency approaches the true probability as the number of trials increases. With few trials a run of luck shifts the fraction a long way; with many trials each result changes the fraction only slightly, so the estimate settles down. Command-word tip: the mark is for the phrase "more trials → relative frequency gets closer to the theoretical probability", not for "it's more accurate".
Q6From the travel table in Section 8, find P(walks | Grade 9) and P(Grade 9 | walks). Why are they different?
Tap to reveal
P(walks | Grade 9) = 10/40 = 1/4 — restricted to the Grade 9 row. P(Grade 9 | walks) = 10/20 = 1/2 — restricted to the walkers column. The numerator (10 students who are both) is the same; the condition changes the denominator, and that is what changes the answer.
Q7A bag has 7 green and 4 white marbles. Two are taken without replacement. Find the probability they are different colours.
Tap to reveal
Two paths. P(GW) = 7/11 × 4/10 = 28/110 and P(WG) = 4/11 × 7/10 = 28/110. Total = 56/110 = 28/55. Note the two paths are equal here — that is normal, but you must still add both. Check: P(GG) = 42/110 and P(WW) = 12/110, and 42 + 28 + 28 + 12 = 110 ✓.
Q8From a bag of 5 red and 3 blue counters, three are taken without replacement. Find the probability that all three are the same colour.
Tap to reveal
P(RRR) = 5/8 × 4/7 × 3/6 = 60/336 and P(BBB) = 3/8 × 2/7 × 1/6 = 6/336. Same colour = 60/336 + 6/336 = 66/336 = 11/56. Keeping everything over 336 until the last line is what makes this safe; the denominators fall 8, 7, 6 and the numerators fall only for the colour being removed.
