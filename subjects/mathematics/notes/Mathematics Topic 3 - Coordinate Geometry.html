Coordinate Geometry

Intuition-first notes for the whole of Topic 3 (E3.1–E3.7). Coordinate geometry is where algebra and shape finally meet: a pair of numbers pins a point, and one small equation carries a whole infinite line. Learn four formulas properly — gradient, length, midpoint, y = mx + c — and every exam question in this topic is a rearrangement of them.

01 / 12 · E3.1 · Coordinates

The plane, and reading a point

Two number lines crossed at right angles turn a flat surface into an address system. Every point gets exactly one address (x, y): how far across first, then how far up. The crossing point is the origin, (0, 0).

💡 The core idea
A coordinate pair is an ordered pair — the order carries the meaning. (4, 3) and (3, 4) are two different points. If you remember nothing else, remember "along the corridor, then up the stairs": the x-coordinate always comes first.

A coordinate grid is just a map

A map of Anand Nagar is printed on a grid where 1 unit = 100 m, with the bus stand at the origin. The temple is at T(4, 3) and Meera's house at H(−3, −2). "4 across, 3 up" from the bus stand means 400 m east and 300 m north — the coordinates carry real distances the moment you know the scale.

⚠️ Three ways students lose this free mark
  • Writing the pair backwards — (3, 4) when the point is (4, 3).
  • Losing a minus sign on the way to the left or downwards.
  • Miscounting the scale: on many exam grids one square is 2 units, not 1. Always read the numbers on the axis, never count squares.
02 / 12 · E3.2 · Drawing linear graphs

Drawing a straight line from its equation

A linear equation is one where x and y appear only to the power 1 — no x², no xy, no 1/x. Its graph is always a perfectly straight line, so you only ever need two points to draw it. Sensible students plot three: the third one catches an arithmetic slip, because if the three don't line up, one of them is wrong.

Linear graph — the set of all the points (x, y) whose coordinates satisfy the equation. A point lies on the line exactly when substituting its coordinates makes the equation true.

Method 1 — a table of values

Best when the equation is already in the form y = …. Choose three x-values, work out each y, plot, join with a ruled line, and label the line with its equation.

draw y = −2x + 5
x = −1 → y = −2(−1) + 5 = 7
x = 0 → y = 0 + 5 = 5
x = 3 → y = −6 + 5 = −1
plot (−1, 7), (0, 5), (3, −1) → join with a ruler

Method 2 — the cover-up (intercept) method

Much faster when the equation is in the form ax + by = c, e.g. 3x + 2y = 12. Cover up one term at a time:

  • Put x = 0 → 2y = 12 → y = 6, so the line cuts the y-axis at (0, 6).
  • Put y = 0 → 3x = 12 → x = 4, so it cuts the x-axis at (4, 0).

Two points, ruler, done. This is the fastest method for 3x + 2y = 5-style equations — a table of values is equally acceptable, just slower.

Fig 2 · The cover-up method: set x = 0 for the y-intercept, y = 0 for the x-intercept, then rule the line through them.

The two special lines

x = k — vertical
Every point has x-coordinate k, whatever y is. x = 3 is the vertical line 3 to the right of the origin. Its gradient is undefined. The y-axis itself is x = 0.
y = k — horizontal
Every point has y-coordinate k, whatever x is. y = −2 is the horizontal line 2 below the origin. Its gradient is 0. The x-axis itself is y = 0.
⚠️ x = 3 is the vertical one
Nearly everyone mixes these up at least once. Say it out loud: "x = 3 means x is stuck at 3 — I can only move up and down, so the line is vertical." Same logic for y = −2: y is stuck, so you can only move sideways.
✅ Marks for the drawing itself
Use a sharp pencil and a ruler, draw the line right across the given grid (not just between your two plotted points), and label it with its equation. A short, freehand or unlabelled line loses marks even when the points are correct.
03 / 12 · E3.3 · Gradient

Gradient — steepness turned into a number

Gradient (m) — how many units the line rises for every 1 unit you move to the right. m = rise / run = (y₂ − y₁) / (x₂ − x₁)
🧩 A ramp at the Metro station
A wheelchair ramp that climbs 1 m for every 12 m along has gradient 1/12 — gentle. One that climbs 1 m for every 2 m along has gradient 1/2 — steep and probably illegal. Gradient is nothing more exotic than "up per across", and the bigger the number, the steeper the climb.

From a grid: draw the staircase

Pick two points where the line passes exactly through grid corners — reading half-squares is how gradient marks get lost. Draw the right-angled triangle underneath, count the run and the rise, and divide.

Fig 3 · Choose two grid-corner points, box in the right-angled triangle, then divide rise by run — and always simplify.

From coordinates: subtract, subtract, divide

gradient of the line through A(−4, −1) and B(2, 3)
m = (y₂ − y₁) / (x₂ − x₁) = (3 − (−1)) / (2 − (−4))
= 4 / 6 = 2/3

It doesn't matter which point you call "1" and which "2" — (−1 − 3)/(−4 − 2) = −4/−6 = 2/3 as well. What does matter is that both subtractions run the same way round. Flipping one and not the other is the single commonest error in this topic.

The four kinds of gradient

Fig 4 · A vertical line has no gradient at all — dividing by a run of zero is undefined, not "infinity" and not zero.

What you should notice
Positive m tilts the line uphill; negative m tilts it downhill. Changing c never changes the tilt — it just slides the whole line vertically. The dashed step triangle always has a run of 1, so its height is m.

What gradient means in a real graph

When the axes carry units, the gradient carries units too — it is a rate. An auto-rickshaw in Pune charges a fixed ₹30 flag-down plus ₹15 per kilometre, so the cost graph is C = 15d + 30:

  • the gradient 15 is the rate — ₹15 for every extra kilometre;
  • the intercept 30 is the cost before you have travelled at all.

At d = 6 km the fare is 15(6) + 30 = ₹120; checking with the gradient, (120 − 30)/6 = 15 ✓.

⚠️ Sign errors and upside-down fractions
Two traps, both worth a mark every time: writing run/rise instead of rise/run, and dropping the minus sign on a downhill line. Look at the picture before you write the answer — if the line goes down as you read left to right, your m must be negative.
04 / 12 · E3.4 · Length (Extended)

Length of a line segment

This is Pythagoras in disguise. The horizontal gap and the vertical gap between two points are the two short sides of a right-angled triangle; the segment joining the points is the hypotenuse.

Length formula — AB = √((x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²). Both differences are squared, so their signs stop mattering — a length is never negative.
Fig 5 · The gap across and the gap up are the two short sides; the segment is the hypotenuse. M is the midpoint (next section).

Worked example — two mobile towers

On a survey map of Nashik (1 unit = 1 km) two towers stand at A(2, 1) and B(8, 9). Find the straight-line distance between them.

differences x₂ − x₁ = 8 − 2 = 6 · y₂ − y₁ = 9 − 1 = 8
square and add 6² + 8² = 36 + 64 = 100
square root AB = √100 = 10 km

When the answer isn't a whole number

Most exam answers are not as tidy as 10. Find the length of PQ where P(−1, 2) and Q(4, 6):

differences 4 − (−1) = 5 · 6 − 2 = 4
PQ = √(5² + 4²) = √(25 + 16) = √41
= 6.403124… = 6.40 (3 s.f.)
✅ Rounding, and when not to round
Unless the question says otherwise, give a non-exact answer to 3 significant figures. If the question says "give your answer in exact form" or "in the form k√n", leave the surd: √80 = 4√5. Never round in the middle of a longer question — keep the full value in your calculator.
⚠️ Square-root the total, not the pieces
√(6² + 8²) is 10, not 6 + 8 = 14. Add the two squares first, then take one square root of the total.
05 / 12 · E3.4 · Midpoint (Extended)

Midpoint of a line segment

Midpoint — M = ((x₁ + x₂)/2 , (y₁ + y₂)/2). It is simply the average of the x's and the average of the y's.
💡 Why an average?
Halfway between two numbers is their mean: halfway between 2 and 8 is 5. Do that separately in each direction and you land exactly halfway along the segment. Notice in Fig 5 that M(5, 5) sits at the centre of A(2, 1) and B(8, 9) — and that the two halves AM and MB are each 5 units, half of 10.

Worked example — where to build the water tank

A village committee wants an overhead tank built exactly halfway between the school S(−4, 2) and the clinic C(6, 8) on the panchayat's grid map.

x of M (−4 + 6) / 2 = 2/2 = 1
y of M (2 + 8) / 2 = 10/2 = 5
so M = (1, 5)

Working backwards — the classic twist

You are given the midpoint and one endpoint, and asked for the other. Don't guess: use the fact that M is the average, so x₂ = 2·(x of M) − x₁.

given M(2, −1) is the midpoint of AB, with A(−3, 4). Find B.
x of B = 2(2) − (−3) = 4 + 3 = 7
y of B = 2(−1) − 4 = −2 − 4 = −6
so B = (7, −6)
check midpoint of (−3, 4) and (7, −6) = ((−3+7)/2, (4−6)/2) = (2, −1) ✓
⚠️ Midpoint ≠ subtract
The gradient and length formulas subtract; the midpoint formula adds. Under exam pressure students blur them and compute (x₂ − x₁)/2. Say it deliberately: "gradient and length subtract, midpoint averages."
✅ Always write M as a coordinate pair
The answer is a point: write (1, 5), with the brackets and the comma — not "1 and 5", and not "x = 1, y = 5" as your final line. Half a mark's worth of presentation, given away for free every year.
06 / 12 · E3.5 · Equation of a line

Finding the equation y = mx + c

Every non-vertical straight line can be written as y = mx + c, where m is the gradient and c is the y-intercept — the y-value where the line crosses the y-axis. Finding the equation of a line is therefore always the same two-part job: get m, then get c.

Point–gradient form — if you know the gradient m and any one point (x₁, y₁) on the line, then y − y₁ = m(x − x₁). Expand it and you are back at y = mx + c.

Case 1 — read it off a graph

Find where the line crosses the y-axis (that's c), then build a gradient triangle on the drawn line (that's m). A line crossing at (0, −2) that rises 3 for every 4 across has equation y = (3/4)x − 2.

Case 2 — a gradient and one point

find the line with gradient 2/3 through (3, −4)
method A — substitute into y = mx + c
−4 = (2/3)(3) + c → −4 = 2 + c → c = −6
method B — point–gradient
y − (−4) = (2/3)(x − 3) → y + 4 = (2/3)x − 2
either way y = (2/3)x − 6

Case 3 — two points (the most common exam version)

find the equation of the line through A(−2, 5) and B(4, −7)
1 · gradient m = (−7 − 5) / (4 − (−2)) = −12/6 = −2
2 · substitute A 5 = −2(−2) + c → 5 = 4 + c → c = 1
3 · write it y = −2x + 1
4 · check with B −2(4) + 1 = −8 + 1 = −7 ✓
✅ Always check with the other point
Step 4 costs five seconds and catches almost every sign slip. Substitute the point you didn't use into your finished equation; if it doesn't give the right y, your m or your c is wrong.

Is this point on the line?

To test whether (5, −9) lies on y = −2x + 1, substitute: −2(5) + 1 = −9 ✓, so yes. A point is on a line exactly when its coordinates satisfy the equation — nothing to draw, nothing to measure.

⚠️ "Fully simplified" is part of the answer
The syllabus says candidates are expected to give equations in a fully simplified form. So y = 4x + 0 should be y = 4x; y = (6/9)x + 2 should be y = (2/3)x + 2; and y = 1x − 3 is just y = x − 3. Leaving an unsimplified fraction can cost the accuracy mark.
07 / 12 · E3.5 · Forms of a line

Other forms: ax + by = c and x = k

Exam questions deliberately hand you lines in a form that hides the gradient. You cannot read m off 5x + 4y = 8 — you must rearrange into y = mx + c first. Make this a reflex.

find the gradient and y-intercept of 5x + 4y = 8
4y = −5x + 8
y = (−5/4)x + 2
so gradient = −5/4, y-intercept = (0, 2)

Going the other way — clearing fractions

If a question asks for the answer "in the form ax + by = c where a, b and c are integers", multiply through by the denominator and tidy up.

start y = (2/3)x − 6
× 3 3y = 2x − 18
rearrange 2x − 3y = 18

From 3x − 6y = 12: −6y = −3x + 12, and dividing by −6 gives y = (1/2)x − 2. Every term changes sign, including the one you're least watching. Gradient +1/2, not −1/2 and certainly not 3.
08 / 12 · E3.6 · Parallel lines

Parallel lines — same tilt, different height

Parallel ⇔ equal gradients — two distinct non-vertical lines are parallel exactly when m₁ = m₂. Their c values must differ (if c matched too, they would be the same line). Two vertical lines such as x = 1 and x = 3 are parallel too — they just have no gradient to compare.
Fig 6 · Same gradient, different intercept: the lines stay a fixed distance apart and never meet.

Worked example — the syllabus's own question

Find the equation of the line parallel to y = 4x − 1 that passes through (1, −3).

1 · copy the gradient parallel → m = 4
2 · substitute the point −3 = 4(1) + c → c = −7
3 · answer y = 4x − 7

When the given line is disguised

Find the line parallel to 2x + 5y = 10 through (5, −1), in the form ax + by = c.

1 · rearrange first 5y = −2x + 10 → y = (−2/5)x + 2, so m = −2/5
2 · substitute −1 = (−2/5)(5) + c → −1 = −2 + c → c = 1
3 · y = (−2/5)x + 1, then × 5 5y = −2x + 5
4 · answer 2x + 5y = 5
✅ The word "parallel" is worth one instant mark
As soon as you read it, write m = … (the same gradient). That's usually M1 in the mark scheme before you have done any real work. Then it's just "substitute the point to find c".
09 / 12 · E3.7 · Perpendicular lines (Extended)

Perpendicular lines — flip it and change the sign

Perpendicular ⇔ m₁ × m₂ = −1 — so the second gradient is the negative reciprocal of the first: m₂ = −1/m₁.
🧩 Why "flip and negate"?
Turning a direction through 90° swaps "across" and "up": a run of 2 with a rise of 1 becomes a run of 1 with a drop of 2. So 1/2 turns into −2/1 — the fraction turns upside down and the sign flips. Check it: (1/2) × (−2) = −1 ✓.

Worked example — the syllabus's own question

Find the gradient of a line perpendicular to 2y = 3x + 1.

1 · rearrange to y = mx + c y = (3/2)x + 1/2, so m₁ = 3/2
2 · negative reciprocal m₂ = −1 ÷ (3/2) = −2/3

And with a point attached

Find the equation of the line perpendicular to y = −4x + 3 that passes through (8, 1).

1 · perpendicular gradient m₁ = −4 → m₂ = 1/4
2 · substitute (8, 1) 1 = (1/4)(8) + c → 1 = 2 + c → c = −1
3 · answer y = (1/4)x − 1
⚠️ Flip AND negate — students do only one
For m₁ = 3/2, the perpendicular gradient is not 2/3 (flipped only) and not −3/2 (negated only). It is −2/3. Do both, then multiply the two gradients as a check — you must get exactly −1.
10 / 12 · E3.4 + E3.7 · The set-piece

The perpendicular bisector — everything at once

This is the question the examiners use to test the whole topic in one go, and the syllabus names it explicitly. It needs three of your formulas in sequence, so do them in a fixed order and label each step.

Perpendicular bisector of AB — the line that cuts AB in half (so it passes through the midpoint) and at right angles (so its gradient is the negative reciprocal of the gradient of AB). Every point on it is the same distance from A as from B.

The four steps, in order

find the perpendicular bisector of the line joining A(−3, 8) and B(9, −2)

1 · midpoint of AB M = ((−3 + 9)/2, (8 + (−2))/2) = (6/2, 6/2) = (3, 3)
2 · gradient of AB m = (−2 − 8) / (9 − (−3)) = −10/12 = −5/6
3 · perpendicular gradient m⊥ = −1 ÷ (−5/6) = 6/5
4 · line through M with gradient 6/5
3 = (6/5)(3) + c → 3 = 18/5 + c → c = 3 − 18/5 = −3/5
answer y = (6/5)x − 3/5, or 6x − 5y = 3
Fig 8 · Midpoint first, then gradient, then flip-and-negate, then substitute M. The bisector is the mirror line of A and B.

✅ Show every step — the method marks are the marks
A perpendicular-bisector question is typically 4–5 marks, and only the last one is for the final equation. Write "M = …", "mAB = …", "m⊥ = …" on separate lines. Even with an arithmetic slip at the end you keep the method marks — and an unlabelled page of numbers earns none of them.
🔁 The order never changes
Midpoint → gradient of AB → negative reciprocal → substitute the midpoint. Students who find the gradient first and then forget the midpoint produce a line that is perpendicular but doesn't bisect — a line through the wrong place, and no accuracy marks.

Q6Find the gradient of a line perpendicular to 2y = 3x + 1.
Tap to reveal
Rearranging: y = (3/2)x + 1/2, so m₁ = 3/2. Perpendicular gradient = −1 ÷ (3/2) = −2/3. Check: (3/2) × (−2/3) = −1 ✓.
Q7Find the equation of the perpendicular bisector of the line joining A(−3, 8) and B(9, −2).
Tap to reveal
M = (3, 3). mAB = (−2 − 8)/(9 + 3) = −10/12 = −5/6, so m⊥ = 6/5. Through M: 3 = (6/5)(3) + c → c = 3 − 18/5 = −3/5. So y = (6/5)x − 3/5, i.e. 6x − 5y = 3.
Q8The line L has equation 3x − 6y = 12. Sasha says "the gradient of L is 3". Explain why she is wrong, and state the correct gradient.
Tap to reveal
She has read the coefficient of x without rearranging into the form y = mx + c — the gradient can only be read off once y is the subject. Rearranging: −6y = −3x + 12 → y = (1/2)x − 2. The correct gradient is 1/2.
