01 / 11 · E9.1 · Collect

First decide what kind of data you have

Everything you are allowed to do later — which chart, which average, whether a histogram is even legal — is decided by what kind of numbers you are holding. So this thirty-second classification is not a warm-up; it is the instruction manual for the rest of the topic.

💡 The core idea
Ask two questions in order. (1) Is it a number, or a word? Words (colours, brands, "agree/disagree") are qualitative — you can count how many, but you can never take their mean. (2) If it is a number, can it be measured to ever-finer decimals? If yes it is continuous (mass, height, time); if it only lands on separate values you could list, it is discrete (number of siblings, goals, cars).
🎯 A picture you know
Discrete data is a staircase — you are on step 3 or step 4, never on step 3.4. Continuous data is a ramp — you are somewhere along it, and how precisely you can say where depends only on how good your ruler is. That is why discrete bars stand apart with gaps, and continuous bars are pushed together with no gaps: on a ramp there is nowhere to put a gap.
Qualitative
Categories, not numbers. Favourite subject, blood group, brand of scooter. Bar chart or pie chart. Only the mode makes sense.
Discrete
Counted, separate values. Number of people in a household, runs scored, siblings. Bars with gaps. Mean, median and mode all work.
Continuous
Measured on a scale. Mass, height, time, rainfall. Normally collected into classes, and drawn with bars that touch. Histograms are built for grouped data like this.

✅ Exam technique
Two-way tables usually arrive with holes in them. Fill them by subtracting along a row or down a column, and always finish by checking the grand total both ways: 40 + 50 = 90 and 32 + 32 + 26 = 90 ✓. If the two disagree you have a mistake, and the checking costs five seconds.
⚠️ The classic mistake
"Number of shoes" is discrete; "shoe size" is a trap — sizes go 6, 7, 8 so it behaves as discrete, even though the foot inside is continuous. The rule is about the values recorded, not the thing being measured. Watch the inequality signs too: mass and time are usually classed as 20 < x ≤ 30, but age last birthday rounds down (someone aged 29.8 is recorded as 29), so age classes are written the other way round, 20 ≤ a < 30.
02 / 11 · E9.4 · Display

Charts and diagrams — pick the one that answers the question

💡 The core idea
Every chart trades something away to show something else. A bar chart keeps the actual frequencies, so you can compare sizes. A pie chart throws the frequencies away and keeps only the proportions. A stem-and-leaf diagram keeps every original value, so you can still work out the median afterwards. Choose by asking what you need to get back out.

Bar charts — dual and composite

From the two-way table above, the same numbers give two different pictures. A dual (side-by-side) bar chart is for comparing the two grades sport by sport. A composite (stacked) bar chart is for comparing the total for each sport, with the split still visible inside.

The same 90 students, two chart types. Dual answers "which grade prefers what"; composite answers "which sport is most popular overall". Both need a key and a labelled frequency axis.

Pie charts — the angle is the whole trick

A pie chart shares out 360° in proportion to the frequencies. Do one division first — degrees per item — and the rest is multiplication. 90 students named a favourite subject, so each student is worth 360 ÷ 90 = 4°:

degrees per student = 360 / 90 = 4°
Maths 25 → 25 × 4 = 100°  ·  Science 20 → 80°  ·  English 15 → 60°
Hindi 12 → 48°  ·  Social Studies 18 → 72°
check: 100 + 80 + 60 + 48 + 72 = 360 ✓
A pie chart shows proportions only. Without the "90 students" written on it you could never recover the frequency 25 from the 100° sector.
✅ Exam technique
Pie-chart questions run both directions. Forwards: frequency → angle (freq ÷ total × 360). Backwards is the one that catches people: given a sector of 72° representing 18 students, the total is 18 ÷ 72 × 360 = 90 — or spot that 72° is one fifth of the circle, so 18 is one fifth of the total.

Pictograms — a symbol worth several

A pictogram is only readable if it carries a key. Part-symbols then stand for part of that value:

Four and a half symbols at 10 bags each is 45. With no key the diagram means nothing — drawing one is a mark.

Stem-and-leaf — the diagram that keeps the data

This is the one display you can still do maths from, because no information is thrown away. The stem holds the tens, each leaf a single unit digit, leaves written in order, and a key shows how to read a row. A batsman's runs in 15 innings:

Because every value survives, you can read the median straight off: the 8th of 15 leaves, counting from the top, is 31 runs.
⚠️ Two marks people give away
A stem-and-leaf diagram is only complete with (1) leaves in order within each row and (2) a key. Both are marked. And a "leaf" is always a single digit — 145 goes on stem 14 with leaf 5, never as leaf "45".
03 / 11 · E9.3 · Summarise

Averages and spread — two numbers describe a data set

💡 The core idea
One number can never describe a data set. You need two: an average saying roughly where the data sits, and a measure of spread saying how tightly it is packed around there. Two classes can have identical mean marks and be completely different classes — one where everyone got 70, one where half got 45 and half got 95.
🎯 A picture you know
Think of the values as children of different weights sitting along a see-saw. The mean is the pivot point where the plank balances — so one very heavy child far out at the end drags the pivot towards them. The median is simply the child sitting in the middle seat; move the heaviest child a mile further out and the middle seat does not budge. That single sentence is why exam questions about house prices, salaries and one enormous outlier always want the median.
The three averages and the two spreads
mean = Σx ÷ n median = middle value once ORDERED mode = most common value
range = largest − smallest IQR = Q₃ − Q₁
The quartiles cut the ordered data into four equal parts: Q₁ is the median of the lower half, Q₂ is the median itself, Q₃ is the median of the upper half.

Worked example — 11 test marks

Eleven students in Anjali's class scored these marks out of 30. They are already in order — always order them first, because the median, the quartiles and the range are all meaningless until you do:

14   18   21   21   23   25   25   25   27   28   30    (n = 11)
mean: Σx = 257, so mean = 257 / 11 = 23.363… = 23.4 (1 d.p.)
median: n = 11 is odd, middle is the (11+1)/2 = 6th value = 25
mode: 25 appears three times, more than any other → 25
range: 30 − 14 = 16
Q₁ = median of the lower half (14, 18, 21, 21, 23) = 21
Q₃ = median of the upper half (25, 25, 27, 28, 30) = 27
IQR: 27 − 21 = 6

Notice how much more the IQR tells you than the range: the middle half of this class is squeezed into just 6 marks, even though the full spread is 16. The range was stretched by the single student on 14.

⚠️ The classic mistake
Writing "the median is the middle number" and then taking the middle of the list as printed. If the marks arrive as 25, 14, 30, 21, … the middle of that list is meaningless. Also: the median of an even-sized set is the mean of the two middle values — for 8 values it is (4th + 5th) ÷ 2, and that answer may well end in ·5 even when every data value is a whole number.

✅ Exam technique
"Which average is most appropriate, and give a reason" has a fixed shape: name the average, then justify it from the data. Median → "because there is an extreme value / outlier that would distort the mean". Mode → "because the data is qualitative (shoe colours) so a mean cannot be found" or "because the shopkeeper wants the size that sells most". Mean → "because it uses every value".

Working backwards from a mean

Half the mean questions in the paper run in reverse, and they all use the same lever: Σx = mean × n. Turn the mean back into a total, do the arithmetic on the total, then divide again.

The mean mass of 8 parcels is 2.4 kg. A 9th parcel of mass 4.8 kg is added. New mean?
total of the 8 = 2.4 × 8 = 19.2 kg
new total = 19.2 + 4.8 = 24 kg   over   9 parcels
new mean = 24 / 9 = 2.666… = 2.67 kg (3 s.f.)

Averages from a frequency table

💡 The core idea
A frequency table is just a compressed list. The row "4 people — frequency 12" means the value 4 is written out twelve times. So you are not learning a new formula, you are learning how to add up a list without writing it out: instead of 4 + 4 + 4 + … twelve times, use 4 × 12. That is the entire content of Σfx ÷ Σf.
Mean from a frequency table
mean = Σfx ÷ Σf
Add an fx column and a cumulative frequency column to the table. Almost every mark in these questions is sitting in those two columns.

Worked example — people per household

The mean of 4.175 people is not a possible household — and that is fine. A mean is a fair share, not a real member of the data set. "4.175 people" is the right answer; rounding it to 4 because "you cannot have 0.175 of a person" throws away the mark.

⚠️ The classic mistake — three of them, all in one table
1. Dividing by the number of rows (6) instead of Σf (40). The denominator is always how many items there are, never how many lines the table has.
2. Giving the mode as 12 — that is the frequency. The mode is the value that occurred most often, so it is 4.
3. Giving the median as the middle row of the table. Use the cumulative column and find where the 20th and 21st items actually land.
✅ Exam technique
If the question gives the mean and hides one frequency, set the missing value as k and build the equation. Suppose the 7-person frequency were blank but you were told the mean is 4.175. The other five rows give Σfx = 153 and Σf = 38, so:
(153 + 7k) / (38 + k) = 4.175 → 153 + 7k = 158.65 + 4.175k → 2.825k = 5.65 → k = 2 ✓
A frequency must be a whole number, so an answer like 2.36 is a signal to go back and re-check your Σfx and Σf.

Grouped data — why the mean becomes an estimate

Continuous data cannot be tallied value by value: no two students spend the exact same number of minutes on homework. So it is collected into classes, and the moment that happens the individual values are gone for good. Discrete data gets grouped too whenever the list of possible values is long — marks out of 80, or runs scored — and everything in this section applies to both.

💡 The core idea
You know 18 students fall in 20 < t ≤ 30 but not where in that band. So you make the fairest possible assumption: pretend all 18 sat at the midpoint, 25 minutes. Some were really at 21, some at 29, and those errors largely cancel. That is why the answer is called an estimate of the mean — the word "estimate" is printed in the question itself, and using the midpoint is the method, not a shortcut.
🎯 A picture you know
It is like being told "18 people are somewhere in coach C" of a train and asked where the passengers' centre of mass is. You cannot see the seats, so you place all 18 at the middle of coach C. Do that for every coach and the whole-train answer comes out very close — but you would never claim it was exact.
Estimated mean of grouped data
estimated mean = Σ(f × midpoint) ÷ Σf
Midpoint of a < x ≤ b is (a + b) ÷ 2 — the two class boundaries added and halved.
Grouped discrete data works the same way but the class is printed differently: for marks in 1–5 the midpoint is (1 + 5) ÷ 2 = 3, and for 11–20 it is 15.5. Add the two numbers you can see and halve them — do not go hunting for boundaries that were never printed.

Worked example — homework time for 80 students

This one data set is used again in section 7 (its cumulative frequency curve) and section 8 (its histogram), so it is worth following closely. Note that the classes are deliberately unequal in width — that is normal for real data, and it is what makes section 8 necessary.

The modal class

You cannot give a single modal value for grouped data, only the modal class. When the classes are equally wide, that is simply the class with the largest frequency.

⚠️ The unequal-width trap
When class widths differ, the tallest bar on a correct histogram is the one with the greatest frequency density, not the greatest frequency — so strictly the modal class is the densest class. Here the two agree: 30 < t ≤ 40 has both the largest frequency (22) and the largest frequency density (2.2 per minute), so the modal class is 30 < t ≤ 40 either way. Compare it with 60 < t ≤ 100: only 8 students, but spread across 40 minutes — the widest class is emphatically not the busiest one.
✅ Exam technique
Write the modal class as an interval, exactly as the table prints it: "30 < t ≤ 40". Answering "35" (the midpoint) or "22" (the frequency) scores nothing. And if a question asks for the greatest possible value in a class, that is the upper boundary, 40 — the ≤ includes it, while the < at the other end excludes 30.
⚠️ Say the word "estimate"
Questions on grouped data ask you to "calculate an estimate of the mean". If a follow-up asks why it is only an estimate, the answer is: the original data values are not known — only the class each one falls in — so the midpoint is used for every value in a class. That is the mark scheme wording.

Scatter diagrams, correlation and the line of best fit

Everything so far has described one variable. A scatter diagram is the first tool that asks whether two variables move together — hours revised against marks scored, rainfall against crop yield, age of a scooter against its resale price.

💡 The core idea
Each student becomes one point, placed by their two measurements. Then you stop looking at points and start looking at the shape of the cloud. Sloping up together = positive correlation. One up while the other goes down = negative correlation. A shapeless blob = zero (no) correlation. Tight cloud = strong; loose cloud = weak.
🎯 A picture you know
Correlation is a crowd walking down a street. If everyone drifts in the same diagonal direction, you can predict where a newcomer will go — strong correlation. If people wander in all directions, knowing one person's path tells you nothing about the next — no correlation. A line of best fit is you pointing along "the way the crowd is heading".
Positive
As one goes up, so does the other. Cloud slopes up to the right. Hours revised & marks.
Negative
As one goes up, the other goes down. Cloud slopes down to the right. Age of a car & its value.
Zero / none
No pattern at all. Draw no line of best fit — there is nothing for it to follow. Shoe size & exam mark.

Worked example — revision time against test mark

Ten students recorded the hours they revised and their mark out of 50:

Hours revised1223445678
Mark / 5014182426303433404246
Strong positive correlation. The line is a single ruled line drawn by inspection, extending across the full data set with a roughly even number of crosses either side. A good line passes through or very near the mean point — a free way to check yours.
✅ Exam technique — how the marks are actually awarded
Plotting: small crosses (×), one per pair, accurate to half a small square. Line of best fit: one straight ruled line, spanning the whole data set, with points spread roughly evenly either side of it — not joined dot to dot, not a curve, not a line forced through the origin. Then read your estimate off your own line with dashed guide lines drawn in, and a range of answers is accepted.

⚠️ Two things correlation does NOT give you
1. Correlation is not cause. Ice-cream sales correlate with drowning deaths; ice cream does not drown anyone — hot weather drives both. If a question asks whether the diagram proves that X causes Y, the answer is no: there may be a third factor, or the link may be coincidence.
2. A line of best fit is only trustworthy inside the data. Reading it far beyond the plotted range (extrapolation) is unreliable — nothing guarantees the pattern continues. Twenty hours of revision does not give a mark of 100 out of 50.

Cumulative frequency — a running total you can read answers off

💡 The core idea
Grouped data hides the individual values, so you cannot find the median by counting. A cumulative frequency diagram gets round it by plotting a running total: "how many students took 20 minutes or less, 30 minutes or less, 40 minutes or less …". Once that curve exists, every "middle" question becomes a read-off — go in at the count you want on the vertical axis, across to the curve, down to the answer.
🎯 A picture you know
It is a queue at a ticket counter. The curve tells you how many people have been served by each moment. Want the median wait? Ask "when had half the queue been served?" — go in at half the total, cross to the curve, drop down to the clock. The steep part of the curve is the rush; the flat tail is the last few stragglers.
Building the diagram — the one rule that matters
Plot each cumulative total against the UPPER class boundary, never the midpoint. Mark points with small crosses and join them with a smooth curve. Start the curve at the lowest boundary with cumulative frequency 0.

Take the homework data from section 5 and turn the frequencies into running totals:

Read in from the vertical axis, across to the curve, then straight down. The dashed lines are part of the answer — draw them and the method mark is yours even if your reading is a square out.
n = 80, so:
median → go in at n/2 = 40  →  t ≈ 34.5 min
lower quartile → go in at n/4 = 20  →  Q₁ ≈ 24 min
upper quartile → go in at 3n/4 = 60  →  Q₃ ≈ 48 min
interquartile range ≈ 48 − 24 = 24 min  (the middle half of the students are spread over 24 minutes)
90th percentile → go in at 0.90 × 80 = 72  →  t ≈ 60 min
every value read off a curve is an ESTIMATE — a small range of answers is accepted
✅ Exam technique — the two directions
Downwards (given a count, find a value): median, quartiles, percentiles. Enter on the vertical axis at n/2, n/4, 3n/4 or p/100 × n.
Upwards (given a value, find a count): "how many took more than 45 minutes?" Go up from 45 to the curve, across to read about 57 — that is how many took 45 minutes or less, so the answer is 80 − 57 = 23 students. Forgetting that final subtraction is the single most common lost mark on this diagram.
⚠️ The classic mistakes
Plotting at the midpoint instead of the upper boundary (this shifts every answer). Joining the crosses with straight ruled segments instead of a smooth curve. Reading the median off the horizontal axis at 50 minutes because "50 is halfway" — the median is halfway through the students, not halfway along the time axis.

Histograms — where height stops meaning frequency

💡 The core idea
In a bar chart the height carries the frequency. In a histogram with unequal class widths that fails: a wide class would look enormous just for being wide. So a histogram moves the information into the area of each bar. Area must equal frequency — and since area = height × width, the height has to be frequency ÷ class width. That quantity has a name: frequency density.
🎯 A picture you know
Think of two neighbourhoods. One has 8 families in 40 hectares, the other 22 families in 10 hectares. Which is crowded? Not the one with more families — the one with more families per hectare. Frequency density is exactly population density: how tightly the data is packed per unit of the measured scale. A histogram draws crowdedness, and lets the bar's width carry the size of the area.
Frequency density
frequency density = frequency ÷ class width
frequency = frequency density × class width
The vertical axis is labelled Frequency density — writing "Frequency" on it is a lost mark. Bars touch (the data is continuous) and each bar spans its class from boundary to boundary.

✅ Exam technique — histograms run backwards too
A very common question gives you a finished histogram and asks for a frequency. Read the density off the vertical axis, read the width off the horizontal axis, and multiply: a bar spanning 20 < t ≤ 35 at density 3.6 holds 3.6 × 15 = 54 items. Harder version: a bar is asked for over part of a class — take the matching fraction of the area, e.g. the half of 40 < t ≤ 60 above 50 minutes holds 1.0 × 10 = 10 students.
⚠️ Bar chart vs histogram — never mix them up
Bar chart: discrete or qualitative data · bars have gaps · vertical axis = Frequency · height carries the meaning.
Histogram: continuous grouped data · bars touch · vertical axis = Frequency density · area carries the meaning.
If every class happens to be the same width the two look alike — but the axis label still has to say Frequency density.
09 / 11 · E9.2 · Compare & conclude

Comparing two data sets — and what you may not claim

💡 The core idea
A comparison question is marked on a fixed pair of statements: one about an average and one about the spread, each with the numbers quoted and each interpreted in context. "A is better" earns nothing, because no figure is given and "better" is not a statistic. Quoting the figures but naming the wrong direction earns nothing either. Two sentences, two numbers, two meanings.

Worked example — two students, eight tests each

Marks (%) in orderMeanMedianRangeIQR
Aarav62 65 68 70 71 73 74 777070.5157
Bhavna45 52 66 70 76 78 85 8870734322.5
Aarav: Σx = 560, mean = 560/8 = 70  ·  median = (70 + 71)/2 = 70.5
Bhavna: Σx = 560, mean = 560/8 = 70  ·  median = (70 + 76)/2 = 73
Aarav Q₁ = (65+68)/2 = 66.5, Q₃ = (73+74)/2 = 73.5 → IQR = 7
Bhavna Q₁ = (52+66)/2 = 59, Q₃ = (78+85)/2 = 81.5 → IQR = 22.5

Their means are identical. Everything interesting is in the spread:

A full-mark answer
"Their mean marks are equal (both 70%), so on average they performed the same. But Aarav's interquartile range is much smaller (7 against 22.5), so Aarav is far more consistent, while Bhavna's marks vary a lot from test to test."
A no-mark answer
"Aarav is better because his marks are closer together." No figures quoted, no average compared, and "better" is a judgement the data does not support — Bhavna's median is actually higher.
✅ Exam technique — the sentence template
"The [average] for A is [higher / lower] than for B ([number] against [number]), which means […in context]. The [range / IQR] for A is [larger / smaller] ([number] against [number]), so A is [more / less] consistent."
Use the IQR rather than the range whenever you can justify it: the range depends on only two values and one outlier destroys it, whereas the IQR describes the middle half and ignores the extremes.
⚠️ Restrictions on what you may conclude
Three claims that are never safe from a data set alone:
1. Cause. Two things moving together does not mean one causes the other (section 6).
2. Generalising past the sample. A survey of 20 students in one class says nothing reliable about the whole school — the sample is too small and it is not representative. A bigger, randomly chosen sample fixes this.
3. Predicting an individual. "The mean is 70" does not mean the next student will score 70. An average describes the group, never a member of it.
⚠️ Which measure, and when
Mean — uses every value; best when there are no extremes. Wrecked by an outlier.
Median — the middle; use it when there is an outlier (incomes, house prices).
Mode — the only average available for qualitative data; also what a shopkeeper wants (the size that sells).
Range — quick, but built from just the two extreme values.
IQR — the spread of the middle half; unaffected by outliers, so it is the better comparison.

Q1Classify each of these as qualitative, discrete or continuous: (a) the number of chapatis eaten at dinner, (b) the mass of a mango, (c) favourite colour, (d) the time an athlete takes to run 400 m, (e) shoe size.
Tap to reveal
(a) discrete — counted, whole values only. (b) continuous — measured, and more precise scales give more decimals. (c) qualitative — a category, so only the mode makes sense. (d) continuous. (e) discrete — the trap: feet are continuous, but the recorded values jump 6, 7, 8, so shoe size behaves as discrete. Always classify what is written down, not what is being measured.
Q230 kirana shops were asked how many brands of atta they stock. Number of brands x: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 with frequencies 3, 7, 9, 8, 3. Find the mean, the median and the mode.
Tap to reveal
Σf = 3+7+9+8+3 = 30 ✓. Σfx = 3(1) + 7(2) + 9(3) + 8(4) + 3(5) = 3 + 14 + 27 + 32 + 15 = 91. Mean = 91/30 = 3.033… = 3.03 (3 s.f.). Mode = the value with the biggest frequency (9) = 3. Median: n = 30 is even, so it is the mean of the 15th and 16th values. Cumulative frequencies are 3, 10, 19, 27, 30 — items 11 to 19 are all "3", so both the 15th and 16th are 3 and the median = 3. Do not divide by 5 rows, and do not give the mode as 9.
Q3The masses m kg of 40 parcels: 0 < m ≤ 5 → 8, 5 < m ≤ 10 → 14, 10 < m ≤ 20 → 10, 20 < m ≤ 40 → 8. Calculate an estimate of the mean, and write down the modal class.
Tap to reveal
Midpoints are 2.5, 7.5, 15 and 30. Σ(f × midpoint) = 8(2.5) + 14(7.5) + 10(15) + 8(30) = 20 + 105 + 150 + 240 = 515. Estimate of the mean = 515/40 = 12.875 kg (accept 12.9 kg). Modal class: the widths are 5, 5, 10, 20, so the densities are 1.6, 2.8, 1.0 and 0.4 — the greatest is 2.8, which is also the greatest frequency (14), so the modal class is 5 < m ≤ 10. Write it as an interval, not as 7.5 or 14.
Q4On a histogram, one bar spans 20 < t ≤ 35 and has a frequency density of 3.6. (a) How many items are in that class? (b) A second class, 35 < t ≤ 55, contains 30 items — what is the height of its bar?
Tap to reveal
(a) Class width = 35 − 20 = 15, so frequency = density × width = 3.6 × 15 = 54 items. (b) Class width = 55 − 35 = 20, so frequency density = 30 ÷ 20 = 1.5. The second bar is much shorter than the first even though it is wide, because its 30 items are thinly spread. Height is crowdedness, not count.
Q5The heights h cm of 120 plants give cumulative frequencies: h ≤ 10 → 8, h ≤ 20 → 32, h ≤ 30 → 74, h ≤ 40 → 104, h ≤ 50 → 120. Estimate the median and the interquartile range.
Tap to reveal
n = 120. Median is read at n/2 = 60: 60 lies between the 32 at 20 cm and the 74 at 30 cm, so median ≈ 20 + (60−32)/(74−32) × 10 = 20 + 6.7 = 26.7 cm (about 27 cm). Q₁ at n/4 = 30: between 8 and 32, so ≈ 10 + (30−8)/24 × 10 = 19.2 cm. Q₃ at 3n/4 = 90: between 74 and 104, so ≈ 30 + (90−74)/30 × 10 = 35.3 cm. IQR ≈ 35.3 − 19.2 = 16.1 cm (about 16 cm). Reading off a hand-drawn curve, anything within about half a square is accepted.
Q6The mean height of 12 plants is 24.5 cm. The tallest plant, of height 41 cm, is removed. Find the mean height of the remaining 11 plants.
Tap to reveal
Turn the mean into a total: Σx = 24.5 × 12 = 294 cm. Remove the plant: 294 − 41 = 253 cm, now shared between 11 plants. New mean = 253 ÷ 11 = 23 cm. Notice the mean fell by 1.5 cm from removing a single value — that is exactly how sensitive the mean is to an extreme. Never try to "adjust the mean" directly; always go via the total.
Q7Two bowlers' runs conceded per over have the same median of 6, but bowler P has an interquartile range of 2 and bowler Q an interquartile range of 9. Compare the two bowlers, using both statistics.
Tap to reveal
A full answer must do both halves. "Their medians are equal (both 6 runs per over), so on average they concede the same. But P's interquartile range is much smaller (2 against 9), so P is far more consistent from over to over, while Q's overs vary widely — some cheap, some expensive." Quoting the two numbers and interpreting them in cricket context is what scores; "P is better" alone scores nothing, and note the medians tell you they are not better on average.
Q8A scatter diagram of the number of hours 15 children spend on a phone each day against their test marks shows strong negative correlation. (a) Explain why this does not prove that phone use causes lower marks. (b) Explain why using the line of best fit to predict the mark of a child who uses a phone for 14 hours a day is unreliable.
Tap to reveal
(a) Correlation shows only that the two vary together. A third factor could be driving both — for example, less time spent studying, or less sleep — or the sample of 15 may simply be too small and unrepresentative to show a real link. So the diagram gives no evidence of cause. (b) 14 hours is far outside the range of the plotted data. A line of best fit only describes the pattern where data exists; nothing guarantees the same straight-line trend continues beyond it, so extrapolating that far is unreliable (and could even give an impossible negative mark).
