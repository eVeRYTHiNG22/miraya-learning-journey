Reversible reactions
& the equilibrium they settle into

The one part of Chemistry where a reaction refuses to finish. This is a deep-dive on syllabus subtopic 6.3 — the ⇌ arrow, hydrated ⇌ anhydrous salts, dynamic equilibrium, how temperature, pressure, concentration and catalysts move it, and the two industrial processes (Haber and Contact) the exam always comes back to. Four Check yourself blocks are built in — do them, don't just read them.

01 / 13 · 6.3 The idea

Most reactions finish. A few refuse to.

💡 Intuition
Burn a piece of magnesium ribbon and you get white magnesium oxide. Leave it on the bench for a hundred years and it will never turn back into magnesium and air. That reaction is a one-way street — we draw it with →.

But some reactions run both ways at once. The products, as soon as they form, start turning back into the reactants. Neither side ever wins outright, so the reaction never goes to completion. We mark those with a special double arrow, ⇌, and everything in subtopic 6.3 follows from that one fact.
Reversible reaction — a reaction in which the products can react together to re-form the reactants. It is shown by the symbol ⇌ in place of →, and changing the conditions can change the direction the reaction goes in.

Reading the arrow

The double arrow has two halves and you must be able to talk about each one separately:

The forward reaction →
Read left to right. Reactants (on the left) turn into products (on the right). In N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ the forward reaction is nitrogen + hydrogen making ammonia.
The reverse (backward) reaction ←
Read right to left. Products turn back into reactants — here, ammonia breaking apart into nitrogen and hydrogen. It is a real reaction happening at the same time in the same vessel.
🎭 Analogy — the wedding hall
Picture a wedding at a banquet hall in Pune. Guests keep drifting from the dining hall out onto the lawn, and other guests keep drifting back in. Nobody is standing still. But if the two flows are equal, a photograph taken at 9 p.m. and another at 9:30 p.m. will show the same number of people in each place — even though every single guest has moved. That is exactly a reversible reaction settling down: constant amounts, furious activity.

Where the ⇌ arrow turns up in your syllabus

Spotting it early tells you the question is about equilibrium, not about "how much product do I get":

Hydrated ⇌ anhydrous salts (6.3 Core)
Haber: N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃
Contact: 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃
Ethene + steam ⇌ ethanol (11.6)
Weak acids: CH₃COOH ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺ (7.1)
⚠️ The ⇌ symbol does NOT mean "half and half"
Students read ⇌ as "50 % each side". It means nothing of the kind. A reversible reaction can settle at 98 % products (that is the Contact process) or at 25 % products (that is the Haber process). All the arrow tells you is that both directions happen. How far it goes is a separate question — and that is what "position of equilibrium" means.
02 / 13 · 6.3 Core — the two named reversible reactions

Water joining and leaving a salt

The Core syllabus names exactly two reversible reactions, and they are both the same idea: a salt that has water built into its crystals, and what happens when you drive that water off and put it back. Learn these two cold — they are guaranteed marks.

Water of crystallisation — water molecules that are chemically built into the crystal structure of a salt. A salt that contains it is hydrated; the same salt with the water removed is anhydrous ("without water").
Heat the hydrated salt and the water of crystallisation is driven off as vapour, which condenses on the cooler glass near the mouth of the tube (this is why the tube is clamped at an angle — so the water cannot run back onto the hot glass and crack it). Add water to the dry residue and the original colour comes straight back. Learn the colours by direction: hydrated copper(II) sulfate is blue, hydrated cobalt(II) chloride is pink.

Example 1 — copper(II) sulfate

⇌ The equation
CuSO₄·5H₂O(s) ⇌ CuSO₄(s) + 5H₂O(l)
blue hydrated copper(II) sulfate  ⇌  white anhydrous copper(II) sulfate + water

Forward (heating): blue → white. This direction is endothermic — you have to keep supplying heat to break the water out.
Reverse (adding water): white → blue, and the test tube gets noticeably warm — this direction is exothermic.

Example 2 — cobalt(II) chloride

⇌ The equation
CoCl₂·6H₂O(s) ⇌ CoCl₂(s) + 6H₂O(l)
pink hydrated cobalt(II) chloride  ⇌  blue anhydrous cobalt(II) chloride + water

Forward (heating): pink → blue.  Reverse (adding water): blue → pink.
⚠️ The four colours — memorise them in this order
copper(II) sulfate: hydrated = BLUE → anhydrous = WHITE cobalt(II) chloride: hydrated = PINK → anhydrous = BLUE copper: wet = blue, dry = white · cobalt: + water → pink

Why this matters: the chemical tests for water

Because the dry form changes colour the instant water touches it, both salts are used as tests for the presence of water:

Test reagentColour change if water is present
Anhydrous copper(II) sulfatewhite → blue
Anhydrous cobalt(II) chloride (usually as cobalt chloride paper)blue → pink
✅ Present vs pure — the two-part answer
Both tests only prove that water is present. They cannot prove the liquid is pure water. To show purity you must also check the physical constants: it boils at exactly 100 °C and freezes at exactly 0 °C (at normal atmospheric pressure). A question that says "show that the liquid is pure water" wants both halves — the chemical test and the boiling/melting point.
🎭 You already own one of these
Those little sachets marked "SILICA GEL — DO NOT EAT" that come inside a new pair of shoes or a camera bag contain beads with a cobalt salt indicator. Fresh out of the box, in a dry room, they are blue. Leave the box open through a Mumbai monsoon and they turn pink — they have absorbed water. Bake them in the oven and they go blue again, ready to reuse. That sachet is a reversible reaction you can hold in your hand.
⚠️ Two marks students throw away here
1. "White vapour." The water that condenses at the mouth of the tube is a colourless liquid. Nothing in this experiment is a white vapour.
2. Getting cobalt backwards. For copper the wet form is blue; for cobalt the dry form is blue. Anchor it on the pink: cobalt + water = pink. Everything else follows.

03 / 13 · 6.3 Supplement — equilibrium

The busy standstill

💡 Intuition
Seal a reversible reaction in a closed container and watch what happens. At the start there is lots of reactant and no product, so the forward reaction is fast and the reverse reaction has nothing to work with — it is at zero. As product builds up, the forward reaction slows down (reactants are being used up) and the reverse reaction speeds up. Sooner or later the two rates meet. From that moment on, product is being made exactly as fast as it is being destroyed, so the amount of everything stops changing. The reaction has not stopped — it has balanced.
Equilibrium (the exam definition) — a reversible reaction in a closed system is at equilibrium when:
(a) the rate of the forward reaction is equal to the rate of the reverse reaction, and
(b) the concentrations of reactants and products are no longer changing.

Those two lines are worth two marks and the examiner wants them almost word-for-word. Notice that "closed system" is part of the setup, not one of the two conditions — but you lose marks if you forget it exists.

The two graphs that define equilibrium — you should be able to sketch both. Left: the forward rate falls as reactants are used up while the reverse rate climbs from zero; where they meet they stay equal, and that instant is equilibrium. Right: from that same instant the amounts stop changing — but they are not equal to each other. Equal rates, unequal amounts. Neither curve ever reaches zero: both reactions are still going.

Why the system must be closed

"Closed system" means no substance can enter or leave (heat still can). If a product can escape — a gas bubbling out of an open flask — it is never available for the reverse reaction, so the reverse rate can never catch up and the mixture never settles. Heating calcium carbonate in a sealed vessel reaches an equilibrium; heating it in an open lime kiln, where the CO₂ blows away, drives it all the way to calcium oxide.

🎭 Analogy — the down escalator
Walk down an up-escalator at exactly the escalator's speed. You are sweating, the steps are flying past, and your position on the staircase never changes. An onlooker photographing you every minute would swear nothing was happening. That is dynamic equilibrium: maximum activity, zero net change.
⚠️ The three classic wrong answers
"The reaction has stopped." No — both reactions are still going at full speed. That is why we call it dynamic.
"There are equal amounts of reactants and products." No — the rates are equal. The mixture can be 98 % product (Contact) or 25 % product (Haber).
"Equilibrium means the reaction is finished." No — a reaction that has finished has run out of a reactant. At equilibrium every substance is still present.
04 / 13 · 6.3 The language

"Position of equilibrium" — and how to talk about it

Once you accept that a reversible reaction stops somewhere in the middle, the only interesting question left is: where? That "where" is the position of equilibrium, and almost every Supplement question about 6.3 is really asking you to move it.

Position of equilibrium — how far the reaction has gone when it settles, i.e. the relative amounts of reactants and products in the equilibrium mixture. If it "lies to the right" there is mostly product (a high yield); if it "lies to the left" there is mostly reactant (a low yield).
Shifts to the right
= the forward reaction is favoured = more product = the yield increases. All three phrases mean the same thing; use whichever the question uses.
Shifts to the left
= the reverse reaction is favoured = more reactant = the yield decreases. Some products decompose back.
No shift
The amounts at equilibrium are unchanged. The mixture may get there faster or slower, but it ends up the same.
✅ Position ≠ rate — keep the two ideas in separate boxes
Every 6.3 question is secretly two questions, and the marks are usually split between them:
Position / yield — how much product you end up with.
Rate — how quickly you get there.

A change can help one and hurt the other. Raising the temperature in the Haber process increases the rate but decreases the yield. If you only write about one of them, you have answered half the question. Train yourself to write a sentence about each.
05 / 13 · 6.3 The rule that answers every question

Disturb it, and it fights back

💡 Intuition
There is only one rule in this whole subtopic, and every prediction you will ever be asked to make comes out of it:

If you change a condition, the equilibrium shifts in the direction that partly cancels out your change.

Heat it, and it shifts the way that absorbs heat. Squeeze it, and it shifts the way that takes up less room. Add more of a substance, and it shifts the way that uses that substance up. It is a system that sulks and pushes back — and it pushes back predictably. (Your teacher may call this Le Chatelier's principle; 0620 does not require the name, only the reasoning.)

The three-step method — use it every single time

Step 1 · Read the data
Off the equation, note (a) is the forward reaction exothermic or endothermic (the sign of ΔH, or the word in the stem), and (b) how many molecules of gas are on each side. Ignore solids and liquids when counting.
Step 2 · Ask "which way opposes it?"
Heat added → the endothermic direction absorbs it. Pressure raised → the side with fewer gas molecules relieves it. Substance added → the direction that removes it.
Step 3 · Say it in exam words
"The equilibrium shifts to the left/right, so the yield of [named product] increases/decreases." Add the rate sentence if the question mentions rate.

Now practise the method on real equilibria. Pick a system, then pick a change — the tool shows you the shift and the full mark-scheme sentence you should have written.

1 · Choose the equilibrium
2 · Choose the change you make
Model answer
⚠️ "Shifts right" is not an answer on its own
A 2-mark "predict and explain" question needs the prediction and the reason. "The equilibrium moves right" scores 1 at best. "The equilibrium moves right because the forward reaction is exothermic and lowering the temperature favours the direction that releases heat" scores both. Always write the word because.
06 / 13 · 6.3 Supplement — the four changes

Temperature, pressure, concentration, catalyst

These are the only four changes the syllabus asks about. Take them one at a time.

1 · Temperature — the only change that works on the ΔH

The temperature rule — raising the temperature shifts the equilibrium in the endothermic direction (the one that absorbs heat); lowering it shifts the equilibrium in the exothermic direction.

If the forward reaction is exothermic (ΔH negative, which is the case for both Haber and Contact), then the reverse reaction must be endothermic. So:

  • Raise the temperature → shifts left (backwards) → yield falls. But both rates increase, so equilibrium is reached faster.
  • Lower the temperature → shifts right (forwards) → yield rises. But both rates fall, so it takes much longer to get there.

That tension — good yield but too slow, versus fast but poor yield — is the whole reason industry uses "compromise" conditions, which is section 10.

✅ Temperature is the ONLY change that alters the reverse-reaction relationship
Pressure and concentration change the amounts; a catalyst changes the speed. Only temperature changes the balance between an exothermic and an endothermic direction — which is why temperature questions always mention exothermic / endothermic in the mark scheme. If the stem gives you a ΔH value, that is the examiner handing you the answer.

2 · Pressure — count the gas molecules

The pressure rule — increasing the pressure shifts the equilibrium to the side with the fewer molecules of gas; decreasing it shifts to the side with more. If both sides have the same number of gas molecules, changing the pressure has no effect on the position.
Read the big numbers in front of each gas and add them up for each side. Haber: 1 + 3 = 4 on the left, 2 on the right, so higher pressure pushes it right. Hydrogen + iodine: 1 + 1 = 2 on each side, so pressure does nothing to the position. Only count substances labelled (g) — solids and liquids are barely compressible and are left out of the count.
⚠️ Two pressure traps
Only gases count. In CaCO₃(s) ⇌ CaO(s) + CO₂(g) the count is 0 gas molecules on the left, 1 on the right — so increasing the pressure shifts it left, back to calcium carbonate.
Higher pressure always increases the rate. Even when it does nothing to the position, squeezing gases together makes collisions more frequent, so equilibrium is reached sooner. A "no effect" answer applies to the yield, not the rate.

3 · Concentration — add to one side, and it moves away from you

The concentration rule — increasing the concentration (or amount) of a substance shifts the equilibrium away from that substance, in the direction that uses it up. Removing a substance shifts the equilibrium towards it, to replace it.
  • Add more reactant → shifts right → more product. (Industry does this with the cheap reactant: the Contact process uses an excess of air because oxygen is free.)
  • Remove the product as it forms → shifts right → the reaction keeps making more to replace it. (The Haber process does this by condensing the ammonia to a liquid.)
  • Add more product → shifts left. Remove a reactant → shifts left.

4 · Catalyst — the one that changes nothing (about yield)

The catalyst rule — a catalyst speeds up the forward and the reverse reactions equally. So it has no effect on the position of equilibrium and no effect on the yield — it only makes the mixture reach equilibrium sooner.

A catalyst works by providing an alternative reaction pathway of lower activation energy. That lower barrier helps molecules going in either direction, which is exactly why the balance point does not move.

📝 Worked example 2 — "predict and explain" for full marks

Q. In the equilibrium N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g) the forward reaction is exothermic. Predict and explain the effect of decreasing the temperature on the yield of ammonia and on the rate at which equilibrium is reached. [4]

Model answer. The yield of ammonia increases (1), because the forward reaction is exothermic, so lowering the temperature shifts the position of equilibrium in the exothermic (forward) direction to release heat and oppose the fall in temperature (1). However, the rate decreases (1), because the particles have less kinetic energy, so they collide less frequently and a smaller proportion of collisions have energy greater than or equal to the activation energy (1).

What loses marks: writing only about the yield (half the question), saying "it moves right" without the word exothermic, or claiming that a lower temperature "makes the reaction go backwards" — it does not reverse the reaction, it moves the balance point.

✅ The sentence skeletons worth memorising
Temperature: "The forward reaction is exo/endothermic, so raising/lowering the temperature shifts the equilibrium to the left/right, and the yield of [product] increases/decreases."
Pressure: "There are __ molecules of gas on the left and __ on the right, so increasing the pressure shifts the equilibrium towards the side with fewer gas molecules."
Concentration: "Removing [product] shifts the equilibrium to the right, because the system acts to replace it."
Catalyst: "A catalyst has no effect on the position of equilibrium or on the yield; it lowers the activation energy so equilibrium is reached faster."
08 / 13 · 6.3 Supplement — industry 1

The Haber process — making ammonia

Ammonia is the starting point for nitrogen fertilisers, and nitrogen fertilisers are the reason India can feed itself. Plants like the ones at Kalol and Phulpur run this exact equilibrium at industrial scale, day and night.

⚠️ The four things you must be able to state
N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g) nitrogen from AIR · hydrogen from METHANE (natural gas) 450 °C · 20 000 kPa (200 atm) · IRON catalyst the forward reaction is EXOTHERMIC
Because the reaction is reversible, only about 15 % of the gases convert on each pass through the converter. Two design tricks rescue the process: the ammonia is removed by cooling it until it condenses to a liquid (N₂ and H₂ stay gases), which pulls the equilibrium further right; and the unreacted gases are recycled back to the converter so that almost none is wasted. A modest equilibrium yield therefore becomes a very high overall conversion.

Where the raw materials come from

Nitrogen ← the air
Air is about 78 % nitrogen, so it is effectively free and unlimited.
Hydrogen ← methane (natural gas)
Methane is reacted with steam to release hydrogen. Say "methane" or "natural gas" — "from water" is the common wrong answer and scores nothing.

Reading the yield graph — the "information provided" question

The exam often hands you a graph like this and asks you to explain what it shows. Everything on it follows from the two facts you already know: the forward reaction is exothermic, and there are 4 gas molecules on the left, 2 on the right.

Approximate equilibrium yields for a 1 : 3 mixture of nitrogen and hydrogen. Every curve falls as the temperature rises — the forward reaction is exothermic, so heat pushes the equilibrium backwards. At any temperature a higher pressure gives a higher yield — the right-hand side has fewer gas molecules. Note that the graph shows the equilibrium yield; the gases do not stay in the converter long enough to reach equilibrium fully, which is why removing the ammonia and recycling the leftovers matters so much.
✅ Three sentences that answer almost any yield-graph question
"As the temperature increases the yield decreases, because the forward reaction is exothermic and the equilibrium shifts in the endothermic (reverse) direction."
"As the pressure increases the yield increases, because there are 4 molecules of gas on the left and 2 on the right, so the equilibrium shifts to the side with fewer gas molecules."
"A higher yield is not used because a lower temperature makes the rate uneconomically slow, and a much higher pressure is too expensive and less safe."
09 / 13 · 6.3 Supplement — industry 2

The Contact process — making sulfur trioxide

Sulfuric acid is the most-produced chemical in the world, and the step that makes it possible is the reversible conversion of sulfur dioxide into sulfur trioxide. That single step is what the syllabus examines.

⚠️ The four things you must be able to state
2SO₂(g) + O₂(g) ⇌ 2SO₃(g) SO₂ from BURNING SULFUR or ROASTING SULFIDE ORES oxygen from AIR 450 °C · 200 kPa (2 atm) · VANADIUM(V) OXIDE, V₂O₅
The Contact process runs at almost the same temperature as the Haber process but at a tiny fraction of the pressure. It can afford to: the equilibrium already lies far to the right at 450 °C, so even at 2 atm the yield is about 98 %. Paying for 200 atm to squeeze out the last 1–2 % would never repay the cost of the compressors and thick-walled vessels.
✅ The pressure contrast is the favourite exam question
"Explain why the Contact process uses a much lower pressure than the Haber process." Model answer: the yield of SO₃ at 2 atm is already about 98 %, so raising the pressure would give very little extra product (1), while high-pressure plant is expensive to build and run and less safe (1). In the Haber process the yield at low pressure is poor, so the extra cost of 200 atm is worth paying.
⚠️ Don't swap the catalysts
Haber = iron. Contact = vanadium(V) oxide, V₂O₅. Multiple-choice papers deliberately offer "iron" as a distractor in a Contact question, because it is the one word most students half-remember. Link them: iron for ammonia, V for the acid that is Vital to industry.
10 / 13 · 6.3 Supplement — why those conditions

Compromise: the answer is never "whatever gives the best yield"

💡 Intuition
Chemistry says: for a maximum yield of ammonia, run the Haber process cold and at enormous pressure. A factory manager says: cold means the reaction takes a week, and enormous pressure means steel vessels that cost a fortune and can fail dangerously. The conditions actually used are a compromise — deliberately not the best yield, because a smaller amount produced quickly, safely and cheaply beats a larger amount produced too slowly to sell.

C4In the Haber process the ammonia is cooled until it becomes a liquid and the remaining gases are returned to the converter. Explain how each of these steps improves the process. [3]
Tap to reveal
Cooling removes the ammonia from the equilibrium mixture (only NH₃ condenses; N₂ and H₂ stay gases) (1), so the equilibrium shifts to the right to replace it and more ammonia is formed (1). Recycling returns the unreacted nitrogen and hydrogen to the converter so they get further chances to react — very little raw material is wasted and the overall conversion is high (1).

"the position of equilibrium shifts to the right / left" — the standard phrasing; use it rather than "the reaction goes forwards".
"because the forward reaction is exothermic" — the temperature reason.

⚠️ The six mistakes that cost the most marks in 6.3
1. Answering about the rate when the question asked about the yield (or the other way round).
2. Saying a catalyst increases the yield. It never does.
3. Counting solids and liquids when working out the pressure effect. Only (g) counts.
4. Saying "at equilibrium the amounts are equal". Equal rates, not equal amounts.
5. Giving the Haber conditions for the Contact process — especially the iron catalyst.

Check yourself — block D · the full exam set

Nine questions in board language, covering everything in 6.3. Cover the screen, write your answer on paper, then tap. If you can do all nine unaided, this subtopic is finished.

D1Define the term reversible reaction. [1]
Tap to reveal
A reaction in which the products can react together to re-form the reactants / a reaction that can go both forwards and backwards, shown by the symbol ⇌ (1).
D2Explain why a reversible reaction can only reach equilibrium in a closed system. [2]
Tap to reveal
In a closed system no substance can enter or leave (1), so the products stay in contact with each other and the reverse reaction can occur — allowing the two rates to become equal. In an open system a product (usually a gas) escapes, so the reverse reaction is starved and the rates never become equal (1).
D3A student says: "At equilibrium the reaction has stopped, and there are equal amounts of reactants and products." Give two reasons why this statement is wrong. [2]
Tap to reveal
The reaction has not stopped — the forward and reverse reactions both continue, at equal rates (it is dynamic) (1). The amounts are not necessarily equal — it is the rates that are equal; the concentrations are simply constant, and can be very different from each other (1).
D4Ethanol is manufactured from ethene: C₂H₄(g) + H₂O(g) ⇌ C₂H₅OH(g), forward reaction exothermic. Predict and explain the effect on the equilibrium yield of ethanol of (a) increasing the pressure and (b) increasing the temperature. [4]
Tap to reveal
(a) The yield increases (1) — there are 2 molecules of gas on the left and 1 on the right, so higher pressure shifts the equilibrium to the right, the side with fewer gas molecules (1).
(b) The yield decreases (1) — the forward reaction is exothermic, so raising the temperature shifts the equilibrium in the endothermic (reverse) direction (1).
D5Sketch, on the same axes, how the rate of the forward reaction and the rate of the reverse reaction change with time from the moment the reactants are mixed in a closed vessel. Label the point at which equilibrium is reached. [3]
Tap to reveal
The forward rate starts high and falls (reactants are being used up) (1). The reverse rate starts at zero and rises (product is building up) (1). The two curves meet and then run flat together — equilibrium is the point where they meet, and neither is zero after it (1). Compare your sketch with the left-hand graph in section 03.
D6Using the graph in section 08, state and explain what happens to the equilibrium yield of ammonia as the pressure is increased from 100 atm to 400 atm at a constant 450 °C. [2]
Tap to reveal
The yield increases — from roughly 17 % at 100 atm to about 27 % at 200 atm and around 43 % at 400 atm (1). This is because there are 4 molecules of gas on the left and only 2 on the right, so increasing the pressure shifts the equilibrium to the right, towards the side with fewer gas molecules (1).
D7State one similarity and two differences between the conditions used in the Haber process and those used in the Contact process. [3]
Tap to reveal
Similarity: both use a temperature of about 450 °C (1). Differences: the Haber process uses a much higher pressure — 200 atm compared with only 2 atm (1); and the catalysts differ — iron in the Haber process, vanadium(V) oxide in the Contact process (1).
D8A sample of hydrated copper(II) sulfate has a mass of 5.00 g. After heating to constant mass the residue has a mass of 3.20 g. Calculate the percentage by mass of water of crystallisation in the hydrated salt, and state what "heating to constant mass" tells you. [3]
Tap to reveal
Mass of water driven off = 5.00 − 3.20 = 1.80 g (1). Percentage = (1.80 ÷ 5.00) × 100 = 36.0 % (1). "Heating to constant mass" means the sample is reheated and reweighed until the mass no longer falls, which shows that all the water of crystallisation has been driven off / the forward reaction is complete (1).
D9In an industrial equilibrium the manufacturer removes the product continuously as it forms. Explain, in terms of the position of equilibrium, why this increases the amount of product obtained. [2]
Tap to reveal
Removing the product lowers its concentration in the mixture (1), so the equilibrium shifts to the right — in the direction that replaces the product — and more reactant is converted; because the product is removed continuously the system never settles, so conversion keeps going (1).
